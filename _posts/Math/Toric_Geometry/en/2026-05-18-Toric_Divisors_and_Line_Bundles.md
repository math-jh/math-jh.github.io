In the previous post we studied toric varieties, and in doing so we already saw that the combinatorial data of the fan defining a toric variety determines many of its properties. In this post we focus in particular on (torus-invariant) divisors defined on a toric variety.

## Torus-Invariant Weil Divisors

We already know that a toric variety $X_\Sigma$ contains the algebraic torus $T_N \cong (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^n$ as an open dense subset. However, divisors on it are trivial, because if we consider the coordinate ring of $T_N$, it is the Laurent polynomial ring

$$\mathbb{C}[M] = \mathbb{C}[t_1^{\pm 1}, \ldots, t_n^{\pm 1}]$$

and hence a UFD ([\[Ring Theory\] §Polynomial Rings, ⁋Theorem 16](/en/math/ring_theory/polynomial_rings#thm16){: data-lid="c1lf7" }). In a UFD, every height-1 prime ideal is principal ([\[Commutative Algebra\] §Primary Decomposition, ⁋Theorem 7](/en/math/commutative_algebra/primary_decomposition#thm7){: data-lid="s57ss" }), so every codimension 1 irreducible subvariety is the zero set of a single Laurent polynomial, and therefore every Weil divisor is principal. Consequently, $\Cl(T_N) = 0$, so there is no motivation to study divisors on it.

In other words, all nontrivial divisor information on $X_\Sigma$ is concentrated in the *boundary* $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N$, and this boundary consists exactly of the part fixed by the action of $T_N$. Therefore, we develop the divisor theory on $X_\Sigma$ by focusing on *torus-invariant* divisors.

For each 1-dimensional cone of the fan $\Sigma$, i.e. each *ray* $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, consider its primitive generator $v_\rho \in N$. The ray $\rho$ corresponds to a codimension 1 torus orbit in $X_\Sigma$, and its Zariski closure defines an irreducible divisor.

::: Definition 1
For each ray $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, we write $D_\rho$ for the Zariski closure of the corresponding torus orbit. Then $D_\rho$ is an irreducible Weil divisor on $X_\Sigma$, and such a $D_\rho$ is called a *torus-invariant prime divisor*.
:::

This definition arises naturally from the orbit structure of a toric variety. As we saw in [§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 5](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#prop5){: data-lid="de10m" }, a $d$-dimensional cone corresponds to a torus orbit of dimension $n-d$, so a 1-dimensional cone $\rho$ corresponds to an orbit of dimension $n-1$, i.e. a codimension 1 torus-invariant subvariety. The Zariski closure of this orbit is naturally a divisor.

::: Definition 2
We denote the free abelian group generated by the torus-invariant Weil divisors on $X_\Sigma$ by $\Div_T(X_\Sigma)$. That is,

$$\Div_T(X_\Sigma) = \bigoplus_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} \mathbb{Z} \cdot D_\rho$$

Every torus-invariant Weil divisor is uniquely expressed in the form $D = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} a_\rho D_\rho$.
:::

We saw earlier that the divisor theory of $T_N$ is trivial; using this, we see that considering divisors on a toric variety ultimately reduces to considering torus-invariant divisors. This is because restricting an arbitrary divisor $D \in \Div(X_\Sigma)$ to the open dense subset $T_N$ gives a divisor $D\rvert_{T_N}$ on $T_N$, which here is of the form of a principal divisor $\divisor(f)$; hence the support of $D - \divisor(f)$ is contained in the boundary $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N = \bigcup_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} D_\rho$ and is therefore torus-invariant. From this, $D$ is linearly equivalent to some torus-invariant divisor, and more formally, this allows us to verify that

$$\Div_T(X_\Sigma) \hookrightarrow \Div(X_\Sigma) \twoheadrightarrow \Cl(X_\Sigma)\tag{1}$$

is surjective. That is, in essence, instead of $\Div(X_\Sigma)$, considering $\Div_T(X_\Sigma)$ is not a trade-off that loses information.

## Principal Divisors and the Class Group

One of our ultimate goals is to compute $\Cl(X_\Sigma)$ explicitly from the combinatorial data of the fan $\Sigma$. Since (1) is a surjection, the first isomorphism theorem gives $\Cl(X_\Sigma) \cong \Div_T(X_\Sigma) / \ker$, where $\Div_T(X_\Sigma)$ is already completely described as the free abelian group $\bigoplus_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} \mathbb{Z} D_\rho$ determined by the rays of the fan. Therefore, to know $\Cl(X_\Sigma)$, it remains only to describe the kernel of (1) in terms of the data of the fan.

By definition, this kernel consists of those elements of $\Div_T(X_\Sigma)$ whose image in $\Cl(X_\Sigma)$ is $0$, that is, the collection of divisors that are *simultaneously torus-invariant and principal*. Therefore, this ultimately reduces to the question of which rational functions on a toric variety define a torus-invariant principal divisor.

Then, for the lattice $M$, the most natural candidate corresponding to an element $m \in M$ is the *character* $\rchi^m$. For each $m \in M$, the character $\rchi^m: T_N \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^\ast$ can, since $T_N \subseteq X_\Sigma$ is open dense, be viewed as a rational function on $X_\Sigma$ ([§Affine Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 11](/en/math/toric_geometry/affine_toric_varieties#prop11){: data-lid="sx5ti" }). Here, $\rchi^m$ itself is not invariant under the action of $T_N$, but for $t \in T_N$, we have

$$(t \cdot \rchi^m)(x) = \rchi^m(t x) = \rchi^m(t) \cdot \rchi^m(x)$$

(by the convention of [§Affine Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 10](/en/math/toric_geometry/affine_toric_varieties#prop10){: data-lid="l5slz" }), so they differ only by a scalar multiple. However, since scalar multiples do not change zeros and poles, even if the $\rchi^m$ themselves are not torus-invariant, $\divisor(\rchi^m)$ is torus-invariant, and $\divisor(\rchi^m) \in \Div_T(X_\Sigma)$. Our first goal is to write this divisor explicitly in terms of the data of the fan.

::: Proposition 3
The principal divisor corresponding to the character $\rchi^m$ is given by

$$\divisor(\rchi^m) = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} \langle m, v_\rho \rangle D_\rho.$$
:::

::: Proof
Since $X_\Sigma$ is normal ([§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#prop4){: data-lid="54n2j" }), the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma, D_\rho}$ at the generic point of each prime divisor $D_\rho$ is a DVR; write its valuation as $v_{D_\rho}: \mathbb{C}(X_\Sigma)^\times \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}$. By the definition of a principal divisor,

$$\divisor(\rchi^m) = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} v_{D_\rho}(\rchi^m) D_\rho$$

so it suffices to show that $v_{D_\rho}(\rchi^m) = \langle m, v_\rho \rangle$ for each $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$. To verify this, consider the affine chart $U_\rho = \Spec\mathbb{C}[\rho^\vee \cap M]$ corresponding to the ray $\rho$. On $U_\rho$, the prime ideal corresponding to the generic point of $D_\rho$ is generated by $\{u \in \rho^\vee \cap M \mid \langle u, v_\rho \rangle > 0\}$, and localizing at this prime yields a DVR whose valuation on characters is given by $\rchi^u \mapsto \langle u, v_\rho \rangle$. Hence for any $m \in M$ we have $v_{D_\rho}(\rchi^m) = \langle m, v_\rho \rangle$.
:::

From this we obtain a natural group homomorphism

$$M \rightarrow \Div_T(X_\Sigma);\qquad m \mapsto \divisor(\rchi^m)$$

The image of this homomorphism is the set of principal divisors, and our claim is that this is exactly the kernel of (1).

::: Proposition 4
Assume that for a fan $\Sigma$, the rays $\Sigma(1)$ span $N_\mathbb{R}$. Then the following sequence is exact:

$$0 \rightarrow M \rightarrow \Div_T(X_\Sigma) \rightarrow \Cl(X_\Sigma) \rightarrow 0.$$

Here the first arrow is $m \mapsto \divisor(\rchi^m)$, and the second arrow is $D \mapsto [D]$.
:::

::: Proof
First we show that $M \rightarrow \Div_T(X_\Sigma)$ is injective. If $\divisor(\rchi^m) = 0$, then for all $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, we have $\langle m, v_\rho \rangle = 0$. Assuming that the fan $\Sigma$ spans $N_\mathbb{R}$, the primitive generators $\{v_\rho\}$ span $N_\mathbb{R}$, so $m = 0$.

Next we show that the kernel of $\Div_T(X_\Sigma) \rightarrow \Cl(X_\Sigma)$ coincides exactly with the divisors of characters. By definition, the kernel is the collection of divisors that are principal and simultaneously $T$-invariant. Suppose $D = \divisor(f)$ is $T$-invariant. Since the support of a $T$-invariant divisor is contained in the boundary $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N = \bigcup_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} D_\rho$, restricting $f$ to the open dense subset $T_N$ gives $\divisor(f \rvert_{T_N}) = 0$. Therefore, $f\rvert_{T_N}$ is a unit in the coordinate ring of $T_N$, $\mathbb{C}[M]$. Since the units of $\mathbb{C}[M]$ are precisely of the form $c \cdot \rchi^m$ ($c \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$, $m \in M$), we have $f\rvert_{T_N} = c \cdot \rchi^m$, and since $T_N$ is dense in $X_\Sigma$, we have $f = c \cdot \rchi^m$ on $X_\Sigma$. Therefore, $D = \divisor(\rchi^m)$.

Finally we show surjectivity. For any divisor $D \in \Div(X_\Sigma)$, its restriction to $T_N$, $D\rvert_{T_N}$, is a divisor on $T_N \cong (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^n$. Since the coordinate ring of $T_N$, $\mathbb{C}[M]$, is a UFD, this is principal, and thus for some $f \in \mathbb{C}(X_\Sigma)^\times$ the support of $D - \divisor(f)$ can be made to lie in $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N = \bigcup_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} D_\rho$. Since the right-hand side is expressed as a sum of torus-invariant divisors, $[D] = [D - \divisor(f)]$ has a torus-invariant representative.
:::

This exact sequence becomes a powerful tool for explicitly computing the class group of a toric variety. Under the assumption, $\Div_T(X_\Sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}$ is a free abelian group of rank $\lvert \Sigma(1) \rvert$ and $M \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ is embedded into it as a free abelian group of rank $n$, so $\Cl(X_\Sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)} / M$ is a finitely generated abelian group of rank $\lvert \Sigma(1) \rvert - n$. Note, however, that in general it can have torsion. For instance, in the affine toric variety defined by $\sigma = \mathrm{cone}(e_2, 2e_1 - e_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, $U_\sigma \cong \mathbb{C}^2/(\mathbb{Z}/2)$, we have $\Cl \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$.

## Cartier divisor

On the other hand, to discuss line bundles via divisors, we need to examine Cartier divisors. We examined Weil divisors above, and since Weil divisors and Cartier divisors coincide in the smooth case, this is partially complete; but for toric varieties, there exists a way to describe Cartier divisors (even if they are not smooth).

::: Definition 5
For a fan $\Sigma$, on the *support* $\lvert \Sigma \rvert = \bigcup_{\sigma \in \Sigma} \sigma$, a function $\psi: \lvert \Sigma \rvert \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called a *piecewise linear function* if for each cone $\sigma \in \Sigma$, $\psi\rvert_\sigma$ is of the form $\psi(v) = \langle m_\sigma, v \rangle$ for some $m_\sigma \in M_\mathbb{R}$. A piecewise linear function $\psi$ is called *integral* if each $m_\sigma$ belongs to $M$.
:::

We write $\PL(\Sigma, M_\mathbb{R})$ for the collection of piecewise linear functions, and $\PL(\Sigma, M)$ for the collection of integral piecewise linear functions. The important point then is that a torus-invariant Cartier divisor $D$ determines a $\psi_D \in \PL(\Sigma, M)$.

::: Proposition 6
For a torus-invariant Cartier divisor $D = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} a_\rho D_\rho$, on each maximal cone $\sigma \in \Sigma$, using $D\rvert_{U_\sigma} = \divisor(\rchi^{-m_\sigma})$ to determine $m_\sigma \in M$, the function defined by

$$\psi_D: \lvert \Sigma \rvert \rightarrow \mathbb{R};\qquad \psi_D(v) = \langle m_\sigma, v \rangle \quad \text{for } v \in \sigma$$

is an element of $\PL(\Sigma, M)$, and in particular, for each ray $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, at the primitive generator $v_\rho$, it takes the value $\psi_D(v_\rho) = -a_\rho$.
:::

::: Proof
Assume that $D$ is a Cartier divisor. The definition itself of a Cartier divisor is only that it is *locally* principal, but on an affine toric variety $U_\sigma$, a stronger fact holds: on $U_\sigma$, every $T$-invariant Cartier divisor is globally principal. This is based on the fact that the coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[\sigma^\vee \cap M]$ has an $M$-grading, so cocycle data for an invertible $\mathcal{O}_{U_\sigma}$-module trivializes. Accepting this, for some rational function $f_\sigma \in \mathbb{C}(X_\Sigma)^\times$, we have $D\rvert_{U_\sigma} = \divisor(f_\sigma)$.

Now, since $D$ is $T$-invariant, for any $t \in T_N$ we have $\divisor(t \cdot f_\sigma) = t \cdot \divisor(f_\sigma) = \divisor(f_\sigma)$, and since two rational functions with the same divisor differ only by a scalar, for some $c(t) \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$ we have $t \cdot f_\sigma = c(t) \cdot f_\sigma$. That is, $f_\sigma$ is a weight vector of the $T_N$-action, and therefore for some $m_\sigma \in M$ and $c \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$, it has the form $f_\sigma = c \cdot \rchi^{-m_\sigma}$. (The reason for writing $-m_\sigma$ in the exponent is to make the sign calculation below neat.) Therefore on $U_\sigma$,

$$D\rvert_{U_\sigma} = \divisor(\rchi^{-m_\sigma}) = \sum_{\rho \in \sigma(1)} \langle -m_\sigma, v_\rho \rangle D_\rho$$

and $a_\rho = -\langle m_\sigma, v_\rho \rangle$ for all $\rho \in \sigma(1)$. Now if we define $\psi_D(v) = \langle m_\sigma, v \rangle$, this is linear on $\sigma$, and at $v_\rho$ it takes the desired value $-a_\rho$. For two maximal cones $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$, on the common face $\tau = \sigma_1 \cap \sigma_2$, for $\psi_D$ to be well-defined, $\langle m_{\sigma_1}, v\rangle = \langle m_{\sigma_2}, v\rangle$ ($v \in \tau$) must hold, which follows from the fact that on $U_\tau$, $\rchi^{m_{\sigma_2} - m_{\sigma_1}}$ must be a regular invertible function, that is, a character $\rchi^{m}$ ($m \in \tau^\perp \cap M$).
:::

This correspondence is in fact an isomorphism. That is, the group $\CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma)$ of torus-invariant Cartier divisors is isomorphic to $\PL(\Sigma, M)$.

## Line Bundles

Now that we have examined Cartier divisors to some extent, we will use them to study line bundles. Given a Cartier divisor $D$, we defined the line bundle $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$ in [\[Algebraic Varieties\] §Line Bundles and Vector Bundles, ⁋Definition 17](/en/math/algebraic_varieties/line_bundles#def17){: data-lid="zp0f3" }. Its sheaf of sections is given for each open set $U \subseteq X_\Sigma$ by

$$\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)(U) = \{f \in \mathbb{C}(X_\Sigma)^\times \mid \divisor(f)\rvert_U + D\rvert_U \ge 0\} \cup \{0\}$$

which intuitively means that $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$ is the sheaf of rational functions on $U$ allowed to have poles along $D$ up to order $D$. Even when $D$ is a general Weil divisor and not Cartier, the formula on the right-hand side still gives a well-defined $\mathcal{O}_X$-module sheaf (though not a line bundle, but rather a rank-$1$ sheaf), and below we adopt this formula as the definition of $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$.

In the case of a toric variety, the global sections of this sheaf have an explicit basis given by characters $\rchi^m$, and the condition is completely described by combinatorial data on the lattice $M$.

::: Proposition 7
For a torus-invariant Weil divisor $D = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} a_\rho D_\rho$, the global section space of the sheaf $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$ is given as follows:

$$H^0(X_\Sigma, \mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)) = \bigoplus_{\substack{m \in M \\ \langle m, v_\rho \rangle \ge -a_\rho \text{ for all } \rho}} \mathbb{C} \cdot \rchi^m.$$
:::

::: Proof
Since $D$ is $T_N$-invariant, $T_N$ acts naturally on the sheaf $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$, and this action defines a *rational* representation of the algebraic torus. Since a rational representation of an algebraic torus always decomposes into weight spaces by characters, the global section space also decomposes as

$$H^0(X_\Sigma, \mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)) = \bigoplus_{m \in M} H^0(X_\Sigma, \mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D))_m.$$

Here, the component of weight $m$ consists of sections that, under the action of $t \in T_N$, are multiplied by $\rchi^m(t)$. However, such sections are precisely of the form $c \cdot \rchi^m$ ($c \in \mathbb{C}$). Indeed, if $s \in H^0(\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D))_m$, then $s/\rchi^m$ is a $T$-invariant rational function on $X_\Sigma$, and since $T_N$ is open dense in $X_\Sigma$, we have $\mathbb{C}(X_\Sigma)^{T_N} = \mathbb{C}(T_N)^{T_N} = \Frac(\mathbb{C}[M])^{T_N} = \mathbb{C}$, so $s = c \cdot \rchi^m$. That is, each weight space is spanned by $\rchi^m$ and is $1$-dimensional (when that $m$ gives a section).

Now, the condition for $c \cdot \rchi^m$ to be a section is $\divisor(\rchi^m) + D \ge 0$. By [Proposition 3](#prop3){: data-lid="aay6a" },

$$\divisor(\rchi^m) + D = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} (\langle m, v_\rho \rangle + a_\rho) D_\rho$$

therefore, the necessary and sufficient condition for this divisor to be effective is that for all $\rho$, $\langle m, v_\rho \rangle + a_\rho \ge 0$, that is, $\langle m, v_\rho \rangle \ge -a_\rho$ holds. For such $m \in M$, the direct sum of the corresponding 1-dimensional character spaces $\mathbb{C} \cdot \rchi^m$ forms the entire global section space.
:::

Considering the condition *$\langle m, v_\rho\rangle \ge -a_\rho$ for all $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$* appearing in [Proposition 7](#prop7){: data-lid="p6ch1" }, we can collect the $m$ satisfying this condition to define the polyhedron in $M_\mathbb{R}$

$$\Delta_D = \{m \in M_\mathbb{R} \mid \langle m, v_\rho \rangle \ge -a_\rho \text{ for all } \rho \in \Sigma(1)\}.$$

At this point, we know that the collection of lattice points of $\Delta_D$ coincides exactly with the $m$'s of the summands.

Meanwhile, this is not the first time we encounter a situation where the lattice points inside a polyhedron carry geometric meaning: in [§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#prop9){: data-lid="pavgm" }, for a lattice polytope $P$, from its lattice points $P \cap M = \{m_0, \ldots, m_s\}$ we constructed a monomial map $\phi_P: T_N \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^s$, and showed that the Zariski closure of the image of this map is isomorphic to $X_P$. Then, viewed from the perspective of [Proposition 7](#prop7){: data-lid="ybvrb" } above, these lattice points, corresponding precisely to $P = \Delta_D$, coincide with the character basis of $H^0(X_\Sigma, \mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D))$. That is, the projective embedding determined by lattice points was in fact the standard linear system embedding determined by the global sections of the line bundle $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$.

This perspective makes it natural to ask how the geometric condition of being ample on a toric variety translates into the combinatorics of the fan. To state the result first, this is completely described by the convexity of a piecewise linear function.

::: Definition 8
A piecewise linear function $\psi: \lvert \Sigma \rvert \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is *strictly convex* if for any two distinct maximal cones $\sigma_1, \sigma_2 \in \Sigma$ with corresponding $m_{\sigma_1}, m_{\sigma_2} \in M_\mathbb{R}$,

$$\psi(v) = \langle m_{\sigma_1}, v \rangle \text{ for } v \in \sigma_1, \qquad \psi(v) = \langle m_{\sigma_2}, v \rangle \text{ for } v \in \sigma_2$$

and outside $\sigma_1 \cap \sigma_2$, for any point in $\sigma_1$, $v$,

$$\psi(v) < \langle m_{\sigma_2}, v \rangle$$

always holds. Equivalently, for each maximal cone $\sigma$, the data $m_\sigma$ defines a linear function $\langle m_\sigma, \cdot\rangle$ that is an *upper bound* for $\psi$, with equality holding exactly when $v \in \sigma$ (under the sign convention $\psi_D(v_\rho) = -a_\rho$ of [Proposition 6](#prop6){: data-lid="zm1jq" }, for ample $D$, $\psi_D$ is convex upward in this sense).
:::

A strictly convex piecewise linear function is a strong condition that completely determines the structure of the fan, and the following theorem shows that this condition corresponds exactly to the ampleness of a divisor.

::: Proposition 9
When the toric variety $X_\Sigma$ is complete, a torus-invariant Cartier divisor $D$ is ample if and only if the corresponding piecewise linear function $\psi_D$ is strictly convex.
:::

::: Proof
($\Rightarrow$) Assume $D$ is ample. By definition, for some $k > 0$ the divisor $kD$ is very ample, inducing a projective embedding $\phi_{kD}: X_\Sigma \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^N$, and this embedding coincides with the monomial map defined by the lattice points of the polytope $\Delta_{kD} \cap M$ ([§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#prop9){: data-lid="d9t7w" }). Since $X_\Sigma$ is complete, the image $\overline{\phi_{kD}(T_N)}$ is isomorphic to the projective toric variety obtained from the normal fan of $\Delta_{kD}$ (see [CLS] Theorem 6.2.1), and the isomorphism with $X_\Sigma$ yields that the normal fan of $\Delta_{kD}$ coincides with $\Sigma$. This means that $\psi_{kD} = k\psi_D$ is strictly convex, and hence $\psi_D$ is also strictly convex.

($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, assume $\psi_D$ is strictly convex. This means that $\Sigma$ is the normal fan of some lattice polytope. Specifically, by strict convexity

$$\Delta_D = \{m \in M_\mathbb{R} \mid \langle m, v_\rho \rangle \ge -a_\rho \text{ for all } \rho \in \Sigma(1)\}$$

is a bounded region and hence a lattice polytope. Since the normal fan of this polytope coincides with $\Sigma$, by [§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#prop8){: data-lid="2q0mm" } the variety $X_\Sigma$ is projective. Moreover, the line bundle $\mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)$ corresponding to $D$ defines a very ample line bundle, so $D$ is ample.
:::

## The Picard Group of a Toric Variety

Finally, we describe the Picard group of a toric variety. The Picard group is the group of isomorphism classes of line bundles under tensor product, and it is isomorphic to the group of linear equivalence classes of Cartier divisors.

::: Proposition 10
Assume that for the fan $\Sigma$, its rays $\Sigma(1)$ span $N_\mathbb{R}$. Then the following hold for the toric variety $X_\Sigma$:

1. The Picard group $\Pic(X_\Sigma)$ is isomorphic to the group of linear equivalence classes of torus-invariant Cartier divisors, $\CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma) / M$.
2. $\Pic(X_\Sigma)$ is a subgroup of $\Cl(X_\Sigma)$, and the following commutative diagram holds:

    {% diagram Math/Toric_Geometry/Toric_Divisors_and_Line_Bundles-1.svg width="32.02em" alt="Pic-to-cl-com.diag" %}

    Here the vertical arrows represent inclusions.
:::

::: Proof
(1) For a general algebraic variety, the quotient of the group of Cartier divisors $\CaDiv(X)$ by principal divisors gives the Picard group. For a toric variety, torus-invariant Cartier divisors alone suffice to represent every linear equivalence class, so $\Pic(X_\Sigma) \cong \CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma) / M$ holds.

(2) The image of $M \rightarrow \Div_T(X_\Sigma)$, namely $\divisor(\rchi^m)$, consists of principal divisors and hence is automatically Cartier, so this arrow factors through $M \rightarrow \CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma)$. Consequently the left two columns of the commutative diagram share the same $M$, and the quotient $\Pic(X_\Sigma) = \CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma)/M$ naturally sits inside $\Cl(X_\Sigma) = \Div_T(X_\Sigma)/M$ as a subgroup. Injectivity follows from the fact that $\CaDiv_T(X_\Sigma) \hookrightarrow \Div_T(X_\Sigma)$ is injective and both quotients are by the same image of $M$.
:::

The Picard group of a toric variety can be described explicitly in the language of piecewise linear functions. Quotienting $\PL(\Sigma, M)$ by the globally linear functions, namely those given globally by a single $m \in M$ as $\psi(v) = \langle m, v \rangle$, yields the Picard group. This provides a powerful method for computing combinatorial invariants of a toric variety.

Finally, let us apply the tools we have developed so far to the most familiar toric variety, projective space $\mathbb{P}^n$. ([§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Example 10](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#ex10){: data-lid="fsnge" })

::: Example 11
The rays of the fan defining $\mathbb{P}^n$ are those generated by $v_0 = -e_1 - \cdots - e_n$ and $v_i = e_i$ ($1 \le i \le n$), namely $\rho_0, \rho_1, \ldots, \rho_n$, and each maximal cone $\sigma_i$, excluding $\rho_i$ from these, is generated by the remaining $n$ as an $n$-dimensional cone, corresponding to $\mathbb{P}^n$'s standard affine chart $U_{\sigma_i} = \{\x_i \neq 0\}$ ([§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Example 10](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#ex10){: data-lid="efajy" }). Each torus-invariant prime divisor $D_i = D_{\rho_i}$ corresponds to the coordinate hyperplane

$$D_0 = \{\x_0 = 0\}, \quad D_1 = \{\x_1 = 0\}, \quad \ldots, \quad D_n = \{\x_n = 0\}$$

in homogeneous coordinates. These are each hyperplane divisors on $\mathbb{P}^n$, and from basic algebraic geometry we know that they determine a single *hyperplane class*. For convenience, let us choose $H = D_0$ as its representative.

For $H$, let us compute the corresponding piecewise linear function $\psi_H$. Under the monomial map convention $(t_1, \ldots, t_n) \mapsto [1 : t_1 : \cdots : t_n]$ of [§Definition of Toric Varieties, ⁋Example 10](/en/math/toric_geometry/toric_varieties#ex10){: data-lid="gw9lo" }, we have $t_i = \x_i/\x_0$, so the local equation of $H = \{\x_0 = 0\}$ on each affine chart is:

- When $i = 0$, on $U_{\sigma_0} = \{\x_0 \neq 0\}$, $H$ is empty, so $H\rvert_{U_{\sigma_0}} = \divisor(1) = \divisor(\rchi^0)$, i.e. $m_{\sigma_0} = 0$;
- When $i \ge 1$, the local equation of $H \cap U_{\sigma_i}$ is $\x_0/\x_i = t_i^{-1} = \rchi^{-e_i^\ast}$, so from the convention $H\rvert_{U_{\sigma_i}} = \divisor(\rchi^{-m_{\sigma_i}})$ of [Proposition 6](#prop6){: data-lid="tbce9" }, we have $m_{\sigma_i} = e_i^\ast$.

From this, $\psi_H$ is given on $\sigma_0$ by $0$, and on $\sigma_i$ ($i \ge 1$) by $\langle e_i^\ast, -\rangle$, and its values on the rays are $\psi_H(v_0) = -1$ and $\psi_H(v_i) = 0$ ($i \ge 1$), which is consistent with $\psi_H(v_\rho) = -a_\rho$ of [Proposition 6](#prop6){: data-lid="n39yn" }.

That this $\psi_H$ is strictly convex can be verified by directly applying [Definition 8](#def8){: data-lid="hfp5t" }: for each maximal cone $\sigma_i$, taking a point $v$ in its interior (i.e., writing it as a positive combination of the generators of $\sigma_i$, so that for $\sigma_i = \mathrm{cone}(v_l : l \neq i)$ and its generators $\{v_l\}_{l \neq i}$, $v = \sum_{l \neq i} a_l v_l$, $a_l > 0$) and, for another maximal cone $\sigma_k$, evaluating with $m_{\sigma_k}$, one can directly compute that in every case $\psi_H(v) = \langle m_{\sigma_i}, v\rangle$ is strictly smaller than $\langle m_{\sigma_k}, v\rangle$. For instance, when $i \ge 1$ and $k = 0$, we have $\langle e_i^\ast, v\rangle = -a_0 < 0 = \langle 0, v\rangle$, and when $i = 0$ and $k \ge 1$, we have $0 = \langle 0, v\rangle < a_k = \langle e_k^\ast, v\rangle$. Therefore, by [Proposition 9](#prop9){: data-lid="gggje" }, $H$ is ample, which is a toric reconfirmation of the familiar fact that the hyperplane divisor on $\mathbb{P}^n$ is ample.
