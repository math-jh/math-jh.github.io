Toric geometry is, quite literally, the study of a special class of algebraic varieties called toric varieties. The advantage that toric varieties hold over general algebraic varieties is that, because they are essentially built from combinatorial information, it is easier to carry out more concrete computations. In this post, we first examine the simplest toric varieties, namely affine toric varieties.

## Lattices and Cones

Intuitively, a toric variety can be thought of as being obtained by generalizing the process of gluing copies of $\mathbb{A}^k_\mathbb{C}$ to obtain $\mathbb{P}^k_\mathbb{C}$. The ways of gluing them are now recorded inside *lattices*.

::: Definition 1
An abelian group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^n$, $N$, is called a *lattice*. The *dual lattice* of a lattice $N$ is given by the formula

$$M = \Hom(N, \mathbb{Z})$$

Then the natural evaluation pairing $M\times N \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}$, $(m, v) \mapsto m(v)$ is called the *dual pairing*.
:::

For convenience of discussion, let us introduce the two notations

$$N_{\mathbb{R}} = N \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{R},\qquad M_{\mathbb{R}} = M \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{R}$$

We now define the following.

::: Definition 2
A subset of $N_{\mathbb{R}}$, $\sigma$, is called a *strongly convex rational polyhedral cone* if it satisfies the following conditions:

1. $\sigma$ is a cone with the origin as its vertex. That is, $\lambda v \in \sigma$ holds for all $v \in \sigma$, $\lambda \ge 0$.
2. $\sigma$ is generated by finitely many vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_s \in N$ as $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$-linear combinations. That is, we may write $\sigma = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} v_1 + \cdots + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} v_s$.
3. $\sigma$ is *strongly convex*. That is, $\sigma \cap (-\sigma) = \{0\}$ holds.
:::

Here, the first condition alone does not guarantee that $\sigma$ is convex, but the $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$-linear combination form in the second condition automatically implies that $\sigma$ is a closed convex cone. In addition, the third condition means that $\sigma$ contains no line passing through the origin. Meanwhile, for each cone $\sigma$, we can define the following.

::: Definition 3
For a cone $\sigma$, a *face* $\tau$ is obtained, where on $\sigma$, $\langle u, v \rangle \ge 0$ holds for all $v \in \sigma$ for some $u \in M_{\mathbb{R}}$, as

$$\tau = \sigma \cap u^{\perp} = \{ v \in \sigma \mid \langle u, v \rangle = 0 \}$$

When $\tau$ is a face of $\sigma$, we write $\tau \prec \sigma$.
:::

The toric varieties we shall define first carry each cone like an affine chart, and the gluing data is determined by how these cones meet along faces. To write this down rigorously, in this post we examine affine toric varieties.

## Affine Toric Varieties

Given a cone $\sigma$, let us consider its *dual cone*

$$\sigma^\vee = \{ u \in M_{\mathbb{R}} \mid \langle u, v \rangle \ge 0 \text{ for all } v \in \sigma \}$$

::: Definition 4
For a cone $\sigma$, we define the semigroup

$$S_\sigma = \sigma^\vee \cap M = \{ u \in M \mid \langle u, v \rangle \ge 0 \text{ for all } v \in \sigma \}$$

and through this define the *semigroup algebra*

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}[{\rchi}^u \mid u \in S_\sigma]$$

([\[Algebraic Structures\] §Algebras, ⁋Definition 5](/en/math/algebraic_structures/algebras#def5){: data-lid="117ax" }). Here $\rchi^u$ is the monomial corresponding to the element of $M$, $u$.
:::

Unwinding this, we have the following. By definition, $S_\sigma$ is a semigroup under addition, and the semigroup algebra $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is most neatly described as the free vector space on the set $S_\sigma$ over $\mathbb{C}$ endowed with the multiplication

$$\rchi^u\cdot\rchi^{u'}=\rchi^{u+u'}$$

. That is, $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ has $\{\rchi^u \mid u\in S_\sigma\}$ as a basis and is the $\mathbb{C}$-algebra generated by the above multiplication. Meanwhile, it is known by [Gordan's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordan%27s_lemma) that $S_\sigma$ is a finitely generated semigroup, and therefore $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{C}$-algebra. Then this $\mathbb{C}$-algebra becomes the coordinate ring of the affine toric variety.

::: Definition 5
For a strongly convex rational polyhedral cone $\sigma \subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}$, we define the *affine toric variety* $U_\sigma$ that it defines by

$$U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma])$$
:::

::: Example 6
In the case where $\sigma = \{0\}$, since $\sigma^\vee = M_{\mathbb{R}}$, we have $S_\sigma = M$. Therefore

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}[M] = \mathbb{C}[\rchi^{\pm e_1^\ast}, \ldots, \rchi^{\pm e_n^\ast}]$$

which corresponds to the coordinate ring of $(\mathbb{C}^\ast)^n$.
:::

::: Example 7
In $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$, consider the case where $\sigma$ is generated by $e_1$ and $e_2$. Then, since

$$\sigma^\vee = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} e_1^\ast + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} e_2^\ast$$

we have $S_\sigma = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2$, and

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}[\rchi^{e_1^\ast}, \rchi^{e_2^\ast}] = \mathbb{C}[\z_1, \z_2]$$

. Therefore, $U_\sigma = \mathbb{C}^2$.
:::

## Smoothness

In [Example 6](#ex6){: data-lid="c33hh" } above with $\sigma = \{0\}$ and in [Example 7](#ex7){: data-lid="v0sbv" } with the standard quadrant $\sigma = \mathrm{cone}(e_1, e_2)$, $U_\sigma$ was $(\mathbb{C}^\ast)^n$ and $\mathbb{C}^2$ respectively, both of which were smooth algebraic varieties. We will now see that this is not a coincidence, and that the smoothness of $U_\sigma$ is completely determined solely by the combinatorial data of the cone $\sigma$.

::: Definition 8
A strongly convex rational polyhedral cone $\sigma \subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}$ is called a *smooth* (or *regular*, *nonsingular*) cone if the primitive ray generators of $\sigma$, $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ ($k = \dim \sigma$), form, in the lattice $N$, part of some $\mathbb{Z}$-basis.
:::

The definition itself may appear somewhat abstract, but in practice it is equivalent to the conjunction of the following two conditions.

1. $\sigma$ is *simplicial*. That is, the number of rays equals the dimension.
2. (When $\sigma$ is full-dimensional) the determinant of the matrix collecting the primitive ray generators $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ as columns, $[v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n] \in \mathrm{Mat}_n(\mathbb{Z})$, is $\pm 1$.

In particular, for a two-dimensional cone in $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$, one only needs to check whether the determinant of the $2 \times 2$ matrix formed by the two ray generators is $\pm 1$, and indeed [Example 7](#ex7){: data-lid="cwte2" } satisfies this.

::: Proposition 9
An affine toric variety $U_\sigma$ is a smooth algebraic variety if and only if $\sigma$ is a smooth cone. More specifically, if $\sigma$ is smooth and $k = \dim \sigma$, then

$$U_\sigma \cong \mathbb{C}^k \times (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^{n-k}$$

holds.
:::

::: Proof
($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\sigma$ is smooth. By definition, the ray generators $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ form part of some basis of $N$, say $\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$. Choosing the dual basis $\{v_1^\ast, \ldots, v_n^\ast\} \subseteq M$ of this basis,

$$\sigma^\vee = \{u \in M_{\mathbb{R}} \mid \langle u, v_i\rangle \ge 0,\ i = 1, \ldots, k\} = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\langle v_1^\ast, \ldots, v_k^\ast\rangle + \mathbb{R}\langle v_{k+1}^\ast, \ldots, v_n^\ast\rangle$$

and therefore

$$S_\sigma = \sigma^\vee \cap M = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\langle v_1^\ast, \ldots, v_k^\ast\rangle \oplus \mathbb{Z}\langle v_{k+1}^\ast, \ldots, v_n^\ast\rangle$$

holds. Then since

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}[\rchi^{v_1^\ast}, \ldots, \rchi^{v_k^\ast}, \rchi^{\pm v_{k+1}^\ast}, \ldots, \rchi^{\pm v_n^\ast}] \cong \mathbb{C}[x_1, \ldots, x_k, x_{k+1}^{\pm 1}, \ldots, x_n^{\pm 1}]$$

$U_\sigma \cong \mathbb{C}^k \times (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^{n-k}$ is smooth.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $U_\sigma$ is smooth. First, setting $N_\sigma = N \cap \span_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)$, the quotient $N/N_\sigma$ is torsion-free, so $N$ decomposes as $N = N_\sigma \oplus N'$, and accordingly $M$ decomposes as $M = M_\sigma \oplus M'$ (the elements vanishing on $N'$ and on $N_\sigma$, respectively). Since the elements of $M'$ vanish on $\sigma \subseteq (N_\sigma)_{\mathbb{R}}$, we have $S_\sigma = (S_\sigma \cap M_\sigma) \oplus M'$, where $S_\sigma \cap M_\sigma$ is the semigroup obtained by regarding $\sigma$ as a cone in the lattice $N_\sigma$. Hence by [Example 6](#ex6){: data-lid="aj052" },

$$U_\sigma \cong \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma \cap M_\sigma]) \times (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^{n-k}$$

and since $U_\sigma$ is smooth, $\Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma \cap M_\sigma])$ is also smooth. Therefore, replacing $N$ by $N_\sigma$, we may assume from the start that $\sigma$ is full-dimensional, that is, $k = n$.

In this case, since $\sigma$ is strongly convex, $\sigma^\vee$ is full-dimensional, and since $\sigma$ is full-dimensional, $\sigma^\vee \cap (-\sigma^\vee) = \{0\}$. From the former, taking a lattice point $u_0 \in M$ in the interior of $\sigma^\vee$, for any $m \in M$ both $\ell u_0$ and $\ell u_0 + m$ belong to $S_\sigma$ for sufficiently large $\ell$, so $S_\sigma$ generates $M$ as a group. From the latter, if $u \in S_\sigma \setminus \{0\}$ and $u' \in S_\sigma$, then $u + u' \neq 0$, so the subspace $\mathfrak{m}$ spanned by the $\rchi^u$ ($u \in S_\sigma \setminus \{0\}$) is a maximal ideal satisfying $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}\rchi^0 \oplus \mathfrak{m}$. Now $\mathfrak{m}^2$ is spanned by the $\rchi^{u + u'}$ ($u, u' \in S_\sigma \setminus \{0\}$), so the cotangent space $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ has as a basis the $\rchi^u$ corresponding to the minimal generators of $S_\sigma$. Since $U_\sigma$ is smooth, this dimension is $n = \dim U_\sigma$, and hence $S_\sigma$ is generated by $n$ elements $u_1, \ldots, u_n$. These generate $M \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ as a group, so they form a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis of $M$; then from $\sigma^\vee = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\langle u_1, \ldots, u_n\rangle$, $\sigma = (\sigma^\vee)^\vee$ is generated by the dual basis $u_1^\ast, \ldots, u_n^\ast \in N$. That is, the primitive ray generators of $\sigma$ form a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis of $N$.

Returning to the general case, it follows that the primitive ray generators of $\sigma$ form a basis of $N_\sigma$, and adjoining a basis of $N'$ to it gives a basis of $N$, so $\sigma$ is smooth.
:::

For example, in $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$, if we take $\sigma = \mathrm{cone}(e_2,\ 2e_1 - e_2)$, the matrix formed by the primitive generators of the two rays satisfies

$$\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = -2$$

so $\sigma$ is simplicial but not smooth. In fact, computing the minimal generators of $S_\sigma$ yields the three elements $u_1 = e_1^\ast$, $u_2 = e_1^\ast + e_2^\ast$, $u_3 = e_1^\ast + 2 e_2^\ast$, and since the relation $\rchi^{u_1}\rchi^{u_3} = (\rchi^{u_2})^2$ holds,

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \cong \mathbb{C}[x, y, z]/(xz - y^2)$$

and $U_\sigma$ becomes an affine toric variety having an $A_1$ singular point at the origin. In general, if the determinant is $\pm d$, then $U_\sigma$ has a $\mathbb{Z}/d$ quotient singularity at the origin.

## Torus Action

In order to define a torus action on $\Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma])$, we examine the already familiar example of $\mathbb{P}^r$. In the case of $\mathbb{P}^r = \Proj(\mathbb{C}[\x_0, \ldots, \x_r])$, when $\mathbb{C}^\ast$ acts by scaling, the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[\x_0, \ldots, \x_r]$ decomposes degree-by-degree. Each homogeneous component $\mathbb{C}[\x_0, \ldots, \x_r]_d$ forms an eigenspace as a $\mathbb{C}^\ast$-module.

Similarly, on $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ there also exists a natural $M$-grading

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \bigoplus_{u \in S_\sigma} \mathbb{C} \cdot \rchi^u$$

and when the $n$-dimensional algebraic torus $T_N$, which will be defined shortly, acts on $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$, each 1-dimensional subspace $\mathbb{C}\cdot\rchi^u$ becomes an eigenspace of weight $u \in M$. That is, for any $t \in T_N$, $\rchi^u$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\rchi^u(t) \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$. This $M$-grading precisely represents the weight decomposition for the torus action.

Meanwhile, we define $T_N$ as follows:

$$T_N = N \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{C}^\ast.$$

This is the tensor product of two abelian groups, but since the operation on $\mathbb{C}^\ast$ is multiplication, it may be somewhat confusing. First, as a set, $T_N$ is formed by taking, for $v \in N$ and $z \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$, finite sums of elements of the form $v \otimes z$, and is an abelian group under addition. Here, by bilinearity, the following relations hold:

$$(v_1 + v_2) \otimes z = v_1 \otimes z + v_2 \otimes z,\qquad v \otimes (z_1 z_2) = v \otimes z_1 + v \otimes z_2,\qquad (k v) \otimes z = v \otimes z^k \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z})$$

Notice here that the left-hand side of the second equation is multiplication in $\mathbb{C}^\ast$ and the right-hand side is addition in $T_N$. That is, inside the tensor product, multiplication in $\mathbb{C}^\ast$ is transferred to addition in $T_N$.

Now, introducing for $N$ a basis $e_1, \ldots, e_n$, any element of $T_N$ can be written as follows:

$$t = e_1 \otimes z_1 + \cdots + e_n \otimes z_n = (z_1, \ldots, z_n), \qquad z_i \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$$

Then under this notation, the addition of two elements is given by

$$t + t' = e_1 \otimes (z_1 z_1') + \cdots + e_n \otimes (z_n z_n') = (z_1 z_1', \ldots, z_n z_n')$$

and therefore $T_N$ is naturally interpreted as the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{C}^\ast)^n$. Now, for an element of $M = \Hom(N, \mathbb{Z})$, say $m$, let us define a group homomorphism $\rchi^m : T_N \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^\ast$ by the formula

$$\rchi^m(t) := z^{m(v)}, \qquad t = v \otimes z \in T_N, \quad m(v) \in \mathbb{Z}$$

Our claim is that this is a well-defined group homomorphism. First, any element of $T_N$ is given in the form of a finite sum $t = \sum_i v_i \otimes z_i$, and if we define for this element

$$\rchi^m(t) := \prod_i z_i^{m(v_i)}$$

one can verify that this gives a well-defined function extending the definition above. Meanwhile, for any two elements

$$t = \sum_i v_i \otimes z_i,\qquad t' = \sum_j v_j' \otimes z_j'$$

we have

$$\rchi^m(t + t') = \rchi^m\left(\sum_i v_i \otimes z_i + \sum_j v_j' \otimes z_j'\right) = \prod_i z_i^{m(v_i)} \prod_j (z_j')^{m(v_j')} = \rchi^m(t)\rchi^m(t')$$

and hence $\rchi^m$ is a group homomorphism. More specifically, introducing for $N$ a basis $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ and the dual basis $e_1^\ast, \ldots, e_n^\ast \in M$, any $m \in M$ can be written as $m = m_1 e_1^\ast + \cdots + m_n e_n^\ast$, and accordingly

$$\rchi^m(t) = z_1^{m_1} \cdots z_n^{m_n}$$

holds. That is, the *character group* $\Hom(T_N, \mathbb{C}^\ast)$ is isomorphic to the dual lattice $M$.

Now, based on the understanding of the torus as above, on $U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma])$ we define a $T_N$-action. From the fact that $\Spec$ is a contravariant functor, the geometric action $T_N \times U_\sigma \rightarrow U_\sigma$ is encoded by a comodule structure on the coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$, and how this contravariance manifests at the level of points is checked directly in [Example 14](#ex14){: data-lid="uyayd" }.

Specifically, let us define the following $\mathbb{C}$-algebra homomorphism:

$$\rho : \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \rightarrow \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C}[M], \qquad \rchi^u \mapsto \rchi^u \otimes \rchi^u.$$

Here $\mathbb{C}[M] = \mathbb{C}[\rchi^m \mid m \in M]$ is the coordinate ring of $T_N$, and $\rchi^u \in \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is an eigenvector with weight $u$. That $\rho$ is a well-defined algebra homomorphism can be verified directly from bilinearity.

Strictly speaking, $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \otimes \mathbb{C}[M]$ corresponds geometrically, upon taking Spec, to $U_\sigma \times T_N \rightarrow U_\sigma$, so this is the comorphism of a right action of $T_N$. However, because $T_N$ is abelian, right and left actions can be treated as the same, and we will regard it as a left action $T_N \times U_\sigma \rightarrow U_\sigma$. Then for each $t\in T_N$, the action of $t$ on $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is given by the composition

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \xrightarrow{\rho} \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C}[M] \xrightarrow{\id \otimes \ev_t} \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$$

Applying this composition to $\rchi^u$,

$$(\id \otimes \ev_t)(\rchi^u \otimes \rchi^u) = \rchi^u \otimes \rchi^u(t) = \rchi^u(t) \rchi^u$$

and specifically, the result of $t$ acting on $\rchi^u \in \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is $\rchi^u(t) \rchi^u$. Since counitality $(\id \otimes \ev_e)\circ\rho = \id$ and coassociativity $(\rho \otimes \id)\circ\rho = (\id\otimes\Delta)\circ\rho$ are directly verified on basis elements $\rchi^u$, $\rho$ defines a $\mathbb{C}[M]$-comodule structure, which corresponds precisely to an algebraic $T_N$-action on $U_\sigma$. Therefore, the following holds.

::: Proposition 10
The algebraic torus $T_N$ acts on the affine toric variety $U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma])$. First, on the coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$, we define the following $\mathbb{C}$-algebra homomorphism $\rho$:

$$\rho : \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \rightarrow \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C}[M], \qquad \rchi^u \mapsto \rchi^u \otimes \rchi^u.$$

Then this $\rho$ defines on $U_\sigma$ a $T_N$-action, and for each $t \in T_N$, the result of $t$ acting on $\rchi^u \in \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is as follows:

$$t \cdot \rchi^u = \rchi^u(t) \rchi^u.$$

Here, $\rchi^u : T_N \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^\ast$ is the character corresponding to $u \in M$, and $\rchi^u(t) \in \mathbb{C}^\ast$ is its value.
:::

A word of caution is that in the discussion so far, the symbol $\rchi^u$ is being used in two different senses. First, $\rchi^u$ is by its definition a group homomorphism from $T_N$ to $\mathbb{C}^\ast$, but as an element of the vector space $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$, when we consider $\rchi^u$, we think of it as an eigenvector having weight $u$. The formula above

$$t\cdot \rchi^u=\rchi^u(t)\rchi^u$$

is precisely what shows the relationship between these two: specifically, on the vector space $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$, when $T_N$ acts, $\rchi^u$ is an eigenvector for this action, and the corresponding eigenvalue is given precisely by $\rchi^u(t)$.

Another subtlety is that the action of $T_N$ on functions defined above, $t\cdot f$, is more precisely given by the *pullback*

$$(t\cdot f)(x) := f(t\cdot x).$$

In general, for an arbitrary group algebra, defining the left action on functions as

$$(g\cdot f)(x) = f(g^{-1}\cdot x)$$

is standard, but because $T_N$ is abelian, defining it without an inverse as above still yields a left action. The advantage of this convention is that $\rchi^u$ becomes precisely an eigenvector having weight $u$; had we followed the standard convention, the weight of $\rchi^u$ would have been $-u$. It is for the same reason that the action on points to be examined shortly turns out to be $(t_1, t_2)\cdot(z_1, z_2) = (t_1 z_1, t_2 z_2)$ without an inverse.

In any case, the torus action defined above has an open dense torus orbit inside $U_\sigma$, and this orbit is precisely the torus $T_N$ itself. That is, the following holds.

::: Proposition 11
Every affine toric variety $U_\sigma$ contains the torus $T_N$ as an open dense subset.
:::

::: Proof
In the case where $\sigma = \{0\}$, since $\sigma^\vee = M_{\mathbb{R}}$, we have $S_\sigma = M$, and thus $U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[M]) = T_N$.

In the general case, since $S_\sigma \subseteq M$, we have $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \subseteq \mathbb{C}[M]$, which induces a natural dominant morphism

$$T_N = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[M]) \rightarrow \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]) = U_\sigma.$$

Moreover, since $\sigma$ is strongly convex, $\sigma^\vee$ is a full-dimensional cone in $M_{\mathbb{R}}$, and there exists a lattice point $u_0 \in M$ in its interior. For any $m \in M$, if we take sufficiently large $\ell$, both $\ell u_0$ and $\ell u_0 + m$ belong to $S_\sigma$, so by $m = (\ell u_0 + m) - \ell u_0$, $S_\sigma$ generates all of $M$ as a group, and the fraction field of $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ coincides with $\mathbb{C}(M)$. Therefore, $T_N \hookrightarrow U_\sigma$ is an open dense subset.
:::

Next, we examine how the face structure manifests on an affine toric variety. For this, the following lemma is key.

::: Lemma 12 (Separation lemma)
For a cone $\sigma$ and its face $\tau$, there exists $u \in S_\sigma$ satisfying

$$\tau = \sigma \cap u^{\perp}.$$

Moreover, for such $u$,

$$\tau^\vee = \sigma^\vee + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}(-u)$$

holds.
:::

The proof of this lemma is beyond the scope of this post, so we omit it and refer to [CLS]. From this, the following proposition follows.

::: Proposition 13
For a face $\tau$ of a cone $\sigma$, $U_\tau$ is a principal open subset of $U_\sigma$. ([\[Algebraic Varieties\] §Affine Varieties, ⁋Definition 5](/en/math/algebraic_varieties/affine_varieties#def5){: data-lid="kc019" }) Specifically, choosing $u \in S_\sigma$ satisfying $\tau = \sigma \cap u^{\perp}$, we have

$$U_\tau = \{ x \in U_\sigma \mid \rchi^u(x) \neq 0 \}$$
:::

::: Proof
Let $u \in S_\sigma$ and $\tau = \sigma \cap u^\perp$. Since $\tau^\vee = \sigma^\vee + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}(-u)$ holds by [Lemma 12](#lem12){: data-lid="cww19" }, we have

$$S_\tau = \tau^\vee \cap M = (\sigma^\vee + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}(-u)) \cap M = S_\sigma + \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}(-u)$$

The last equality is obtained by verifying, from the fact that $u \in S_\sigma$ and $-u \in M$, that any lattice point of $\sigma^\vee + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}(-u)$ can be expressed in the form $w - ku$ for some $w \in S_\sigma$ and $k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$. From this we obtain the localization of the semigroup algebra

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\tau] = \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma + \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}(-u)] = \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma][\rchi^{-u}] = \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]_{\rchi^u}$$

Here $\rchi^u \in \mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ and $\rchi^{-u}$ is the inverse of $\rchi^u$. Therefore,

$$U_\tau = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\tau]) = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]_{\rchi^u}) = (U_\sigma)_{\rchi^u} = \{ x \in U_\sigma \mid \rchi^u(x) \neq 0 \}$$

holds.
:::

For instance, in the case of the two-dimensional cone $\sigma$ of [Example 7](#ex7){: data-lid="jz4bh" }, the faces of $\sigma$, $\tau_1=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}e_1$ and $\tau_2=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}e_2$, are each also one-dimensional cones, and their face is the origin. Under this structure, inside the whole $U_\sigma$, each of $U_{\tau_1}$ and $U_{\tau_2}$ is a principal open set, and according to this face structure $U_\sigma$ acquires a stratification similar to a CW complex.

We claim that the above open embedding $U_\tau \hookrightarrow U_\sigma$ is $T_N$-equivariant. That is, when $T_N$ acts on $U_\tau$ and $U_\sigma$ respectively, these actions are compatible with the inclusion. This can be shown by a straightforward computation, and therefore the above inclusion is also an inclusion as toric varieties.

::: Example 14
In [Example 7](#ex7){: data-lid="w82de" }, we saw that when $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$ and $\sigma = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}e_1+ \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}e_2$, we have $U_\sigma = \mathbb{C}^2$. Let us now examine concretely how the torus $T_N = (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^2$ acts on $U_\sigma = \mathbb{C}^2$.

First, as seen in [Example 7](#ex7){: data-lid="5hr15" }, $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] = \mathbb{C}[\z_1, \z_2]$ ($\z_i = \rchi^{e_i^\ast}$), and $U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[\z_1, \z_2]) = \mathbb{C}^2$. Now according to [Proposition 10](#prop10){: data-lid="k15rn" }, $t = (t_1, t_2) \in T_N = (\mathbb{C}^\ast)^2$ acts on the coordinate ring via the formula

$$t \cdot \z_i = \rchi^{e_i^\ast}(t) \z_i = t_i \z_i \qquad i = 1, 2$$

To read off the action on points from this, recall that in $\mathbb{C}^2$, a point $(z_1, z_2)$ corresponds to the maximal ideal of $\mathbb{C}[\z_1, \z_2]$, $\mathfrak{m} = (\z_1 - z_1, \z_2 - z_2)$. At this time, for the generators of $\mathfrak{m}$, the ideal obtained by applying the ring map $\z_i \mapsto t_i \z_i$ is

$$(t_1\z_1 - z_1, t_2\z_2 - z_2) = (\z_1 - z_1/t_1, \z_2 - z_2/t_2)$$

which corresponds to the point $(z_1/t_1, z_2/t_2)$. When taking $\Spec$, by contravariance this is interpreted as $(z_1/t_1, z_2/t_2)$ being carried to $(z_1,z_2)$, and therefore computing this on points, the torus action is given by

$$(t_1, t_2) \cdot (z_1, z_2) = (t_1 z_1, t_2 z_2)$$

This is the most natural action, in which each component of $(\mathbb{C}^\ast)^2$ scales the corresponding coordinate of $\mathbb{C}^2$.

Let us now examine the orbit structure of this action. The open dense orbit given by [Proposition 11](#prop11){: data-lid="0x8w0" } is

$$(\mathbb{C}^\ast)^2 = \{(z_1, z_2) \mid z_1 \neq 0, z_2 \neq 0\}$$

which is the $2$-dimensional torus $T_N$ itself. Also of interest are the lower-dimensional orbits, to which the coordinate axes $(\mathbb{C}^\ast) \times \{0\}$ and $\{0\} \times (\mathbb{C}^\ast)$ correspond. These are orbits where one coordinate becomes 0, so that the degree of freedom of the torus action is reduced by one. Similarly, the origin $(0, 0)$ is a fixed point of the torus action, which formally can be thought of as a $0$-dimensional orbit.
:::

Finally, we conclude this post by proving the basic properties of affine toric varieties.

::: Proposition 15
For an affine toric variety $U_\sigma$, the following hold:

1. $U_\sigma$ is a normal variety.
2. $U_\sigma$ is irreducible.
3. $U_\sigma$ is an $n$-dimensional variety.
:::

::: Proof
1. For normality, by Gordan's lemma, $S_\sigma = \sigma^\vee \cap M$ is a finitely generated semigroup. Furthermore, $S_\sigma$ is *saturated*: that is, whenever $k \cdot u \in S_\sigma$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ and $u \in M$, we have $u \in S_\sigma$. Indeed, if $ku \in S_\sigma$, then for all $v \in \sigma$ we have $\langle ku, v \rangle \ge 0$, and hence $\langle u, v \rangle \ge 0$, so $u$ also belongs to $S_\sigma$. Since an affine semigroup algebra is a normal domain if and only if the semigroup is saturated, $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is a normal domain and its spectrum $U_\sigma$ is normal.
2. For irreducibility, since $S_\sigma$ is a subsemigroup of the torsion-free abelian group $M$, there are no zero divisors in $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$. Therefore $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ is an integral domain and $U_\sigma = \Spec(\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma])$ is irreducible.
3. Since $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \subseteq \mathbb{C}[M]$, the fraction field is contained in $\mathbb{C}(M)$. On the other hand, since $\sigma$ is strongly convex, $\sigma^\vee$ is a full-dimensional cone in $M_{\mathbb{R}}$, and hence in the interior of $\sigma^\vee$ there exists a lattice point $u_0 \in M$. For any $m \in M$, if we choose $\ell$ sufficiently large, both $\ell u_0$ and $\ell u_0 + m$ belong to $S_\sigma$; hence from $m = (\ell u_0 + m) - \ell u_0$, $S_\sigma$ generates all of $M$ as a group, and the fraction field of $\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma]$ coincides exactly with $\mathbb{C}(M)$. Since $M \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$, the transcendence degree of $\mathbb{C}(M)$ is $n$, from which $\dim U_\sigma = n$ holds.
:::
