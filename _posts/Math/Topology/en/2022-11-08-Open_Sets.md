## Open Sets and Topology

In algebraic structures, concepts such as distance, which we commonly think of when we say *space*, may not be well-defined. On the other hand, the spaces that mathematicians are interested in can differ from these commonly thought-of spaces; for example, there exist many spaces to which it is impossible to give a distance. In this post, we examine in what way a space is defined.

::: Definition 1
A *topology* defined on a set $X$ refers to a subset $\mathcal{T}$ of $\mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying the following conditions.

1. $\emptyset,X\in\mathcal{T}$ holds.
2. An arbitrary union of elements of $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
3. A *finite* intersection of elements of $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.

A set $X$ given a topology in this way is called a *topological space*; in this context, the elements of $X$ are called *points*, and the elements of $\mathcal{T}$ are called *open sets*.
:::

If we adopt the convention from [\[Set Theory\] §Union and Intersection](/en/math/set_theory/union_and_intersection){: data-lid="7494p" }, one can show from the second and third conditions of the above definition that the two sets

$$\bigcup_{U\in\emptyset}U=\emptyset,\qquad\bigcap_{U\in\emptyset}U=X$$

must belong to $\mathcal{T}$, but it is customary to also specify the first condition to avoid confusion.

::: Example 2
For any set $X$, the smallest possible topology defined on $X$ is $\{\emptyset,X\}$. Indeed, the structure defined by this set satisfies conditions 1 through 3 of the definition, so it becomes a topological space. This is called the *trivial topology*. 

Conversely, the largest possible topology defined on $X$ is $\mathcal{P}(X)$. This also forms a topological space, and we call it the *discrete topology*.
:::

In general, when there is no room for confusion about the topology given on $X$, we simply use expressions such as the topological space $X$.

::: Definition 3
Suppose two topologies $\mathcal{T}_1$, $\mathcal{T}_2$ are given on a set and that they are comparable by $\subseteq$. If for $\mathcal{T}_1$, $\mathcal{T}_2$, $\mathcal{T}_1\subseteq\mathcal{T}_2$ holds, then $\mathcal{T}_2$ is called a *stronger topology* than $\mathcal{T}_1$, and $\mathcal{T}_1$ is called a *weaker topology* than $\mathcal{T}_2$. 
:::

For example, on a set $X$, *any* topology $\mathcal{T}$ defined on it is always weaker than the discrete topology on $X$ and always stronger than the trivial topology on $X$. 

To give a notion of nearness to $X$, the best tool is *distance*, but since spaces to which a distance can be given are very few, a more generalized concept is needed. If we examine a kind of nearness defined by $\mathcal{T}$ through how many open sets two points are commonly contained in, we can make statements similar to when a distance is given.

First, let us examine the case of the trivial topology. If $X=\{x,y\}$ and $X$ is given the trivial topology, then in $X$, the two points $x,y$ are completely equivalent from the viewpoint of a topological space. This is because one cannot distinguish the two points $x,y$ using the topological structure[^1]. In this way, if in a topological space $X$ two points $x,y$ are contained in exactly the same open sets, we say that these two points are *topologically indistinguishable*.

On the other hand, if the same set $X$ is given the discrete topology, the two points $x$ and $y$ become distinguishable in these topological spaces. This is because, containing the point $x$ but not containing $y$, an open set $\{x\}$ exists. In this respect, one can think of all points as being stuck close together in the trivial topology, but all points as being separated from one another in the discrete topology. 

Since an open set containing the point $x$ in the above argument will have to be used frequently in the future as well, it is good to give it a name as follows.

::: Definition 4
Let a topological space $X$ be given, and let an arbitrary subset of $X$, $A$, be given. Any open set containing $A$ is called an *open neighborhood* of $A$. For an arbitrary subset of $X$, $S$, to be a *neighborhood* of the set $A$ means that there exists an open neighborhood of $A$, $U$, such that $U\subseteq S$.
:::

In particular, when $A$ is a singleton $\{x\}$, the open neighborhoods and neighborhoods of $A$ are also called the open neighborhoods and neighborhoods of $x$, respectively. 

In many cases, for topological properties it suffices to examine only open neighborhoods, and in cases such as **[Mun]**, open neighborhoods are even simply called neighborhoods. However, distinguishing the two is essential in order to properly state several results in topology. In any case, since there is nothing particularly difficult about this general setting, we choose to distinguish them clearly.

::: Proposition 5
In a topological space $X$, a subset $U$ is open if and only if $U$ is a neighborhood of every $x\in U$.
:::
::: Proof
By [Definition 4](#def4){: data-lid="j25lo" }, unpacking the latter condition is equivalent to:

> whenever an arbitrary $x\in U$ is given, there exists, contained in $U$, an open neighborhood of $x$, $V$.

First, if $U$ is open, $U$ itself is contained in $U$ as an open neighborhood of $x$, so there is nothing to prove. It therefore suffices to show only the converse. For each $x\in U$, suppose that, contained in $U$, there exists an open neighborhood of $x$, and to indicate the dependence on $x$, let us denote it by $V(x)$. That is, $V(x)$ is an open set satisfying $\{x\}\subseteq V(x)\subseteq U$. Now, that $U$ is open is clear from the following equality:

$$U=\bigcup_{x\in U}\{x\}\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in U} V(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in U} U=U$$
:::

## Neighborhood filter

Let a topological space $X$ and a point $x\in X$ be given, and define $\mathcal{N}(x)$ to be the collection of all neighborhoods of $x$.

::: Proposition 6
A topological space $X$ and $\mathcal{N}(x)$ satisfy the following properties.

1. Any set containing an element of $\mathcal{N}(x)$ that is a subset of $X$ belongs to $\mathcal{N}(x)$.
2. The *finite* intersection of any elements of $\mathcal{N}(x)$ again belongs to $\mathcal{N}(x)$.
3. Every element of $\mathcal{N}(x)$ contains $x$.
4. Whenever an element of $\mathcal{N}(x)$, $V$, is given, there exists a suitable $W\in\mathcal{N}(x)$ such that for any $y\in W$, we have $V\in\mathcal{N}(y)$.

Conversely, for an arbitrary set $X$, suppose that for each element $x\in X$, a collection $\mathcal{N}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying the above conditions is given.[^2] Then, so that these $\mathcal{N}(x)$ are the collections of all neighborhoods of $x$, there exists on $X$ a unique topology $\mathcal{T}$.
:::
::: Proof
The first and third properties are trivial by definition.

To show the second property, suppose $V_1,\ldots, V_n\in\mathcal{N}(x)$ are given. Then, such that $x\in U_i\subseteq V_i$, there exist open neighborhoods of $x$, $U_1,\ldots, U_n$. Now by the third condition of [Definition 1](#def1){: data-lid="lufpt" }, their finite intersection $U_1\cap\cdots\cap U_n$ is also an open set, and therefore

$$x\in \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^n V_i$$

so $\bigcap V_i$ is a neighborhood of $x$. 

For the fourth property, it suffices to take $W$ contained in $V$ as an open neighborhood of $x$. 

Now we must show the converse. Suppose collections $\mathcal{N}(x)$ satisfying the above conditions are given. Then by [Proposition 5](#prop5){: data-lid="dlck1" }, the elements of $\mathcal{T}$ must satisfy the following condition:

> For any $x\in U$, $U\in\mathcal{N}(x)$

From this, it is clear that $\mathcal{T}$ must be unique. 

To show existence, let $\mathcal{T}$ be the collection of all sets satisfying the above condition, and let us show that this collection satisfies the conditions of [Definition 1](#def1){: data-lid="pauex" }. First, it is clear that $\emptyset$ and $X$ satisfy the above condition. Suppose elements of $\mathcal{T}$, $(U_i)_{i\in I}$, are given. Let $U=\bigcup U_i$, and choose an arbitrary $x\in U$. Then, since $x\in U_i$ for some $U_i$, we have $U_i\in\mathcal{N}(x)$ and $U_i\subseteq U$, so by the first condition $U\in\mathcal{N}(x)$, and therefore $U\in\mathcal{T}$. Similarly, one can show that the intersection of finitely many elements of $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.

To complete the proof of existence, we must show that for the topological space $(X,\mathcal{T})$ thus constructed, $\mathcal{N}(x)$ actually coincides with the collection of all neighborhoods of each point $x$. By the definition of $\mathcal{T}$, any open neighborhood of $x$, $U$, belongs to $\mathcal{N}(x)$, and hence any neighborhood of $x$ belongs to $\mathcal{N}(x)$ by the first condition.  
Conversely, choose an arbitrary set belonging to $\mathcal{N}(x)$, $V$. To show that $V$ is a neighborhood in $\mathcal{T}$ of $x$, we must find a suitable open neighborhood of $x$, $U\subseteq V$. Define $U$ as the collection of all elements satisfying $V\in\mathcal{N}(y)$ for $y\in X$. 
- Since $V\in\mathcal{N}(x)$, it is clear that $x\in U$.
- Pick an arbitrary $y\in U$. By definition of $U$, we have $V\in\mathcal{N}(y)$, and from the third condition for $\mathcal{N}(y)$, we have $y\in V$. That is, $x\in U\subseteq V$. 
- Finally, we must show that $U\in\mathcal{T}$. That is, let us show that for any $y\in U$, we have $U\in\mathcal{N}(y)$. Then since $V\in\mathcal{N}(y)$, from the fourth condition there exists a suitable $W\in\mathcal{N}(y)$ such that for any $z\in W$, $V\in\mathcal{N}(z)$ holds. Thus since $z\in U$, we have $W\subseteq U$, and from this we see that $U\in\mathcal{N}(y)$. 
:::

The first and second properties of the above proposition show that $\mathcal{N}(x)$ is a filter defined on the ordered set $(\mathcal{P}(X),\subseteq)$, and the third property shows that this filter does not contain the empty set $\emptyset$. ([\[Set Theory\] §Filters, Ideals, and Galois Connections, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/set_theory/filter_and_ideal#def1){: data-lid="g4ab4" }) A subset of $\mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying these three conditions is called a *filter defined on the set $X$*. In particular, the filters $\mathcal{N}(x)$ are called *neighborhood filters*.
