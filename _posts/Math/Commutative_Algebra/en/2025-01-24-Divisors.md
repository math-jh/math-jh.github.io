In this post, we take a closer look at $\CaDiv(A)$ and $\Pic(A)$, which we defined in [§Fractional Ideals, ⁋Definition 5](/en/math/commutative_algebra/fractional_ideals#def5).

## Dedekind Domains

First, let us make the following definition.

::: Definition 1
An ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of a ring $A$ is said to be *pure codimension $1$* if every associated prime ideal of $\mathfrak{a}$ has codimension $1$.
:::

In particular, when $\mathfrak{a}=A$ there are no associated prime ideals, so in this case $A$ vacuously has codimension $1$. Then the following holds.

::: Theorem 2
Let $A$ be a Noetherian integral domain, and suppose that the localization $A_\mathfrak{m}$ at every maximal ideal is a UFD.

1. An ideal $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A$ is invertible if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ is pure codimension $1$.
2. Every invertible fractional ideal $\mathfrak{A}\subseteq K$ can be expressed uniquely as a finite product of codimension $1$ prime ideals; consequently, $\CaDiv(A)$ is the free abelian group generated by the codimension $1$ prime ideals.
:::
::: Proof
1. First, suppose $\mathfrak{a}$ is invertible. Then for any maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, the localization $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{m}$ (viewing $A_\mathfrak{m}$ as a subring of $\Frac(A)$) is a principal ideal generated by a non-zerodivisor of $K$. Meanwhile, for any maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}\subseteq A$, $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a normal domain by [§Integral Extensions, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/commutative_algebra/integral_extension#prop9), so applying [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Theorem 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#thm8), for an associated prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ of the principal ideal $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{m}$,

    $$(\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m})(A_\mathfrak{m})_{\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}}\cong \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$$

    is a principal ideal in $(A_\mathfrak{m})_{\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}}\cong A_\mathfrak{p}$, and hence

    $$\codim \mathfrak{p}=\dim A_\mathfrak{p}=\codim \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}\leq 1$$

    and since $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain, we conclude from $(0)\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ that $\codim \mathfrak{p}=1$.

    Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak{a}$ is pure codimension $1$, and let us show that it is invertible.  
    First, under the hypotheses of the claim, every codimension $1$ prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ is invertible. Indeed, if $\mathfrak{p}\not\subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, then $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}=A_\mathfrak{m}$, and if $\mathfrak{p}\subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, then by the computation above $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a codimension $1$ prime ideal of $A_\mathfrak{m}$, and since $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a UFD by assumption, it is a principal ideal. To see this explicitly, take a nonzero $x\in \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ and factor it into a product of irreducible elements; since $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is prime, one of these factors $\pi$ lies in $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$, and since the ideal generated by an irreducible element in a UFD is prime, $(0)\subsetneq (\pi)\subseteq \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a chain of prime ideals, so $\codim \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}=1$ forces $(\pi)=\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$. Now $\pi$ is a nonzero element of a domain, hence a non-zerodivisor, and therefore $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible. Since products of invertible modules are again invertible, it now suffices to show that $\mathfrak{a}$ is a product of codimension $1$ prime ideals.  
    Suppose, contrary to the conclusion, that there exists an ideal that is pure codimension $1$ but cannot be expressed as a product of codimension $1$ prime ideals, and let $\mathfrak{A}$ be maximal among such ideals. If $\mathfrak{p}$ is a codimension $1$ prime ideal containing $\mathfrak{A}$, then $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{p}=A$, and hence $A\subsetneq\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$. Now, if $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{A}$, then by [§Integral Extensions, ⁋Lemma 5](/en/math/commutative_algebra/integral_extension#lem5), the elements of $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ are integral over $A$. But by assumption each $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a UFD, hence a normal domain by [§Integral Extensions, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/commutative_algebra/integral_extension#prop9), and since an intersection of normal domains with a common quotient field is again a normal domain, $A=\bigcap_\mathfrak{m}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is also normal. (Here, if $x\in K$ belongs to every $A_\mathfrak{m}$, then the ideal $\{a\in A\mid ax\in A\}$ is contained in no maximal ideal, so $x\in A$.) Therefore $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{A}$ is impossible, and we must have $\mathfrak{A}\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}$. Meanwhile, from $\mathfrak{A}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ we have $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{p}=A$, so $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}$ is again an ideal of $A$, and from $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1}=A$ we have $\mathfrak{p}\otimes_A A/\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}\cong \mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{A}$. At this point, by the third result of [§Associated Primes of Ideals, ⁋Theorem 7](/en/math/commutative_algebra/associated_primes#thm7), associated primes are detected by localization, and since $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible, tensoring with $\mathfrak{p}$ does not change the associated primes. Therefore, applying [§Associated Primes of Ideals, ⁋Lemma 5](/en/math/commutative_algebra/associated_primes#lem5) to $\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{A}\subseteq A/\mathfrak{A}$, we obtain $\Ass A/\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}=\Ass \mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{A}\subseteq \Ass A/\mathfrak{A}$, and since all elements of the right-hand side have codimension $1$, $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}$ is also pure codimension $1$. Now, by the maximality of $\mathfrak{A}$, $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{A}$ can be expressed as a product of codimension $1$ prime ideals, and multiplying both sides by $\mathfrak{p}$ yields a contradiction, giving the desired result. Therefore, every ideal that is pure codimension $1$ is a product of codimension $1$ prime ideals, and since each codimension $1$ prime ideal is invertible, these are all invertible.

2. Now let us prove the uniqueness of the expression. Given two expressions, by multiplying through by the inverse of one side it suffices to show that if $\prod_i \mathfrak{p}_i^{n_i}=A$ for distinct codimension $1$ prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{p}_r$ and integers $n_1,\ldots, n_r$, then all $n_i$ are $0$. Set $\mathfrak{P}=\prod_{n_i>0}\mathfrak{p}_i^{n_i}$ and $\mathfrak{Q}=\prod_{n_i<0}\mathfrak{p}_i^{-n_i}$; these are both ideals of $A$, and by assumption $\mathfrak{P}=\mathfrak{Q}$. If there exists an $i$ with $n_i>0$, then $\mathfrak{Q}=\mathfrak{P}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}_i$; if there is no $j$ with $n_j<0$, then $\mathfrak{Q}=A$, a contradiction, and otherwise, since $\mathfrak{p}_i$ is prime, $\mathfrak{p}_j\subseteq \mathfrak{p}_i$ for some $j$, and since $A$ is a domain, from $(0)\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_j\subseteq \mathfrak{p}_i$ and $\codim \mathfrak{p}_i=1$ we get $\mathfrak{p}_j=\mathfrak{p}_i$, contradicting the fact that they are distinct. Therefore all $n_i$ are at most $0$, and applying the same argument to $\mathfrak{Q}$ shows that all $n_i$ are $0$. Now, for an arbitrary invertible fractional ideal $\mathfrak{A}$, applying [§Fractional Ideals, ⁋Corollary 6](/en/math/commutative_algebra/fractional_ideals#cor6), we have $\mathfrak{A}=(a \mathfrak{A})(a)^{-1}$ for a suitable non-zerodivisor $a$ of $A$, and since $a \mathfrak{A}$ and $(a)$ are both invertible ideals, they are products of codimension $1$ prime ideals by the result above. That is, $\mathfrak{A}$ is a product of integer powers of codimension $1$ prime ideals, and from this we conclude that $\CaDiv(A)$ is the free abelian group generated by the codimension $1$ prime ideals.
:::

::: Definition 3
A Noetherian normal domain of dimension $1$ is called a *Dedekind domain*.
:::

Then the following holds.

::: Corollary 4
Every nonzero ideal of a Dedekind domain $A$ is invertible and can be expressed uniquely as a product of prime ideals, and the same holds for the fractional ideals of $A$. Consequently, $\CaDiv(A)$ is the free abelian group generated by the maximal ideals of $A$.
:::

By [§Fractional Ideals, ⁋Corollary 6](/en/math/commutative_algebra/fractional_ideals#cor6), we know that $\Pic(A)\cong \CaDiv(A)/K^\times$. Dedekind domains are used mostly in algebraic number theory, and following the terminology of that field, when $A$ is a Dedekind domain we call $\Pic(A)$ the *class group*.

## Discrete Valuation Rings

DVRs arise naturally as localizations of Dedekind domains and play a central role in understanding divisors.

::: Definition 5
A subring $A$ of a field $K$ is called a *valuation ring* if, for every $x \in K^\times$, either $x \in A$ or $x^{-1} \in A$.

A valuation ring $A$ is called a *discrete valuation ring* (DVR) if $A$ is Noetherian and the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$ is a nonzero principal ideal.
:::

In [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#def6), we first defined a valuation $\nu:\Frac(A)^\times \rightarrow G$ and then defined the valuation ring as the set of elements whose value under $\nu$ is nonnegative; a ring obtained in this way satisfies the condition above since $\nu(x^{-1})=-\nu(x)$. Conversely, for an $A$ satisfying the condition above, defining $\bar{x}\geq \bar{y}$ on $G=K^\times/A^\times$ by $x/y\in A$ makes $G$ a totally ordered abelian group and the quotient map a valuation, and the valuation ring of this valuation is again $A$. Also, when $G=\mathbb{Z}$, that is, for the valuation ring of a discrete valuation, the ring is Noetherian with nonzero principal maximal ideal, as we saw after [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#def6), so it is a DVR in the above sense; conversely, for a DVR in the above sense, the map defined by $f\mapsto n$ in the expression $f=\pi^n u$ of [Proposition 8](#prop8) is a surjective valuation to $\mathbb{Z}$, so the two definitions agree.

::: Proposition 6
For a valuation ring $A$, the following hold.

1. $A$ is a local ring.
2. $A$ is an integrally closed domain.
3. The ideals of $A$ are totally ordered.
:::
::: Proof
1. If we show that the set $\mathfrak{m}$ of non-unit elements of $A$ is an ideal, then every proper ideal of $A$ contains no unit and hence is contained in $\mathfrak{m}$, so $\mathfrak{m}$ is the unique maximal ideal. First, let $x, y \in \mathfrak{m}$. If one of them is $0$, it is clear that $x+y \in \mathfrak{m}$, so we may assume that $x, y$ are both nonzero; then by the definition of a valuation ring, either $x/y \in A$ or $y/x \in A$. By symmetry, suppose $x/y \in A$; then $x + y = y(1 + x/y) \in (y)$, and if $x+y$ were a unit, then $y$ would also be a unit, contradicting $y \in \mathfrak{m}$. Therefore $x + y \in \mathfrak{m}$. Also, for any $a \in A$, if $ax$ were a unit, then $a(ax)^{-1}$ would be an inverse of $x$, again a contradiction, so $ax \in \mathfrak{m}$. Thus $\mathfrak{m}$ is an ideal and $A$ is a local ring.

2. Let $x$ be an element of $K = \Frac(A)$ that is integral over $A$. Then there exists a monic polynomial $f(t) \in A[t]$ such that $f(x) = 0$. If $x \notin A$, then $x^{-1} \in A$. Writing $f(t) = t^n + a_{n-1}t^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$, we have $x^n = -a_{n-1}x^{n-1} - \cdots - a_0$, and multiplying both sides by $x^{-(n-1)}$ gives $x = -a_{n-1} - \cdots - a_0 x^{-(n-1)} \in A$, a contradiction. Therefore $x \in A$, and $A$ is integrally closed.

3. Suppose two proper ideals $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$ are given. If $\mathfrak{a} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{b}$, then there exists $a \in \mathfrak{a} \setminus \mathfrak{b}$, and we show that $\mathfrak{b} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}$. For any $b \in \mathfrak{b}$, if $b = 0$ then clearly $b \in \mathfrak{a}$, so suppose $b \neq 0$. Also, since $0 \in \mathfrak{b}$, we have $a \neq 0$, and hence $a/b \in K^\times$; by the definition of a valuation ring, either $a/b \in A$ or $b/a \in A$. If $a/b \in A$, then $a = (a/b) \cdot b \in \mathfrak{b}$, contradicting $a \notin \mathfrak{b}$. Therefore $b/a \in A$, and $b = (b/a) \cdot a \in \mathfrak{a}$.
:::

::: Definition 7
An element $\pi$ generating the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = (\pi)$ of a DVR $A$ is called a *uniformizer* or a *uniformizing parameter*.
:::

::: Proposition 8
For the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of a DVR $A$ and $K = \Frac(A)$, the following hold.

1. Every generator $\pi$ of $\mathfrak{m}$ is a uniformizer, and two uniformizers $\pi, \pi'$ satisfy the relation $\pi' = u\pi$ (with $u \in A^\times$). Hence the uniformizer is unique up to multiplication by a unit.
2. For any $f \in K^\times$, there exist a unique integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and a unit $u \in A^\times$ such that $f = \pi^n u$.
:::
::: Proof
1. If $\mathfrak{m} = (\pi) = (\pi')$, then there exist $a, b \in A$ with $\pi' = a\pi$ and $\pi = b\pi'$. From this, $\pi = ab\pi$, and since $\pi \neq 0$, we have $ab = 1$, and therefore $a, b \in A^\times$.

2. First consider the case $f \in A \setminus \{0\}$. Since $A$ is a Noetherian local ring and $\mathfrak{m} = (\pi)$, by [§Blowup Algebras, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/blowup_algebra#cor8) we have $\bigcap_{n \geq 0} \mathfrak{m}^n = 0$. Therefore, for $f \neq 0$, there exists a unique nonnegative integer $n$ such that $f \in \mathfrak{m}^n \setminus \mathfrak{m}^{n+1}$. Now since $\mathfrak{m}^n = (\pi^n)$, we can write $f = \pi^n u$. Here $u \notin \mathfrak{m}$, so $u \in A^\times$.

In the case $f \in K^\times \setminus A$, we have $f^{-1} \in \mathfrak{m}$ by the definition of a valuation ring. Applying the argument above to $f^{-1}$, we get $f^{-1} = \pi^m v$ (with $m \geq 1$, $v \in A^\times$), and hence $f = \pi^{-m} v^{-1}$. Here $v^{-1} \in A^\times$, so we obtain the desired expression.

Let us show the uniqueness of the expression. Suppose $\pi^n u = \pi^{n'} u'$ with $n \geq n'$. Then $\pi^{n-n'} u = u'$. If $n > n'$, the left-hand side belongs to $\mathfrak{m}$ while the right-hand side is a unit, a contradiction. Therefore $n = n'$ and $u = u'$.
:::

There is a close relationship between DVRs and Dedekind domains.

::: Proposition 9
For a Noetherian integral domain $A$, the following are equivalent.

1. $A$ is a Dedekind domain.
2. For every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$, the localization $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a DVR.
:::
::: Proof
(1) ⇒ (2): If $A$ is a Dedekind domain, then $A$ is a Noetherian normal domain of dimension 1. For any maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a 1-dimensional Noetherian local domain and is normal. By [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Theorem 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#thm8), $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a regular local ring, and hence $\mathfrak{m}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a principal ideal. That is, $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a DVR.

(2) ⇒ (1): If $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a DVR, then it is 1-dimensional, so $\dim A = 1$. Also, since DVRs are integrally closed, $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is integrally closed for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, and therefore $A$ is also integrally closed. That the localizations are Noetherian does not by itself imply that the original ring is Noetherian, so we must keep the additional assumption that $A$ is Noetherian. Indeed, if $A$ is Noetherian and every localization $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is a DVR, then $A$ is a Dedekind domain.
:::

## Divisors

[Corollary 4](#cor4) shows that, just as every element of a UFD can be expressed uniquely as a product of irreducible elements, in a Dedekind domain every ideal can be expressed uniquely as a product of codimension $1$ prime ideals, and fractional ideals can be expressed by additionally allowing negative powers. Generalizing this further, we make the following definition for an arbitrary ring $A$.

::: Definition 10
A *Weil divisor* of a ring $A$ is an element of the free abelian group $\Div(A)$ generated by the codimension $1$ prime ideals of $A$.
:::

It is customary to write the operation of $\Div(A)$ as $+$. Thus an element of $\Div(A)$ is a formal linear combination of codimension $1$ prime ideals.

::: Theorem 11
Fix a Noetherian ring $A$. Then there exists a group homomorphism $\Phi: \CaDiv(A) \rightarrow \Div(A)$ sending every invertible ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ to

$$\mathfrak{a}\mapsto \sum_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}\subseteq A$ a codimension $1$ prime} \length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}) \mathfrak{p}$$
:::
::: Proof
From [§Fractional Ideals, ⁋Corollary 6](/en/math/commutative_algebra/fractional_ideals#cor6) and the universal property of free abelian groups, it suffices to show that the above formula is well-defined and preserves products of ideals.

First, let us show that the above formula is well-defined. That is, we must show that for any invertible ideal $\mathfrak{a}$, the given expression is a finite sum. For an arbitrary codimension $1$ prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, consider the 1-dimensional local ring $A_\mathfrak{p}$. Then by [§Fractional Ideals, ⁋Theorem 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/fractional_ideals#thm3), $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a non-zerodivisor of $A$, and therefore $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ also contains a non-zerodivisor $a$ of $A_\mathfrak{p}$. Meanwhile, by [§Associated Primes of Ideals, ⁋Theorem 7](/en/math/commutative_algebra/associated_primes#thm7), the minimal prime ideals of $A_\mathfrak{p}$ consist only of zerodivisors, so $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$, which contains the non-zerodivisor $a$, is not contained in any minimal prime ideal. But since $\dim A_\mathfrak{p}=1$, a prime ideal of $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is either minimal or the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ itself, and hence the only prime ideal containing $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ is $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$, so $\dim A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}=0$. From this, by [§Krull Dimension, ⁋Corollary 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/Krull_dimension#cor3) and [§The Jordan-Hölder Theorem, ⁋Theorem 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/Jordan-Holder_theorem#thm3), we know that $\length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})<\infty$.

Now, if $\mathfrak{a}\not\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, then $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ contains a unit of $A_\mathfrak{p}$, so $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}=A_\mathfrak{p}$ and hence $\length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})=0$. Also, if $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, then $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ must be one of the minimal prime ideals containing $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$, and therefore only finitely many such $\mathfrak{p}$ exist once $\mathfrak{a}$ is given. From the discussion above, we see that the given expression is well-defined.

Now we must show that $\Phi$ preserves products. That is, if $\mathfrak{a}=\prod \mathfrak{a}_i$, we must show that the equation

$$\length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})=\sum \length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}_iA_\mathfrak{p})$$

holds. For convenience of notation, let us write $A$ for the local ring obtained by localization, $\mathfrak{p}$ for its maximal ideal, and let the $\mathfrak{a}_i$ be ideals of $A$. Then since the $\mathfrak{a}_i$ are invertible ideals, they are generated by single non-zerodivisors $a_i$, and we have the filtration

$$A\supseteq \mathfrak{a}_1=(a_1)\supseteq \mathfrak{a}_1 \mathfrak{a}_2=(a_1a_2)\supseteq\cdots\supseteq \prod_{i\in I} \mathfrak{a}_i=\left(\prod_{i\in I} a_i\right)$$

Now, to prove the given equation, it suffices to show that $(\prod_{j< i}a_j)/(\prod_{j\leq i} a_j)\cong A/(a_i)$. Since each $a_i$ is a non-zerodivisor, multiplication by $\prod_{j< i}a_j$ induces an isomorphism from $A$ to $(\prod_{j< i}a_j)$, and considering the kernel of the map

$$(\prod_{j< i}a_j) \rightarrow A \rightarrow A/(a_i)$$

this kernel is exactly $(a_i\prod_{j< i}a_j)=(\prod_{j\leq i}a_j)$, so the claim is immediate.
:::

In particular, if $A$ is $1$-dimensional, then by the computation in the second result of [§The Jordan-Hölder Theorem, ⁋Theorem 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/Jordan-Holder_theorem#thm3),

$$\length A/\mathfrak{a}=\sum_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ a codimension $1$ prime of $A$} \length (A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})$$

holds, and therefore, defining $\Div(A) \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}$ by $\sum n_\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}\mapsto \sum n_\mathfrak{p}$, composition with the map in [Theorem 11](#thm11) yields a map from $\CaDiv(A)$ to $\mathbb{Z}$ satisfying $\mathfrak{a}\mapsto \length(A/\mathfrak{a})$.

Meanwhile, by endowing $\CaDiv(A)$ with an appropriate equivalence relation, we were able to write $\Pic(A)$ as the set of equivalence classes of elements of $\CaDiv(A)$. In a similar spirit, we define the following.

::: Definition 12
Let a Noetherian ring $A$ and its total ring of fractions $K$ be given. Then for any $a\in K^\times$, the image of the invertible ideal $(a)$ under $\Phi:\CaDiv(A) \rightarrow \Div(A)$ is called a *principal divisor*.  
For the group $\Prin(A)\subseteq \Div(A)$ of principal divisors of $A$, the quotient group $\Div(A)/\Prin(A)$ is called the codimension $1$ *Chow group* of $A$ and is denoted $\Chow(A)$.
:::

Then by definition, $\Phi:\CaDiv(A) \rightarrow \Div(A)$ induces a map $\Psi: \Pic(A) \rightarrow \Chow(A)$.

## Dimension and Normalization

Above, we defined the group homomorphism $\Phi:\CaDiv(A) \rightarrow \Div(A)$ and the group homomorphism $\Psi:\Pic(A) \rightarrow \Chow(A)$ obtained from it.

::: Definition 13
For a reduced ring $A$, the *normalization* of $A$ is the integral closure of $A$ in its total ring of fractions $K$. If the normalization of $A$ equals $A$ itself, we call $A$ a *normal ring*.
:::

This is a restatement of the definition given in [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Definition 9](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#def9). Also, if $A$ is an integral domain, then $K=\Frac(A)$, so in this case the notions of normalization and normal ring coincide with those defined in [§Integral Extensions, ⁋Definition 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/integral_extension#def3). Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 14
For a normal Noetherian ring $A$, both $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ defined above are injective.
:::
::: Proof
Considering the diagram

{% diagram Math/Commutative_Algebra/Divisors-1.svg width="26.29em" alt="relations_between_divisors" %}

by [\[Homological Algebra\] §Diagram chasing, ⁋Proposition 1](/en/math/homological_algebra/diagram_chasing#prop1), it suffices to show that $\Phi$ is injective. That is, it suffices to prove that if two invertible ideals of $A$ are sent to the same element of $\Div(A)$ via $\Phi$, then they are equal.

So suppose two invertible ideals $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$ satisfy $\Phi(\mathfrak{a})=\Phi(\mathfrak{b})$, and let us show $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{b}$. By symmetry, it suffices to show $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \mathfrak{b}$, and by the result of [§Associated Primes of Ideals, ⁋Corollary 4](/en/math/commutative_algebra/associated_primes#cor4), it in turn suffices to show that $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}\subseteq \mathfrak{b}A_\mathfrak{p}$ holds for every associated prime $\mathfrak{p}$. But now $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ is an associated prime of $\mathfrak{b}A_\mathfrak{p}$, and since $\mathfrak{b}A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a principal ideal generated by a non-zerodivisor, by the equivalence in [§Regular Local Rings, ⁋Theorem 10](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#thm10), $\codim \mathfrak{p}=1$ and $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR. Now, from $\Phi(\mathfrak{a})=\Phi(\mathfrak{b})$ we have $\length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})=\length(A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{b}A_\mathfrak{p})$, and from this we conclude that $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}A_\mathfrak{p}$.
:::
