## The Cayley–Hamilton Theorem

We begin with the following generalized form of the Cayley–Hamilton theorem.

::: Theorem 1
Fix a ring $A$ and an ideal $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A$, and let $M$ be an $A$-module generated by $n$ elements $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. Then for any $u\in\End_\rMod{A}(M)$ such that $u(M)\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$, there exists a monic polynomial

$$p(\x)=\x^n+p_1\x^{n-1}+\cdots+p_n,\qquad p_k\in \mathfrak{a}^k$$

satisfying $p(u)=0$.
:::
::: Proof
From the assumption $u(M)\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$ and the equality $\mathfrak{a}M=\sum_j \mathfrak{a}e_j$, we can choose, for each $i$, elements $a_{ij}\in \mathfrak{a}$ such that $u(e_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}e_j$. Now let $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_n$ be the standard basis of $A^n$, and consider the surjection $\pi: A^n \rightarrow M$ given by $\epsilon_i\mapsto e_i$ together with the endomorphism $\tilde u\in \End_\rMod{A}(A^n)$ given by $\tilde u(\epsilon_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}\epsilon_j$; then $\pi\circ \tilde u=u\circ \pi$.

Since $A^n$ is a free module, we may apply [\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Determinants, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/determinants#prop9) to $\tilde u$; hence the characteristic polynomial $p=\chi_{\tilde u}$ of $\tilde u$ is a monic polynomial of degree $n$ satisfying $p(\tilde u)=0$. From the relation above we then have $p(u)\circ \pi=\pi\circ p(\tilde u)=0$, and since $\pi$ is surjective, we obtain $p(u)=0$.

It remains to verify the condition on the coefficients of $p$. This follows from the fact that the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are given by

$$p(\x)=\sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k\tr\left({\bigwedge}^k(\tilde u)\right)\x^{n-k}$$

Since the entries of the matrix representation of $\tilde u$ are the $a_{ij}$'s, the diagonal entries of the matrix representation of ${\bigwedge}^k(\tilde u)$ are the $k\times k$ minors of this matrix, each of which is a sum of products of $k$ elements of $\mathfrak{a}$. In other words, $p_k=(-1)^k\tr({\bigwedge}^k(\tilde u))\in \mathfrak{a}^k$.
:::

The following then holds.

::: Proposition 2
Let $A$ be a ring, let $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A[\x]$ be an ideal, set $B=A[\x]/\mathfrak{a}$, and write $b$ for $\x+\mathfrak{a}\in B$. Then the following holds.

1. $B$ being generated by at most $n$ elements as an $A$-module is equivalent to $\mathfrak{a}$ containing a monic polynomial of degree at most $n$. In this case, $B$ is generated by $1,b,\cdots,b^{n-1}$.
2. $B$ being a free $A$-module of rank $n$ is equivalent to $\mathfrak{a}$ being generated by a monic polynomial of degree $n$. In this case, $1,b,\cdots,b^{n-1}$ form a basis of $B$.
:::
::: Proof
1. One direction is trivial. Conversely, suppose $B$ is generated by $n$ elements as an $A$-module. Consider the $A$-module endomorphism $b:B \rightarrow B$ given by multiplication by $b$. Applying [Theorem 1](#thm1) with the ideal $A$, this endomorphism satisfies a monic polynomial $p(x)$ of degree $n$, and substituting the element $b$ itself must also yield $0$. By the definition of $b$, this means $p(\x)\in \mathfrak{a}$.
2. First, suppose $\mathfrak{a}$ is generated by a monic polynomial of degree $n$. Then by the result just proved, $B$ is generated by $1,b,\ldots, b^{n-1}$, so it suffices to show that these elements are linearly independent. If $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i b^i=0$ in the $A$-module $B$, then $q(\x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_i\x^i$ must belong to $\mathfrak{a}$, and for degree reasons we must have $q=0$.
Conversely, if $B$ is a free $A$-module of rank $n$, then $B$ can be generated by $n$ elements, so by the result just proved $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a monic polynomial $p$ of degree $n$, and it follows that $1,b,\ldots, b^{n-1}$ form a basis of $B$. It remains to show that $p$ generates $\mathfrak{a}$. Given any $f\in \mathfrak{a}$, consider the remainder $r$ upon division by $p$. Since both $f$ and $p$ belong to $\mathfrak{a}$, this remainder must also map to $0$ in $B$. But this is the same as substituting $\x=b$ into the polynomial $r(\x)$, and viewing it as a linear combination of the basis $1,\ldots, b^{n-1}$ of $B$, we conclude that all the coefficients of $r$ are $0$.
:::

## Integral Extensions

Earlier we saw that an $A$-algebra $E$ consists of exactly the same data as a ring homomorphism $\phi:A \rightarrow E$. More precisely, a ring homomorphism $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ endows the $E$-module $E$ with an $A$-module structure $\phi^\ast E$ via restriction of scalars, and this $A$-module structure is compatible (by the commutativity of multiplication in $E$) with the multiplicative structure of $E$, so that we may also regard $E$ as an $A$-algebra.

Now, since a composition of ring homomorphisms is again a ring homomorphism, for an $A$-algebra $E$ the ring homomorphism defined by

$$A\overset{\phi}{\rightarrow}E\hookrightarrow E[\x]$$

gives $E[\x]$ an $A$-algebra structure. Therefore, by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Algebras, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/algebraic_structures/algebras#prop8), a unique $A$-algebra homomorphism $\phi[\x]:A[\x]\rightarrow E[\x]$ is defined; concretely, it is the map that takes an arbitrary polynomial

$$p(\x)=a_n\x^n+\cdots+a_0\in A[\x]$$

to the polynomial given by

$$(\phi[\x](p))(\x)=\phi(a_n)\x^n+\cdots+\phi(a_0)\in E[\x]$$

::: Definition 3
Let $\phi:A\rightarrow E$ be a ring homomorphism.

1. If $\phi$ is injective, we call $E$ an *extension* of $A$.
2. An element $x$ of $E$ is said to be *integral* over $\phi$ if there exists a monic polynomial $p\in A[\x]$ such that $(\phi[\x](p))(x)=0$. If every element of $E$ is integral over $\phi$, we call $\phi$ an *integral homomorphism*. If an extension $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism, we call $E$ an *integral extension* of $A$.
3. The collection of all elements of $E$ that are integral over $\phi$ is called the *integral closure of $A$ in $E$*, or the *normalization of $A$ in $E$*. If $A$ is an integral domain, the integral closure of $A$ in $\Frac(A)$ is called, with no further qualification, the *normalization of $A$*. An integral domain $A$ is a *normal domain* if its normalization is $A$ itself.
4. If the $A$-module $\phi^\ast E$ is finitely generated, we call $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ a *finite homomorphism*.
5. If $E$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra, we call $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ a *homomorphism of finite type*.
:::

When the structure morphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ is clear from context, we also say that $x$ is integral over $A$ instead of saying that $x$ is integral over $\phi$. Similarly, in this situation we say that $E$ is integral over $A$ instead of saying that $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism.

Any finite homomorphism is of finite type by definition. On the other hand, given a finite homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ and any $x\in E$, the map $x\times-: E \rightarrow E$ is an $A$-module endomorphism, so applying [Theorem 1](#thm1) shows that $x$ is integral over $\phi$. That is, finite homomorphisms are also integral. The following lemma proves the converse.

::: Lemma 4
A ring homomorphism $\phi$ is finite if and only if $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism of finite type.
:::
::: Proof
One direction was shown above. Conversely, suppose $E$ is generated by finitely many integral elements; we must show that $E$ is also finitely generated as an $A$-module. We induct on the number of generators of $E$ as an $A$-algebra. Suppose $E$ is generated as an $A$-algebra by $n$ integral elements $x_1,\ldots, x_n$, and consider the $A$-subalgebra $E'$ of $E$ generated by the $n-1$ elements $x_1,\ldots, x_{n-1}$; by the inductive hypothesis, $E'$ is finitely generated as an $A$-module. Let $\{s_i\}$ be generators of $E'$ as an $A$-module. Since $x_n$ is integral over $A$, it is also integral over $E'$, and therefore $E$ is finitely generated as an $E'$-module. Denoting these generators by $\{t_j\}$, we see that $\{s_i t_j\}$ generates $E$ as an $A$-module.
:::

Now, it is natural to expect a relationship between $E$ being integral over $A$ and the integrality of the individual elements of $E$. To this end, we first prove the following lemma.

::: Lemma 5
Let $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ be a ring homomorphism and let $x$ be an element of $E$. Then $x$ is integral over $A$ if and only if there exist an $E$-module $N$ and an $A$-submodule $M$ of $N$, finitely generated as an $A$-module, such that $M$ is not annihilated by any nonzero element of $E$ and the inclusion $xM\subseteq M$ holds.
:::
::: Proof
First, suppose $x$ is integral over $A$. Then taking $N=E$, we know by [Proposition 2](#prop2) that $M=A[x]$ is finitely generated. For the converse, as in the proof of [Proposition 2](#prop2), regard multiplication by $x$ as an endomorphism of $M$ and apply [Theorem 1](#thm1).
:::

The following theorem states exactly what one would expect, but proving it directly from the definition without [Lemma 5](#lem5) is nearly impossible.

::: Theorem 6
For an $A$-algebra $E$, the integral closure of $A$ in $E$ is again an $A$-algebra.
:::
::: Proof
Suppose two elements $x,y\in E$ are integral over $A$. We must show that $x+y$ and $xy$ are integral over $A$. Let $M=A[x]$ and $M'=A[y]$, two submodules of $E$, and consider the subalgebra $MM'$ of $E$ generated by products of an element of $M$ and an element of $M'$; since each of $M,M'$ is finitely generated, so is $MM'$. Now, since

$$(xy)MM'=(xM)(yM')\subseteq MM',\qquad (x+y)MM'\subseteq xMM'+M(yM')\subseteq MM'$$

the desired result follows from [Lemma 5](#lem5).
:::

## Nakayama's Lemma

We now prove a very useful lemma. First, recall that the *nilradical* $\mathfrak{N}(A)=\sqrt{(0)}$ of [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Definition 14](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#def14) is given by

$$\mathfrak{N}(A)=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ prime} \mathfrak{p}$$

([§Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8)) In a similar fashion, we define the *Jacobson radical* of $A$ by

$$J(A)=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{m}$ maximal} \mathfrak{m}$$

To prove Nakayama's lemma, we must first prove the following lemma.

::: Lemma 7
Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module and let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an ideal of $A$ satisfying $\mathfrak{a}M=M$. Then there exists some $a\in \mathfrak{a}$ such that $(1-a)M=0$.
:::
::: Proof
Since the given condition implies $M\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$, by [Theorem 1](#thm1) there exists a monic polynomial

$$p(\x)=\x^n+p_1\x^{n-1}+\cdots+p_n,\qquad p_k\in \mathfrak{a}^k$$

such that $p(\id_M)=0$. That is,

$$(1+p_1+\cdots+p_n)M=0$$

and setting $a=-(p_1+\cdots+p_n)$ gives the desired result.
:::

We can finally state Nakayama's lemma.

::: Lemma 8 (Nakayama)
Let $\mathfrak{a}$ be an ideal of $A$ contained in the Jacobson radical $J(A)$, and let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module. Then the following holds.

1. If $\mathfrak{a}M=M$, then $M=0$.
2. If the images of $x_1,\ldots, x_n$ in $M/\mathfrak{a}M$ generate $M/\mathfrak{a}M$ as an $A$-module, then $x_1,\ldots, x_n$ generate $M$ as an $A$-module.
:::
::: Proof
For the first statement, the element $a\in \mathfrak{a}$ furnished by [Lemma 7](#lem7) lies in every maximal ideal by assumption. In other words, $1-a$ cannot belong to any maximal ideal, so $1-a$ is a unit, and the desired result follows.

For the second statement, let $N=M/\sum_i Ax_i$. Then one can show that $N/\mathfrak{a}N=0$, and the first statement gives $N=0$.
:::

## Localization

We now examine several results related to localization.

::: Proposition 9
A unique factorization domain is a normal domain.
:::
::: Proof
Let $a/b\in \Frac(A)$ with $a,b$ coprime, and suppose $a/b$ lies in the normalization of $A$. Then there exists a monic polynomial such that

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n+a_{n-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)+a_0=0$$

holds. It follows that

$$\x^n+a_{n-1}b \x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1b^{n-1}\x+a_0b^n\in A[\x]$$

is a monic polynomial that vanishes upon substituting $\x=a$. That is, $a^n$ is divisible by $b$, and since $a$ and $b$ are coprime, this forces $b$ to be a unit. Hence $a/b\in A$, which shows that $A$ is a normal domain.
:::

More generally, the following holds.

::: Proposition 10
Let $A\subseteq B$ be rings and let $p\in A[\x]$ be a monic polynomial. If there exist monic polynomials $q_1,q_2\in B[\x]$ such that $p=q_1q_2$ in $B[\x]$, then the coefficients of $q_1,q_2$ are integral over $A$.
:::
::: Proof
By successively adjoining roots of equations, we can find a ring $C$ containing $B$ such that in $C[\x]$, both $q_1$ and $q_2$ split into the form $\prod (\x-\alpha_i)$, $\prod(\x-\beta_j)$. The $\alpha_i,\beta_j$'s are all roots of the monic polynomial $p=q_1q_2$ in $A[\x]$, so they are integral over $A$, and therefore the subring $C'$ of $C$ they generate is an integral $A$-algebra. On the other hand, expanding $q_1=\prod(\x-\alpha_i)$ and $q_2=\prod(\x-\beta_j)$, we see that their coefficients are obtained from the $\alpha_i,\beta_j$'s by ring operations, so they belong to $C'$.
:::

Consequently, the following holds.

::: Corollary 11
Over a normal domain $A$, every monic irreducible polynomial is prime.
:::

Normalization also commutes with localization, and the proof is trivial.

::: Proposition 12
Let $A\subseteq B$ be rings and let $S$ be a multiplicative subset of $A$. If $A'$ is the integral closure of $A$ in $B$, then $S^{-1}A'$ is the integral closure of $S^{-1}A$ in $S^{-1}B$.
:::

Another result related to localization is a slight strengthening of [§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop4). Recall first that a ring $A$ is called a *semilocal ring* if it has only finitely many maximal ideals. Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 13
Let $A$ be a semilocal ring and let $M,N$ be finitely presented $A$-modules. If $M_\mathfrak{m}\cong N_\mathfrak{m}$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, then $M\cong N$.
:::
::: Proof
Write $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{m}_n$ for the maximal ideals of $A$, and suppose that for each $k$ an isomorphism $u_k: M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}\rightarrow N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ is given. Since $A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ is a flat $A$-module and $M$ is finitely presented by assumption, we have an isomorphism

$$\Hom_{A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}}( M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}, N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}) \rightarrow \Hom_A(M,N)_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$$

([§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 5](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop5)) The image of $u_k$ under this isomorphism is an element of $\Hom_A(M,N)$ with a denominator in $A\setminus \mathfrak{m}_k$, so, multiplying $u_k$ by this denominator if necessary, we may treat the result $v_k$ as an element of $\Hom_A(M,N)$.

Meanwhile, since $\mathfrak{m}_k$ is prime, if $\bigcap_{l\neq k} \mathfrak{m}_l\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$ then we would have $\mathfrak{m}_l\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$ for some $l$, a contradiction. That is,

$$\bigcap_{l\neq k} \mathfrak{m}_l\not\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$$

so there exists an element $a_k$ with $a_k\in \bigcap_{l\neq k}\mathfrak{m}_l$ but $a_k\not\in \mathfrak{m}_k$. For the elements $a_k$ so constructed, set $v=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kv_k$; we claim this is the desired isomorphism. By [§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop4), it suffices to check this after localizing at each maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_k$.

More generally, we show that for any local ring $(B, \mathfrak{n})$ and any maps $s,t:K \rightarrow L$ between finitely generated $B$-modules, if $s$ is an isomorphism and $t(K)\subseteq \mathfrak{n}L$, then $s+t$ is also an isomorphism. Applying this to the local ring $(A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}, \mathfrak{m}_kA_{\mathfrak{m}_k})$ and the maps $s=a_k v_k$ and $t=\sum_{l\neq k} a_lv_l$ from $M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ to $N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ will then complete the proof.

Let us prove the claim. Since $t$ induces the zero map from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$ while $s$ induces an epimorphism from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$, the map $s+t$ also induces an epimorphism from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$. By [Lemma 8](#lem8), the morphism $s+t: K \rightarrow L$ is therefore an epimorphism as well. Now take the inverse $s^{-1}$ of the isomorphism $s$ and consider the surjective endomorphism $w=s^{-1}(s+t): K \rightarrow K$. Regard $K$ as a $B[\x]$-module on which $\x$ acts as $w$; since $K$ is finitely generated as a $B$-module, it is also finitely generated as a $B[\x]$-module. Since $w$ is surjective, for the ideal $\mathfrak{a}=(\x)\subseteq B[\x]$ we have $\mathfrak{a}K=w(K)=K$, so by [Lemma 7](#lem7) there exists some $q\in B[\x]$ such that $(1-\x q(\x))K=0$, i.e. $q(w)\circ w=\id_K$. Hence $w$ is also a monomorphism, thus an isomorphism, and therefore $s+t$ is a monomorphism as well, which gives the desired result.
:::

Now let us examine the properties of the ring homomorphisms in [Definition 3](#def3) a bit further, beginning with the following proposition.

::: Proposition 14
Let $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ and $\rho: A \rightarrow A'$ be ring homomorphisms, and set $E'=A'\otimes_AE$. Then the following holds for $\rho_!\phi: A' \rightarrow E'$.

1. If $\phi$ is integral, then $\rho_!\phi$ is also integral.
2. If $\phi$ is finite, then $\rho_!\phi$ is also finite.
:::
::: Proof
The proofs of the two claims are similar, so we only prove the first. Assume $\phi$ is integral, and let $x_i$ be integral elements generating $E$. Then each $x_i$ satisfies some monic polynomial $p_i\in A[\x]$. The elements $1\otimes x_i$ therefore generate $E'$, and each satisfies $(\rho[\x])(p_i)$. It follows that $E'$ is integral over $A'$.
:::

The following also holds.

::: Proposition 15
Let $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ be a ring homomorphism. If $(a_1, \ldots, a_n)=A$, then the following holds.

1. If each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is integral, then so is $A \rightarrow E$.
2. If each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is finite, then so is $A \rightarrow E$.
:::
::: Proof
As above, the proofs of the two claims are similar, so we only prove the first. For any $x\in E$, let $\mathfrak{A}$ be the ideal of $A[\x]$ consisting of the polynomials $p$ satisfying $p(x)=0$. One can then verify that the subset $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$ consisting of the leading coefficients of these polynomials is an ideal of $A$.

Now, by the assumption that each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is integral, the image of $x$ in $E_{a_i}$ is integral over $A_{a_i}$; writing down an integral equation for this element and clearing the powers of $a_i$ in the denominators, we see that for each $i$ there exists some $n_i$ such that $a_i^{n_i}\in \mathfrak{a}$. On the other hand, raising both sides of

$$1=\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i a_i$$

to a sufficiently high power shows that these $a_i^{n_i}$'s also generate the unit ideal, and therefore $1\in \mathfrak{a}$. By definition, this means $x$ is integral.
:::

::: Proposition 16
Let $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ be a ring homomorphism and let $x\in E$. Then $x$ is integral over $\phi$ if and only if, for every prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$, the image of $x$ in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is integral over $\phi_\mathfrak{p}: A_\mathfrak{p} \rightarrow E_\mathfrak{p}$.
:::
::: Proof
One direction follows from [Proposition 14](#prop14). So suppose that for every prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}\subseteq A$, the image of $x$ in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is integral over $A_\mathfrak{p}$; we show that $x$ is integral. For this, it suffices to take the $A$-subalgebra $E'$ of $E$ generated by $\phi(A)$ and $x$ and show that $A \rightarrow E'$ is integral.

By assumption, in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ there exist $a_i\in A_\mathfrak{p}$ such that

$$x^d+\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_{d-1})x^{d-1}+\cdots+\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_0)=0$$

Taking a common denominator of the $\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_k)$'s, we may regard all of the $a_k$'s as belonging to $A_f$ for some $f\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$. The left-hand side of the above equation can then be regarded as an element of $E_f$, and this element maps to $0$ in $E_\mathfrak{p}$. But $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is the localization of $E_f$ at the image of $A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$, so there exists some $h\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$ such that multiplying this left-hand side by $h$ gives $0$ in $E_f$; replacing $f$ by $fh$, the above equation becomes one that holds in $E_f$. Then the image of $x$ in $E_f$ is integral over $A_f$, and since $E_f'$ is generated by this element as an $A_f$-algebra, [Lemma 4](#lem4) tells us that $A_f \rightarrow E_f'$ is a finite homomorphism.

In this way we obtain an element $f_\mathfrak{p}\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$ for each prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$; the ideal of $A$ generated by these elements is contained in no prime ideal, so it equals $A$ itself. We can therefore choose finitely many prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{p}_m$ such that $(f_{\mathfrak{p}_1},\ldots, f_{\mathfrak{p}_m})=A$, and since each $A_{f_{\mathfrak{p}_i}} \rightarrow E_{f_{\mathfrak{p}_i}}'$ is finite, it is integral. By [Proposition 15](#prop15), $A \rightarrow E'$ is then integral, and in particular $x$ is integral over $\phi$.
:::
