## The Cayley–Hamilton Theorem

We begin by first stating the generalized Cayley–Hamilton theorem.

::: Theorem 1
Fix a ring $A$ and an ideal $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A$, and let $M$ be generated by $n$ elements $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ as an $A$-module. Then for any $u\in\End_\rMod{A}(M)$, if $u(M)\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$ holds, there exists a monic polynomial

$$p(\x)=\x^n+p_1\x^{n-1}+\cdots+p_n,\qquad p_k\in \mathfrak{a}^k$$

satisfying $p(u)=0$.
:::
::: Proof
From the assumption $u(M)\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$ and $\mathfrak{a}M=\sum_j \mathfrak{a}e_j$, for each $i$, we can obtain $u(e_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}e_j$ by choosing $a_{ij}\in \mathfrak{a}$. Now let the standard basis of $A^n$ be $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_n$, and consider the surjection given by $\epsilon_i\mapsto e_i$, namely $\pi: A^n \rightarrow M$, and the endomorphism given by $\tilde{u}(\epsilon_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}\epsilon_j$, namely $\tilde{u}\in \End_\rMod{A}(A^n)$; then $\pi\circ \tilde{u}=u\circ \pi$ holds.

Meanwhile, since $A^n$ is a free module, we may apply [\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Determinants, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/determinants#prop9){: data-lid="g9pl4" } to $\tilde{u}$, and therefore, the characteristic polynomial of $\tilde{u}$, $p=\chi_{\tilde{u}}$, satisfies $p(\tilde{u})=0$ and is a monic polynomial of degree $n$. Then from the commutation relation above, we have $p(u)\circ \pi=\pi\circ p(\tilde{u})=0$, and since $\pi$ is surjective, we obtain $p(u)=0$.

What remains is the condition on the coefficients of $p$, which follows from the fact that the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are given by

$$p(\x)=\sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k\tr\left({\bigwedge}^k(\tilde{u})\right)\x^{n-k}$$

Since the entries of the matrix representation of $\tilde{u}$ are all $a_{ij}$, the diagonal entries of the matrix representation of ${\bigwedge}^k(\tilde{u})$ are the $k\times k$ minors of this matrix, each of which is a sum of products of elements of $\mathfrak{a}$ consisting of $k$ factors. That is, $p_k=(-1)^k\tr({\bigwedge}^k(\tilde{u}))\in \mathfrak{a}^k$.
:::

Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 2
For a ring $A$, suppose that an ideal $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A[\x]$ is given, let $B=A[\x]/\mathfrak{a}$, and write $b$ for $\x+\mathfrak{a}\in B$. Then the following hold.

1. $B$ is generated as an $A$-module by at most $n$ elements if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a monic polynomial of degree at most $n$. In this case, $B$ is generated by $1,b,\cdots,b^{n-1}$. 
2. $B$ is a free $A$-module of rank $n$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a}$ is generated by a monic polynomial of degree $n$. In this case, $1,b,\cdots,b^{n-1}$ forms a basis of $B$.
:::
::: Proof
1. One direction is trivial. Conversely, suppose that $B$ is generated as an $A$-module by $n$ elements. Now consider multiplying elements of $B$ by $b$ to obtain the $A$-module endomorphism $b:B \rightarrow B$. Applying [Theorem 1](#thm1){: data-lid="15pu9" } to the ideal $A$, we know that this endomorphism satisfies a degree-$n$ monic polynomial $p(x)$, and substituting $b$ as an element must also yield $0$. Therefore, by the definition of $b$, we know that $p(\x)\in \mathfrak{a}$.
2. First, suppose that $\mathfrak{a}$ is generated by a monic polynomial of degree $n$. Then by the previous result, we know that $B$ is generated by $1,b,\ldots, b^{n-1}$. It now suffices to show that these are linearly independent. In the $A$-module $B$, if $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i b^i=0$, then $q(\x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_i\x^i$ must belong to $\mathfrak{a}$, and because of its degree, $q=0$.  
Conversely, if $B$ is a rank-$n$ free $A$-module, then $B$ can be generated by $n$ elements, and again from the previous result, $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a degree-$n$ monic polynomial $p$, from which we can also deduce that $1,b,\ldots, b^{n-1}$ forms a basis of $B$. It remains to show that $p$ generates $\mathfrak{a}$; for this, suppose that an arbitrary $f\in \mathfrak{a}$ is given, and upon dividing by $p$, consider the remainder $r$. Since both polynomials $f$ and $p$ belong to $\mathfrak{a}$, sending this remainder to $B$ must also yield $0$. However, this is equal to evaluating the polynomial $r(\x)$ at $\x=b$, and viewing this as a linear combination of the basis of $B$, $1,\ldots, b^{n-1}$, we know that all coefficients of $r$ must be $0$. 
:::

## Integral Extensions

Earlier, we saw that an $A$-algebra $E$ consists of the same data as a ring homomorphism $\phi:A \rightarrow E$. More precisely, the ring homomorphism $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ endows the $E$-module $E$ via restriction of scalars with an $A$-module structure $\phi^\ast E$, and this $A$-module structure preserves (by the commutativity of multiplication in $E$) the multiplication structure of $E$, so that it can also be viewed as an $A$-algebra. 

Meanwhile, since any composition of ring homomorphisms is a ring homomorphism, we know that for an $A$-algebra $E$, the ring homomorphism defined by

$$A\overset{\phi}{\rightarrow}E\hookrightarrow E[\x]$$

gives $E[\x]$ an $A$-algebra structure. Therefore, by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Algebras, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/algebraic_structures/algebras#prop8){: data-lid="77068" }, a unique $A$-algebra homomorphism $\phi[\x]:A[\x]\rightarrow E[\x]$ is defined; concretely, this is the map that takes an arbitrary polynomial

$$p(\x)=a_n\x^n+\cdots+a_0\in A[\x]$$

and yields the polynomial

$$(\phi[\x](p))(\x)=\phi(a_n)\x^n+\cdots+\phi(a_0)\in E[\x]$$

given by this expression.

::: Definition 3
Let $\phi:A\rightarrow E$ be a ring homomorphism. 

1. If $\phi$ is injective, we call $E$ an *extension* of $A$.
2. In $E$, an element $x$ is said to be *integral* over $\phi$ if there exists a monic polynomial $p\in A[\x]$ such that $(\phi[\x](p))(x)=0$. If every element of $E$ is integral over $\phi$, we call $\phi$ an *integral homomorphism*. If an extension $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism, we call $E$ an *integral extension* of $A$. 
3. Among the elements of $E$, the collection of all elements integral over $\phi$ is called the *integral closure in $E$ of $A$*, or the *normalization in $E$ of $A$*. If $A$ is an integral domain, the integral closure in $\Frac(A)$ of $A$ is called, with no further qualification, the *normalization of $A$*. An integral domain $A$ is a *normal domain* if the normalization of $A$ is itself. 
4. If the $A$-module $\phi^\ast E$ is a finitely generated $A$-module, we call $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ a *finite homomorphism*. 
5. If the $A$-algebra $E$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra, we call $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ a *finite type homomorphism*. 
:::

When the structure morphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ is clear from context, instead of saying that $x$ is integral over $\phi$, we also use the expression that $x$ is integral over $A$. Similarly, in such cases, instead of saying that $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism, we say that $E$ is integral over $A$. 

Any finite homomorphism is a finite type homomorphism by definition. On the other hand, for a finite homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ and any $x\in E$, since $x\times-: E \rightarrow E$ is an $A$-module endomorphism, applying [Theorem 1](#thm1){: data-lid="unsxg" } shows that $x$ is integral over $\phi$. That is, a finite homomorphism is also integral. The following lemma shows the converse. 

::: Lemma 4
A ring homomorphism $\phi$ is finite if and only if $\phi$ is an integral homomorphism of finite type.
:::
::: Proof
One direction was shown above. Conversely, suppose $E$ is generated by finitely many integral elements; we must show that $E$ is also finitely generated as an $A$-module. For this, let us use induction on the number of generators of $E$ (as an $A$-algebra). If $E$ is generated as an $A$-algebra by $n$ integral elements $x_1,\ldots, x_n$, we can consider, generated by $n-1$ of these elements $x_1,\ldots, x_{n-1}$, the subalgebra of $E$, namely the $A$-subalgebra $E'$, which by the inductive hypothesis is finitely generated as an $A$-module. Let the generators of $E'$ (as an $A$-module) be $\{s_i\}$. Then, since $x_n$ is integral over $A$, it is also integral over $E'$, and therefore as an $E'$-module, $E$ must be finitely generated. If we denote these elements by $\{t_j\}$, we see that $\{s_i t_j\}$ finitely generates $E$ as an $A$-module. 
:::

Meanwhile, it is natural to expect a relationship between $E$ being integral over $A$ and each element of $E$ being integral. To this end, we first prove the following lemma.

::: Lemma 5
Let a ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ and an element of $E$, $x$, be given. Then $x$ is integral over $A$ if and only if there exist an $E$-module $N$ and an $A$-submodule of $N$, a finitely generated $A$-module $M$, such that $M$ is not annihilated by any nonzero element of $E$ and the inclusion $xM\subseteq M$ holds. 
:::
::: Proof
First, suppose $x$ is integral over $A$. Then taking $N=E$, we know that $M=A[x]$ is finitely generated by [Proposition 2](#prop2){: data-lid="e77qo" }. For the converse, as in the proof of [Proposition 2](#prop2){: data-lid="kpb02" }, regard multiplication by $x$ as an endomorphism of $M$ and apply [Theorem 1](#thm1){: data-lid="cplj4" }.
:::

The following theorem states a property that is natural to expect, but proving it directly from the definition without [Lemma 5](#lem5){: data-lid="wu8lk" } is nearly impossible.

::: Theorem 6
For an $A$-algebra $E$, the integral closure in $E$ of $A$ is again an $A$-algebra.
:::
::: Proof
Suppose two elements $x,y\in E$ are integral over $A$. Then we must show that $x+y$ and $xy$ are integral over $A$. Now let $M=A[x]$ and $M'=A[y]$ be two submodules of $E$. If we take products of elements of $M$ and elements of $M'$ and consider the generated subalgebra of $E$, $MM'$, then since each of $M,M'$ is finitely generated, $MM'$ is also finitely generated. Now, since

$$(xy)MM'=(xM)(yM')\subseteq MM',\qquad (x+y)MM'\subseteq xMM'+M(yM')\subseteq MM'$$

by using [Lemma 5](#lem5){: data-lid="ty99a" }, we obtain the desired result.
:::

## Nakayama's Lemma

We now prove a very useful lemma. First, recall that the *nilradical* $\mathfrak{N}(A)=\sqrt{(0)}$ of [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Definition 14](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#def14){: data-lid="p5wt3" } is given by

$$\mathfrak{N}(A)=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ prime} \mathfrak{p}$$

([§Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8){: data-lid="zba36" }) In a similar fashion, we define the *Jacobson radical* of $A$ by

$$J(A)=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{m}$ maximal} \mathfrak{m}$$

To prove Nakayama's lemma, we must first prove the following lemma.

::: Lemma 7
Suppose a finitely generated $A$-module $M$ and an ideal of $A$, $\mathfrak{a}$, satisfy $\mathfrak{a}M=M$. Then there exists some $a\in \mathfrak{a}$ such that $(1-a)M=0$.
:::
::: Proof
From the given condition, $M\subseteq \mathfrak{a}M$, so by [Theorem 1](#thm1){: data-lid="m9bbz" } there exists a monic polynomial

$$p(\x)=\x^n+p_1\x^{n-1}+\cdots+p_n,\qquad p_k\in \mathfrak{a}^k$$

such that $p(\id_M)=0$. That is,

$$(1+p_1+\cdots+p_n)M=0$$

and setting $a=-(p_1+\cdots+p_n)$ gives the desired result.
:::

Now we can finally state Nakayama's lemma.

::: Lemma 8 (Nakayama)
Suppose an ideal of $A$ contained in the Jacobson radical $J(A)$, $\mathfrak{a}$, is given, and let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module. Then the following holds.

1. If $\mathfrak{a}M=M$, then $M=0$.
2. If the images of $x_1,\ldots, x_n$ in $M/\mathfrak{a}M$ generate $M/\mathfrak{a}M$ as an $A$-module, then $x_1,\ldots, x_n$ generate $M$ as an $A$-module.
:::
::: Proof
For the first statement, the element $a\in \mathfrak{a}$ obtained from [Lemma 7](#lem7){: data-lid="ovi08" } belongs to every maximal ideal by assumption. In other words, $1-a$ cannot belong to any maximal ideal, so $1-a$ is a unit. Therefore, we obtain the desired result.

For the second statement, let $N=M/\sum_i Ax_i$. Then one can show that $N/\mathfrak{a}N=0$, and from the first statement we know that $N=0$.
:::

## Localization

We now examine several results related to localization.

::: Proposition 9
A unique factorization domain is a normal domain.
:::
::: Proof
For any $a/b\in \Frac(A)$, suppose that $a,b$ are coprime and $a/b$ is contained in the normalization of $A$. Then there exists a monic polynomial such that

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n+a_{n-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)+a_0=0$$

holds. Now from this, we know that

$$\x^n+a_{n-1}b \x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1b^{n-1}\x+a_0b^n\in A[\x]$$

is a monic polynomial that, upon substituting $\x=a$, becomes $0$. That is, $a^n$ is divisible by $b$, and for this not to be a contradiction, $b$ must be a unit. That is, $A$ must be a normal domain, and this completes the proof.
:::

More generally, the following holds.

::: Proposition 10
Let rings $A\subseteq B$ be given and let a monic polynomial $p\in A[\x]$ be given. If in $B[\x]$ one can find, such that $p=q_1q_2$, monic polynomials $q_1,q_2\in B[\x]$, then the coefficients of $q_1,q_2$ are integral over $A$.
:::
::: Proof
By adjoining roots of the equations, with $B$ contained in a suitable ring $C$, we can ensure that in $C[\x]$, $q_1,q_2$ both factor into the form $\prod (\x-\alpha_i)$, $\prod(\x-\beta_j)$. Then the $\alpha_i,\beta_j$ are all roots of the monic polynomial in $A[\x]$, $p=q_1q_2$, so they are integral over $A$, and therefore the subring of $C$ generated by them, $C'$, is an integral $A$-algebra. On the other hand, expanding $q_1=\prod(\x-\alpha_i)$ and $q_2=\prod(\x-\beta_j)$, we see that their coefficients are obtained by ring operations on the $\alpha_i,\beta_j$, so they belong to $C'$.
:::

Therefore, the following holds.

::: Corollary 11
For a normal domain $A$, every monic irreducible polynomial is prime.
:::

Meanwhile, normalization commutes with localization, and its proof is also trivial.

::: Proposition 12
Fix rings $A\subseteq B$ and a multiplicative subset of $A$, $S$. Then, for the integral closure of $A$ in $B$, $A'$, $S^{-1}A'$ is the integral closure of $S^{-1}A$ in $S^{-1}B$.
:::

Another result related to localization is a slight strengthening of [§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop4){: data-lid="tmmtd" }. First, that a ring $A$ is a *semilocal ring* means that $A$ has only finitely many maximal ideals. Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 13
For a semilocal ring $A$ and finitely presented $A$-modules $M,N$, if $M_\mathfrak{m}\cong N_\mathfrak{m}$ holds for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, then $M\cong N$.
:::
::: Proof
First, let the maximal ideals of $A$ be $\mathfrak{m}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{m}_n$, and suppose that for each $k$ an isomorphism $u_k: M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}\rightarrow N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ is given. Then $A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ is a flat $A$-module, and since $M$ is finitely presented by assumption, there exists an isomorphism

$$\Hom_{A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}}( M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}, N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}) \rightarrow \Hom_A(M,N)_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$$

([§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 5](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop5){: data-lid="h9qsv" }). The image of $u_k$ under this isomorphism is an element of $\Hom_A(M,N)$ with an element of $A\setminus \mathfrak{m}_k$ placed in the denominator, so by multiplying $u_k$ by this denominator if necessary, we can treat $v_k$ as an element of $\Hom_A(M,N)$.

Meanwhile, since $\mathfrak{m}_k$ is prime, if $\bigcap_{l\neq k} \mathfrak{m}_l\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$, then for some $l$ we must have $\mathfrak{m}_l\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$, which is a contradiction. That is,

$$\bigcap_{l\neq k} \mathfrak{m}_l\not\subseteq \mathfrak{m}_k$$

holds, and from this, an element satisfying $a_k\in \bigcap_{l\neq k}\mathfrak{m}_l$ but $a_k\not\in \mathfrak{m}_k$, namely $a_k$, exists. For these $a_k$ so constructed, if we define $v=\sum_{k=1}^n a_kv_k$, this becomes our desired isomorphism, and for its proof, by [§Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop4){: data-lid="tn3u7" }, it suffices to examine the localization at each maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_k$.

More generally, we show that for any local ring $(B, \mathfrak{n})$ and finitely generated $B$-modules with maps $s,t:K \rightarrow L$ between them, if $s$ is an isomorphism and $t(K)\subseteq \mathfrak{n}L$, then $s+t$ is also an isomorphism. Applying this result to the local ring $(A_{\mathfrak{m}_k}, \mathfrak{m}_kA_{\mathfrak{m}_k})$, and to the maps from $M_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ to $N_{\mathfrak{m}_k}$ given by $s=a_k v_k$ and $t=\sum_{l\neq k} a_lv_l$, will then complete the proof.

Let us prove this claim. First, $t$ can be viewed as the zero map from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$, and $s$ can be viewed as an epimorphism from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$, so $s+t$ can also be viewed as an epimorphism from $K$ to $L/\mathfrak{n}L$. Then by [Lemma 8](#lem8){: data-lid="7sqmu" }, as a morphism from $K$ to $L$, $s+t$ is also an epimorphism. Now, for the isomorphism $s$, take its inverse $s^{-1}$ and consider the surjective endomorphism $w=s^{-1}(s+t): K \rightarrow K$. Then we can view $K$, with $\x$ acting as $w$, as a $B[\x]$-module; since $K$ is finitely generated as a $B$-module, it is also so as a $B[\x]$-module. Meanwhile, since $w$ is surjective, for the ideal $\mathfrak{a}=(\x)\subseteq B[\x]$ we have $\mathfrak{a}K=w(K)=K$, and therefore by [Lemma 7](#lem7){: data-lid="49j4o" } there exists some $q\in B[\x]$ such that $(1-\x q(\x))K=0$, that is, $q(w)\circ w=\id_K$. From this, $w$ is also a monomorphism and thus an isomorphism, and therefore $s+t$ is a monomorphism, which gives the desired result.
:::

Now let us examine the properties of the ring homomorphisms in [Definition 3](#def3){: data-lid="1zd0u" } a bit further. First, we begin with the following proposition.

::: Proposition 14
Let ring homomorphisms $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ and $\rho: A \rightarrow A'$ be given. Setting $E'=A'\otimes_AE$, the following holds for $\rho_!\phi: A' \rightarrow E'$.

1. If $\phi$ is integral, then $\rho_!\phi$ is also integral.
2. If $\phi$ is finite, then $\rho_!\phi$ is also finite.
:::
::: Proof
Since the proofs of both claims are similar, we only prove the first. Assume that $\phi$ is integral, and let the integral elements generating $E$ be $x_i$. Then each $x_i$ satisfies a suitable monic polynomial $p_i\in A[\x]$. Therefore, each $1\otimes x_i$ generates $E'$ and satisfies $(\rho[\x])(p_i)$. From this, we see that $E'$ is integral over $A'$.
:::

The following also holds.

::: Proposition 15
Let a ring homomorphism $\phi:A \rightarrow E$ be given. If $(a_1, \ldots, a_n)=A$, then the following holds.

1. If each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is integral, then so is $A \rightarrow E$.
2. If each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is finite, then so is $A \rightarrow E$.
:::
::: Proof
As above, since the proofs of the two claims are similar, we only prove the first claim. For any $x\in E$, let the ideal of polynomials in $A[\x]$ satisfying $p(x)=0$ be $\mathfrak{A}$. Then one can verify that the subset of $A$ consisting of the leading coefficients of these polynomials, $\mathfrak{a}$, is an ideal of $A$.

Meanwhile, from the assumption that each $A_{a_i} \rightarrow E_{a_i}$ is integral, the image of $x$ in $E_{a_i}$ is integral over $A_{a_i}$; thus, after writing down an integral equation for this element and multiplying by all the powers of $a_i$ in the denominators, we know that for each given $i$, there exists an appropriate $n_i$ such that $a_i^{n_i}\in \mathfrak{a}$ holds. On the other hand, raising both sides of the equation

$$1=\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i a_i$$

to a sufficiently high power, we know that these $a_i^{n_i}$ also generate the unit ideal, and therefore $1\in \mathfrak{a}$. From this, we know that $x$ is integral by definition.
:::

::: Proposition 16
For a ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow E$ and $x\in E$, $x$ is integral over $\phi$ if and only if, for every prime ideal of $A$, $\mathfrak{p}$, the image of $x$ in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is integral over $\phi_\mathfrak{p}: A_\mathfrak{p} \rightarrow E_\mathfrak{p}$.
:::
::: Proof
One direction follows from [Proposition 14](#prop14){: data-lid="or5m2" }. Therefore, for any prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}\subseteq A$, suppose that the image of $x$ in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is integral over $A_\mathfrak{p}$, and let us show that $x$ is integral. For this, after taking the subalgebra generated by $\phi(A)$ and $x$ in $E$, namely the $A$-subalgebra $E'$, it suffices to show that $A \rightarrow E'$ is integral.

First, from the assumption, in $E_\mathfrak{p}$ the equation

$$x^d+\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_{d-1})x^{d-1}+\cdots+\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_0)=0$$

holds for some $a_i\in A_\mathfrak{p}$. Now, finding a common denominator for the $\phi_\mathfrak{p}(a_k)$, for a suitable $f\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$ we can view each $a_k$ as belonging to $A_f$. Then the left-hand side of the above equation can be viewed as an element of $E_f$, and when sent to $E_\mathfrak{p}$, this element becomes $0$. However, since $E_\mathfrak{p}$ is the localization of $E_f$ with respect to the image of $A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$, there exists some $h\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$ such that multiplying this left-hand side by $h$ is, in $E_f$, equal to $0$, and therefore, replacing $f$ by $fh$, the above equation becomes an equation that holds in $E_f$. Then the image of $x$ in $E_f$ is integral over $A_f$, and since $E_f'$ is generated by this element as an $A_f$-algebra, by [Lemma 4](#lem4){: data-lid="gz8h9" } we know that $A_f \rightarrow E_f'$ is a finite homomorphism.

In this way, for each prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, we obtain an element $f_\mathfrak{p}\in A\setminus \mathfrak{p}$; the ideal of $A$ generated by these elements is contained in no prime ideal, so it is $A$ itself. We can therefore choose finitely many prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_1,\ldots, \mathfrak{p}_m$ such that $(f_{\mathfrak{p}_1},\ldots, f_{\mathfrak{p}_m})=A$, and since each $A_{f_{\mathfrak{p}_i}} \rightarrow E_{f_{\mathfrak{p}_i}}'$ is finite, it is integral. Then by [Proposition 15](#prop15){: data-lid="c0rt6" }, $A \rightarrow E'$ is integral, and in particular $x$ is integral over $\phi$.
:::
