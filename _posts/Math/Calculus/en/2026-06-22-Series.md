The properties of infinite sequences studied in [§Limits of Sequences](/en/math/calculus/sequences){: data-lid="sbrkc" } play a particularly important role in our setting when dealing with *infinite series*.

## Partial Sums and Infinite Series

::: Definition 1
For a sequence $(a_n)$, if the *partial sums* $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n$ form a sequence $(s_N)$ that converges to $S$, we say that the *infinite series* $\sum a_n$ *converges* to $S$ and write $\sum a_n=S$. If $(s_N)$ diverges, we say that the series *diverges*.
:::

Thus, the convergence or divergence of a series is simply the convergence problem for the sequence of partial sums $s_N$, so we can apply the tools of [§Limits of Sequences](/en/math/calculus/sequences){: data-lid="szg8m" } directly. The following are the partial sums of two especially common series.

::: Example 2
- The *geometric series* $\sum r^n$ is the infinite series defined by the partial sums
    
    $$s_N=\sum_{n=0}^N r^n=\frac{1-r^{N+1}}{1-r} \qquad (r \neq 1)$$
  
    where the identity above can be verified from $(1-r)s_N=(1-r)(1+r+\cdots+r^N)=1-r^{N+1}$. If $\lvert r\rvert<1$, then $r^{N+1} \rightarrow 0$, so we see that this infinite sum converges to $1/(1-r)$. If $\lvert r\rvert \geq 1$, we can prove that this sequence is either unbounded above or oscillates, and therefore diverges.
- The *$p$-series* $\sum_n 1/n^p$ converges if $p>1$ and diverges if $p \leq 1$. If $p \leq 0$, then for every $n$ the general term satisfies $1/n^p=n^{-p} \geq 1$, so $s_N \geq N$; in particular, the partial sums are not bounded above and therefore diverge. Now consider the case $p>0$. Excluding the first term of the partial sum and grouping the terms into blocks of $2^k$ up to term $2^{m+1}-1$, we have
    
    $$s_{2^{m+1}-1}=1+\left(\frac{1}{2^p}+\frac{1}{3^p}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{4^p}+\cdots+\frac{1}{7^p}\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac{1}{(2^m)^p}+\cdots+\frac{1}{(2^{m+1}-1)^p}\right)$$
    
    Here, the $k$-th parenthesized group contains $2^k$ terms, and every term in it is less than or equal to $1/(2^k)^p$, so
    
    $$s_{2^{m+1}-1} \leq \sum_{k=0}^m 2^k \cdot \frac{1}{2^{kp}}=\sum_{k=0}^m (2^{1-p})^k$$
    
    If $p>1$, then $2^{1-p}<1$, so the right-hand side is a partial sum of a geometric series and is hence bounded; applying [§Limits of Sequences, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/calculus/sequences#prop7){: data-lid="sfc1c" } shows that the $p$-series converges. On the other hand, if $0<p \leq 1$, then each of the $2^k$ terms in a group is at least as large as the smallest term $1/(2^{k+1}-1)^p$, so the sum of each group is at least

    $$\frac{2^k}{(2^{k+1}-1)^p} \geq \frac{2^k}{2^{k+1}-1} \geq \frac{1}{2}$$

    Therefore, from
    
    $$s_{2^{m+1}-1} \geq 1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2} \geq 1+\frac{m}{2}$$
    
    we can confirm that the partial sums diverge to infinity.
:::

In this way, the fact that determining the convergence of a series is essentially the same as determining the convergence of a sequence implies that the properties of limits of sequences carry over directly to series. The most basic of these is linearity: the termwise sum of two convergent series and a scalar multiple of a convergent series also yield convergent series, whose values equal those obtained by applying the same operations to the original limits. This simply transfers the linearity of [§Limits of Sequences, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/calculus/sequences#prop2){: data-lid="sdfx4" } at the level of partial sums.

::: Proposition 3 (Linearity of series)
If $\sum a_n=A$ and $\sum b_n=B$ converge and $\alpha, \beta$ are constants, then $\sum (\alpha a_n+\beta b_n)$ also converges, and its sum is $\alpha A+\beta B$.
:::
::: Proof
If we set the partial sums to be $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n$ and $t_N=\sum_{n=1}^N b_n$, respectively, then since these are finite sums, we have

$$\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n=1}^N (\alpha a_n+\beta b_n) &= \alpha \sum_{n=1}^N a_n+\beta \sum_{n=1}^N b_n \\
&= \alpha s_N+\beta t_N
\end{aligned}$$

Since $s_N \rightarrow A$ and $t_N \rightarrow B$, the linearity of convergent sequences implies that the right-hand side converges to $\alpha A+\beta B$. Hence the partial sums on the left-hand side converge to the same value, yielding the conclusion.
:::

## Convergence Tests

In practice, the value itself to which a series converges is often difficult to compute; for instance, in the case of the $p$-series examined in [Example 2](#ex2){: data-lid="p010p" } alone, even the fact that the value at $p=3$,

$$\zeta(3)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^3}$$

is irrational is nontrivial. ([Wikipedia, Apéry's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ap%C3%A9ry%27s_constant)) Generally, when dealing with series, we often only need to know whether a series converges rather than the value itself to which it converges, and various convergence tests are useful for this purpose.

The most basic yet powerful tool for testing the convergence of a series is the following proposition.

::: Proposition 4 (General term test)
If the series $\sum a_n$ converges, then $a_n \rightarrow 0$. Consequently, if $a_n \not\rightarrow 0$, then the series diverges.
:::
::: Proof
If $s_N \rightarrow S$, then $s_{N-1} \rightarrow S$ as well, so $a_N=s_N-s_{N-1} \rightarrow S-S=0$.
:::

::: Example 5 (Harmonic series)
The *harmonic series*

$$\sum \frac{1}{n}=1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\cdots$$

is an example of a series that diverges even though its general term tends to $0$. This is the special case $p=1$ of [Example 2](#ex2){: data-lid="bpkzx" }; as in that proof, if we group the terms into blocks of $2^k$ and examine

$$1+\frac{1}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{4}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{5}+\cdots+\frac{1}{8}\right)+\cdots$$

then since the sum of each parenthesized group is at least $1/2$, the partial sums grow without bound.
:::

As the harmonic series illustrates, $a_n \rightarrow 0$ is only a necessary condition for convergence, not a sufficient one. Meanwhile, for a series with positive terms, the partial sums are monotonically increasing, so applying [§Limits of Sequences, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/calculus/sequences#prop7){: data-lid="1qxye" } reduces the question of convergence to checking whether the sum is bounded above. Using this, we obtain the following result.

::: Theorem 6
For series with positive terms, the following hold.

1. If for sufficiently large $n$ we have $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ also converges.
2. (Ratio test) When $a_{n+1}/a_n \rightarrow L$, the series converges if $L<1$ and diverges if $L>1$.
:::
::: Proof
1. Denote the sequence of partial sums of $\sum a_n$ by $s_N$ and that of $\sum b_n$ by $t_N$. Then by hypothesis, $t_N$ converges to a constant $B$, and there exists some $N_0$ such that whenever $n>N_0$, we have $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$. First, since $b_n \geq 0$, we have $t_N \leq B$, and therefore, whenever $N>N_0$, for every such $N$ we have

    $$s_N=s_{N_0}+\sum_{n=N_0+1}^N a_n \leq s_{N_0}+\sum_{n=N_0+1}^N b_n=s_{N_0}+t_N-t_{N_0} \leq s_{N_0}+B-t_{N_0}$$

    Here, the right-hand side of the inequality does not depend on $N$ and is a *constant* depending only on $N_0$. That is, for $N>N_0$, the sequence of partial sums $s_N$ is bounded above, and since $a_n\geq 0$ by hypothesis, $s_N$ is an increasing sequence; therefore, it converges by [§Limits of Sequences, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/calculus/sequences#prop7){: data-lid="vuooi" }.

2. If $L<1$, choose a number satisfying $L<r<1$; for this $r$, using the assumption $a_{n+1}/a_n\rightarrow L$ and its $\epsilon$-$N$ definition, we can ensure that whenever $n>N$, we have $a_{n+1} \leq r a_n$, for a suitable choice of $N$. Hence $a_{N+k} \leq r^k a_N$, so to test convergence we can compare the series with a convergent geometric series ([Example 2](#ex2){: data-lid="7w241" }). On the other hand, if $L>1$, the terms increase and do not tend to $0$, so the series diverges by [Proposition 4](#prop4){: data-lid="wh6zw" }.
:::

Observe that the ratio test is inconclusive when $L=1$. For instance, all $p$-series have $L=1$, yet their convergence depends on $p$; for this reason, when testing a series for convergence, it is important to choose an appropriate test.

Because the comparison test can be applied to any sequence as long as we can find a suitable inequality, it is among the strongest tests we will consider in this post; however, it comes with the burden of directly bounding the terms against those of another series. For actual series, it is often clear that the terms are *of the same order of magnitude* as those of some reference series, yet a clean inequality is not easy to obtain. In such cases, the following variant is convenient, as it suffices to examine only the limit of the ratio of the terms.

::: Proposition 7 (Limit comparison test)
For positive-term series $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$, if $a_n/b_n \rightarrow c$ and $0<c<\infty$, then the two series either both converge or both diverge.
:::
::: Proof
By the definition of a limit, we can ensure that for sufficiently large $n$,

$$\frac{c}{2}<\frac{a_n}{b_n}<2c$$

holds. Multiplying through by $b_n>0$ yields

$$\frac{c}{2} b_n &< a_n<2c b_n$$

Now, if $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum 2c b_n$ also converges, so by the right-hand inequality and [Theorem 6](#thm6){: data-lid="rjdc7" }, $\sum a_n$ converges. Conversely, if $\sum a_n$ converges, then the left-hand inequality $b_n<(2/c) a_n$ and the comparison test imply that $\sum b_n$ converges. Taking contrapositives yields the statement for divergence.
:::

The usefulness of the limit comparison test lies in replacing terms combining polynomials and powers by the terms that actually govern their convergence. For example, if the terms have the form of a rational expression, we may take as our reference the power obtained by keeping only the fastest-growing terms in the numerator and the denominator; the resulting reference sequence will have the form of a $p$-series, so its convergence can be determined immediately.

Another effective test is the following root test.

::: Proposition 8 (Root test)
For a positive-term series $\sum a_n$ with $\sqrt[n]{a_n} \rightarrow L$, the series converges if $L<1$ and diverges if $L>1$.
:::
::: Proof
If $L<1$, we can choose a number satisfying $L<r<1$; with this $r$, for sufficiently large $n$ we have $\sqrt[n]{a_n}<r$, that is, $a_n<r^n$. Here, the right-hand side defines a convergent geometric series with common ratio $r<1$, so $\sum a_n$ converges by the comparison test. If $L>1$, then for sufficiently large $n$ we have $a_n>1$, so the terms do not tend to $0$, and hence the series diverges by [Proposition 4](#prop4){: data-lid="0kw4f" }.
:::

In general, the ratio test is easier to apply to a sequence, whereas the root test can determine convergence for a wider range of series. However, just like the ratio test, the root test is inconclusive when $L=1$, in which case another method must be tried.

## Absolute Convergence and Alternating Series

For general series that are not positive-term series, mechanical tools such as [§Limits of Sequences, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/calculus/sequences#prop7){: data-lid="bu3li" } or [Theorem 6](#thm6){: data-lid="8gcnm" } cannot be used, so determining convergence becomes somewhat harder. On the other hand, if the signs of a series are mixed, its partial sums neither only increase nor only decrease as we continue adding terms, so convergence of the series itself is more plausible. In this section, we examine series whose signs alternate.

::: Definition 9
If $\sum \lvert a_n\rvert$ converges, we say that $\sum a_n$ is *absolutely convergent*.
:::

An absolutely convergent series always converges. Indeed, since $0 \leq a_n+\lvert a_n\rvert \leq 2\lvert a_n\rvert$, it follows from [Theorem 6](#thm6){: data-lid="vegcr" } that $\sum (a_n+\lvert a_n\rvert)$ converges, and therefore $\sum a_n=\sum (a_n+\lvert a_n\rvert)-\sum \lvert a_n\rvert$ also converges. However, the converse does not hold. When the terms contain both positive and negative numbers, the partial sums cancel one another, so the original series can converge even if the series of absolute values diverges. The next test is the most basic tool guaranteeing such convergence.

::: Proposition 10 (Alternating series test)
If a positive sequence $b_n \geq 0$ is monotonically decreasing and $b_n \rightarrow 0$, then the resulting *alternating series* $\sum (-1)^{n+1} b_n$ converges. Moreover, the error between its sum $S$ and the partial sum $s_N$ is controlled by $\lvert S-s_N\rvert \leq b_{N+1}$.
:::
::: Proof
The even partial sums

$$s_{2m}=(b_1-b_2)+\cdots+(b_{2m-1}-b_{2m})$$

are increasing because each parenthesized term is nonnegative, and since

$$s_{2m}=b_1-(b_2-b_3)-\cdots-b_{2m} \leq b_1$$

they are bounded above. Hence $s_{2m}$ converges, and from $s_{2m+1}=s_{2m}+b_{2m+1}$ with $b_{2m+1} \rightarrow 0$, the odd partial sums also converge to the same limit $S$. On the other hand, since $s_{2m+1}-s_{2m-1}=-b_{2m}+b_{2m+1} \leq 0$, the odd partial sums are decreasing, and thus $s_{2m} \leq S \leq s_{2m+1}$, so the partial sums alternate on either side of $S$. From this, the assertion about the error also follows immediately.
:::

When a series converges without converging absolutely, we call this *conditional convergence*. For example, the alternating harmonic series $\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n$ converges by the alternating series test, but taking absolute values yields the harmonic series, which diverges, so it is conditionally convergent.
