## Vector Bundles

Now we need to define vector fields, and to define concepts such as $C^\infty$ vector fields it is better to first define the notion of a vector bundle. First, let us define a vector bundle over a topological space.

::: Definition 1
A *vector bundle* over a topological space $B$, $\pi:E \rightarrow B$, is an object defined as follows.

- The *total space* $E$ and the *base space* $B$ are both topological spaces, and $\pi:E \rightarrow B$ is a continuous surjection.
- For each $b\in B$, $E_b=\pi^{-1}(b)$ carries the structure of a $k$-dimensional vector space.
- For each $b_0\in B$ there exists a suitable open neighborhood $U\subseteq B$ and a homeomorphism $h:U\times\mathbb{R}^k \rightarrow\pi^{-1}(U)$ such that for every $b\in U$, $x\mapsto h(b,x)$ is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{R}^k$ to $E_b$.

Here, $k$ is called the *rank* of the vector bundle $E\rightarrow B$. The homeomorphism $h$ in the third condition is called a *local trivialization*, and if we can take $U=B$, then $E$ is called a *trivial vector bundle*.
:::

Similarly, we can define a vector bundle over a manifold. To do this, we replace both $E$ and $B$ with manifolds, $\pi$ with a $C^\infty$ surjection, and replace the third condition with

> For each $b_0\in B$ there exists a suitable coordinate system $U\subseteq B$ and a diffeomorphism $h:U\times\mathbb{R}^k\rightarrow\pi^{-1}(U)$ such that for every $b\in U$, $x\mapsto h(b,x)$ is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{R}^k$ to $E_b$.

## Tangent Bundle

A typical example of a vector bundle is the tangent bundle.

::: Example 2 (Tangent bundle)
Define the set $TM$ by

$$TM=\bigsqcup_{p\in M} T_pM$$

Then there is a natural projection map $\pi:TM\rightarrow M$. To regard $TM$ as a vector bundle, we must endow it with a manifold structure.

First, let us define a coordinate system on $TM$. For an arbitrary coordinate system $(U,\varphi)$, define the function $\tilde{\varphi}:\pi^{-1}(U)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^m\times\mathbb{R}^m$ by the formula

$$\tilde{\varphi}(v)=\bigl(x^1(\pi(v)), \ldots, x^m(\pi(v)), \dd{x}^1(v),\ldots, \dd{x}^m(v)\bigr)$$

Then $\tilde{\varphi}$ is a bijection from $\pi^{-1}(U)$ onto the open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{2m}$, $\varphi(U)\times\mathbb{R}^m$.

These are $C^\infty$-compatible. Suppose another coordinate system $(V,\psi)$, $\psi=(y^j)_{j=1}^m$ is given, and define $\tilde{\psi}$ as above. Then on $\pi^{-1}(U)\cap\pi^{-1}(V)=\pi^{-1}(U\cap V)$ we have

$$\begin{aligned}(\tilde{\psi}\circ\tilde{\varphi}^{-1})(p^1, \ldots, p^m, v^1, \ldots, v^m)&=\tilde{\psi}\left(\varphi^{-1}(p), \sum v^i\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\bigg\vert_{\varphi^{-1}(p)}\right)\end{aligned}$$

Writing $v=\sum v^i\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}$ here, the right-hand side can simply be written as

$$\left((\psi\circ\varphi^{-1})(p), \dd{y}^1(v), \ldots, \dd{y}^m(v)\right)$$

Now for any $j$,

$$\dd{y}^j\left(\sum v^i\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\bigg\vert_{\varphi^{-1}(p)}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^m v^i\frac{\partial y^j}{\partial x^i}\bigg\vert_{\varphi^{-1}(p)}$$

Therefore, since each component of the transition map $\tilde{\psi}\circ\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}$ above is $C^\infty$, $\tilde{\psi}\circ\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}$ is also $C^\infty$.

Meanwhile, the topology on $TM$ is obtained by taking the sets

$$\{\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}(W)\mid \text{$W$ open in $\mathbb{R}^{2m}$, $(U,\varphi)\in\mathcal{A}$}\}$$

as a basis. Taking $W=\mathbb{R}^{2m}$, we can check that the $\pi^{-1}(U)$ are all open sets in the topology generated by the above collection, and we can also verify that this topology yields a $2m$-dimensional topological manifold.

Now all that remains is the local trivialization on $TM$. For an arbitrary coordinate system $(U,\varphi)$, it suffices this time to define $\phi:\pi^{-1}(U)\rightarrow U\times\mathbb{R}^m$ by the formula

$$v\vert_p\mapsto (p, \dd{x}^1(v),\ldots, \dd{x}^m(v))$$

On a fixed $\pi^{-1}(p)$, that $\phi$ is an isomorphism between vector spaces is obvious, and it is also obvious that for any $(x,v)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^m$ we have $(\pi\circ\phi^{-1})(x,v)=x$. That $\phi$ is a diffeomorphism holds because

$$\tilde{\varphi}=(\varphi\times\id_{\mathbb{R}^m})\circ\phi$$

and in this formula the two functions other than $\phi$ are both diffeomorphisms.
:::

In particular, when $TM$ is a trivial bundle, $M$ is called a *parallelizable manifold*.

## Smooth functors

The reason the tangent bundle $TM$ is important is that most vector bundles defined on a manifold are defined from $TM$. For example, the cotangent bundle $T^\ast M$ is the vector bundle where at each $p\in M$, the cotangent space $T_p^\ast M$, which is the dual space of the tangent space, is attached. Similarly, various vector bundles are defined at each point $p$ through operations in linear algebra ([Example 5](#ex5){: data-lid="s5l93" }).

Ordinarily, each time these are defined, we would have to show that they satisfy the conditions of a vector bundle, but there is a slightly more fundamental approach in **[Mil]**.

::: Definition 3
Suppose two vector bundles $E\rightarrow B$, $E'\rightarrow B'$ are given. Then a *bundle map* from $E \rightarrow B$ to $E' \rightarrow B'$ means, among pairs $E\rightarrow E', B \rightarrow B'$ making the diagram

{% diagram Math/Manifolds/Tangent_and_Cotangent_Bundles-1.svg width="5.60em" alt="bundle_map" %}

commute, those for which $E_b\rightarrow E'_{b'}$ is an isomorphism.
:::

Now consider the category of finite-dimensional $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces whose morphisms are isomorphisms, $\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$. Then $\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\times\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$ is the category whose

- objects are pairs $(V,W)$ of finite-dimensional vector spaces,
- morphisms are pairs $(V,W)\overset{(f,g)}{\rightarrow}(V',W')$ of isomorphisms between finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Therefore, a functor from $\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\times\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$ to $\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$, $F$, must take $(V,W)$ to an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space $F(V,W)$ and $(f,g)$ to an isomorphism $F(f,g)$.

::: Definition 4
A functor $F:\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\times\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\rightarrow \mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$ is a *smooth functor* if $F(f,g)$ depends smoothly on $f,g$.
:::

If $f\in\Hom(V,V'), g\in\Hom(W,W')$ are isomorphisms, then $F(f,g)\in\Hom(F(V,W),F(V',W'))$. Since these are all vector spaces, they carry the smooth structure described in [§Examples of Differentiable Manifolds, ⁋Example 2](/en/math/manifolds/examples_of_manifolds#ex2){: data-lid="48lcb" }, and through this we can apply the above definition. Also, it is not difficult to extend this definition to a general $k$-fold product

$$\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\times\cdots\times\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}\rightarrow \mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$$

as well.

::: Example 5
$\Hom(-,-)$ is a smooth functor. Suppose two arbitrary isomorphisms $f:V\rightarrow V'$, $g:W\rightarrow W'$ are given. Then $\Hom(f,g)$ is a linear map from $\Hom(V,W)$ to $\Hom(V',W')$ making the diagram below

{% diagram Math/Manifolds/Tangent_and_Cotangent_Bundles-2.svg width="7.11em" alt="Hom_functor" %}

commute. Written as a formula, we can write $\Hom(f,g)(u)=g\circ u\circ f^{-1}$. We can easily verify that $\Hom(f,g)$ depends smoothly on $g$. Consider the correspondence $g\mapsto \Hom(f,g)$. Then for a basis of $\Hom(W,W')$, $w_i^j$,

$$(g+tw_i^j)\circ u\circ f^{-1}=g\circ u\circ f^{-1}+tw_i^j\circ u\circ f^{-1}$$

holds for all $u$, so the directional derivative of this correspondence in the $w_i^j$-direction is $u\mapsto w_i^j\circ u\circ f^{-1}$, which is continuous. Moreover, since this argument holds no matter what linear map is substituted for $g$, we know from this that any higher directional derivative of $g\mapsto\Hom(f,g)$ is always continuous. That is, $g\mapsto\Hom(f,g)$ is $C^\infty$. That this correspondence also depends smoothly on $f$ is somewhat more tedious than for $g$, but the map assigning $f^{-1}$ to an isomorphism $f$ is $C^\infty$ by Cramer's rule and $\Hom(f,g)(u)=g\circ u\circ f^{-1}$ is linear in $f^{-1}$, so we can repeat the above argument for $f^{-1}$.

The following are all examples of smooth functors.

- Dual functor $(-)^\ast$ ([\[Linear Algebra\] §Dual Space](/en/math/linear_algebra/dual_space){: data-lid="cqup0" }),
- $k$-th tensor functor $\mathcal{T}^k(-)$ ([\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Tensor Algebra](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/tensor_algebras){: data-lid="y0f09" }),
- $k$-th symmetric functor $\mathcal{S}^k(-)$ ([\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Tensor Algebra](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/tensor_algebras){: data-lid="pba1v" }),
- $k$-th exterior functor $\bigwedge\nolimits^k(-)$ ([\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Tensor Algebra](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/tensor_algebras){: data-lid="rdkzz" }),
- Tensor product $-\otimes -$,
- Direct sum $-\oplus-$.
:::

The proof of the following theorem can be found in Theorem 3.6 of **[MS]**.

::: Theorem 6
Let an arbitrary smooth functor $F:(\mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso})^n\rightarrow \mathbf{FVect}_\text{iso}$ and, with a common base space $B$, $n$ vector bundles $E_i\rightarrow B$ be given. Then, with the fiber at each $b\in B$ given by

$$E_b=F((E_1)_b,\ldots,(E_n)_b)$$

there exists a vector bundle $E\rightarrow B$.
:::

We denote the vector bundle $E$ obtained by the above process simply as $F(E_1,\ldots, E_n)$.

## Cotangent Bundle

Applying the above [Theorem 6](#thm6){: data-lid="7iizs" } to an arbitrary manifold $M$, the tangent bundle $E=TM\rightarrow M$, and the dual functor $(-)^\ast$, we obtain the following.

::: Definition 7
The *cotangent bundle* defined on a manifold $M$ is the vector bundle $(TM)^\ast$ obtained by [Theorem 6](#thm6){: data-lid="rs087" }. Following the notation for the cotangent space $T_p^\ast M$, we denote this by $T^\ast M$.
:::

$T^\ast M$ is the space where, at each point $p$, the vector space $T_p^\ast M$ is attached. Here, $T_p^\ast M$ is the dual space of the vector space $T_pM$, that is, the space of linear maps that take a vector in $T_pM$ and output a real number. We will revisit vector bundles obtained by applying other smooth functors before long.
