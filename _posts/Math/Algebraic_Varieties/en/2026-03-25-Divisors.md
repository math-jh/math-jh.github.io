Consider a rational function $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)$ defined on a variety $X$. This function vanishes at some points and is undefined at others. Since rational functions are essentially rational expressions, the points where the function is undefined are poles, and we may speak of the order of each pole. To organize this information systematically, we introduce the notion of a *divisor*.

For this framework to be valid, the stalk $\mathcal{O}_{X, \eta_Y}$ at the generic point $\eta_Y$ of every codimension 1 irreducible subvariety $Y$ of $X$ must be a discrete valuation ring. This requires that $X$ be a normal domain ([[Commutative Algebra] §Integral Extensions, ⁋Definition 3](/en/math/commutative_algebra/integral_extension#def3)), or more generally that $X$ satisfy the $R_1$ condition of [[Commutative Algebra] §Regular Local Rings, ⁋Theorem 10](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#thm10). Normality is not a stringent condition, and if a variety fails to be normal we may circumvent the issue by passing to the normalization of [[Commutative Algebra] §Regular Local Rings, ⁋Definition 9](/en/math/commutative_algebra/regular_local_rings#def9). Hence we tacitly assume that $X$ is normal whenever divisors are discussed.

## Weil Divisors

We first examine the most intuitive definition of a divisor, the *Weil divisor*. This is a formal $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of codimension 1 closed subvarieties, where the integer coefficient of each term records the order of the zero or pole along the corresponding subvariety.

::: Definition 1
A *Weil divisor* on a variety $X$ is a formal $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of codimension 1 (irreducible) closed subvarieties of $X$:

$$D = \sum_{i=1}^{r} n_i Y_i$$

We denote the set of Weil divisors by $\Div(X)$.
:::

Then $\Div(X)$ forms an abelian group under addition.

::: Definition 2
A Weil divisor $D = \sum_i n_i Y_i$ is *effective* if all $n_i \ge 0$. We write $D \ge 0$.
:::

As noted above, the basic idea of a Weil divisor is to encode the zeros and poles of a rational function $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$. We call this a *principal divisor*.

::: Definition 3
The *principal divisor* $\divisor(f)$ of a rational function $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ is defined by

$$\divisor(f) = \sum_{Y} v_Y(f) \cdot Y$$

where the sum ranges over all codimension 1 irreducible closed subvarieties $Y$ of $X$, and $v_Y(f)$ is the integer giving the order of the zero or pole of $f$ along $Y$.
:::

If $Y$ is a smooth subvariety of codimension 1, then in a neighborhood of each point $x$ on $Y$, the subvariety $Y$ is defined by a single regular function $\pi$. We call this $\pi$ a *local equation* near $x$. Expanding the rational function $f$ near $x$ as $f = \pi^{v_Y(f)} \cdot u$, all information about the zeros and poles of $f$ is carried by $\pi^{v_Y(f)}$, while $u$ is a function having neither zeros nor poles near $x$; thus $v_Y(f)$ is the zero/pole order along $Y$.

To make this mathematically rigorous, observe that the stalk $\mathcal{O}_{X, \eta}$ of the structure sheaf at the generic point $\eta$ of $Y$ is a discrete valuation ring, and the local equation $\pi$ corresponds to a uniformizer of this ring. Then $v_Y(f)$ is precisely the valuation in this discrete valuation ring. For this to make sense, the stalk $\mathcal{O}_{X, \eta}$ at the generic point of $Y$ must be a discrete valuation ring, and this is exactly why we require $X$ to be a normal variety.

::: Example 4
Consider the regular function $f(\x, \y) = \x^2 \y$ on $\mathbb{A}^2$. This function has no poles, and its zeros lie only along the two irreducible closed subvarieties $D_1=Z(\x)$ and $D_2=Z(\y)$. The zero along $D_1$ has order 2, and the zero along $D_2$ has order $1$, so the principal divisor associated to $f$ is

$$\divisor(f)=2D_1+D_2$$
:::

::: Example 5
Consider the rational function on $\mathbb{A}^1$

$$g(\x) = \frac{(\x-a_1)^{n_1} \cdots (\x-a_k)^{n_k}}{(\x-b_1)^{m_1} \cdots (\x-b_l)^{m_l}}$$

This function has zeros of order $n_i$ at $a_i$ and poles of order $m_j$ at $b_j$, so its principal divisor is

$$\divisor(g) = n_1(a_1) + \cdots + n_k(a_k) - m_1(b_1) - \cdots - m_l(b_l)$$

where $(a_i)$ denotes the divisor supported at the point $a_i$; positive coefficients indicate zeros and negative coefficients indicate poles.
:::

::: Proposition 6
$\divisor: \mathbb{K}(X)^\times \rightarrow \Div(X)$ is a group homomorphism.
:::
::: Proof
For $f, g \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ and each $Y$,

$$v_Y(fg) = v_Y(f) + v_Y(g)$$

hence

$$\divisor(fg) = \sum_Y v_Y(fg) \cdot Y = \sum_Y (v_Y(f) + v_Y(g)) \cdot Y = \divisor(f) + \divisor(g)$$
:::

Our goal is to extract properties of $X$ from $\Div(X)$. However, $\Div(X)$ is unnecessarily large for this purpose. Since the elements of $\mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ are already well understood, we regard two divisors as equivalent if they differ by a principal divisor. That is, we make the following definition.

::: Definition 7
Two Weil divisors $D_1, D_2$ are *linearly equivalent* if there exists a rational function $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ such that $D_1 - D_2 = \divisor(f)$. We write $D_1 \sim D_2$.
:::

Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 8
Linear equivalence $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $\Div(X)$.
:::

::: Proof
First, $D - D = 0 = \divisor(1)$, so $D \sim D$. If $D_1 \sim D_2$, then $D_1 - D_2 = \divisor(f)$ for some $f$, and then $D_2 - D_1 = \divisor(f^{-1})$. Finally, assume $D_1 \sim D_2$ and $D_2 \sim D_3$, so that $D_1 - D_2 = \divisor(f)$ and $D_2 - D_3 = \divisor(g)$ for some $f, g$. Then $D_1 - D_3 = \divisor(fg)$, hence $D_1 \sim D_3$.
:::

::: Definition 9
The *divisor class group* $\Cl(X)$ of $X$ is the quotient of $\Div(X)$ by linear equivalence:

$$\Cl(X) = \Div(X) / \{\divisor(f) \mid f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times\}$$
:::

To determine which elements of $\Div(X)$ coincide in $\Cl(X)$, it suffices to ask whether a given divisor is linearly equivalent to $0$. This is essentially an algebro-geometric translation of [[Algebraic Topology] §Homotopy, ⁋Definition 9](/en/math/algebraic_topology/homotopy#def9). For a fixed space $X$ and a subspace $A$, we say that $A$ is a *deformation retract* of $X$ if there exists a continuous map

$$H:[0,1]\times X\rightarrow X$$

satisfying (i) $H(0, x) = x$, (ii) $H(1, x) \in A$, (iii) $H(t, a) = a$ for all $a \in A$. Thus at $t=0$ we see all of $X$, at $t=1$ we land in $A$, and $A$ itself remains fixed throughout the deformation. To translate this into algebraic geometry, we use $\mathbb{P}^1$ as the parameter space in place of $[0,1]$. More concretely, viewing an element $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ as a rational map $X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^1$, we may consider its graph

$$\Gamma_f =\{(x, t)\in X\times \mathbb{P}^1\mid f(x)=t\}\subseteq X \times \mathbb{P}^1$$

Pulling back the coordinate of $\mathbb{P}^1$ via the canonical projection $\pr_2:\Gamma_f\rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1$ yields $f$ itself, so $\divisor(f)$ measures the difference between the $t=0$ section and the $t=\infty$ section on $\Gamma_f$.

From this motivation, we may say that $\Cl(X)$ is smaller than $\Div(X)$ yet still retains information about the properties of $X$.

::: Example 10
$\Cl(\mathbb{A}^n) = 0$. To verify this, let an arbitrary Weil divisor $D=\sum n_i Y_i$ be given. We must show that it is the principal divisor of some function. Since each $Y_i$ is an irreducible closed subvariety, it corresponds to a prime ideal $I(Y_i)$ of the coordinate ring $\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$, and $\codim Y_i=\codim I(Y_i)=1$. But $\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$ is a unique factorization domain by [[Ring Theory] §Polynomial Rings, ⁋Theorem 16](/en/math/ring_theory/polynomial_rings#thm16), so every height 1 prime ideal is principal. Hence there exists a function $f_i\in \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$ with $I(Y_i)=(f_i)$, and each $Y_i$ is $Z(f_i)$.
:::

Of course, to understand what kind of information $\Cl(X)$ carries, we must consider cases where $\Cl(X)\neq 0$.

::: Example 11
$\Cl(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. To verify this, fix an arbitrary hyperplane class, say $H=Z(\x_0)$, as a reference. We first show that a hypersurface $Z(F)$ defined by an arbitrary degree $d$ homogeneous polynomial $F$ equals $dH$. Consider the function $F/\x_0^d\in \mathbb{K}(\mathbb{P}^n)^\ast$; then

$$\divisor(F/\x_0^d)=\divisor(F)-d\cdot \divisor(\x_0)=Z(F)-dH$$

Thus a Weil divisor $D=\sum n_i Y_i$ is determined by the orders $n_i$ and the degrees $d_i$ of the homogeneous polynomials defining the $Y_i$. Guided by this intuition, we define the map

$$\deg: \Cl(\mathbb{P}^n) \rightarrow \mathbb{Z};\qquad D=\sum n_i Y_i \mapsto \sum n_i \deg(Y_i)$$

where $\deg(Y_i)$ is the degree of the homogeneous polynomial defining $Y_i$. Since every hypersurface is the zero set of some homogeneous polynomial, this definition is unambiguous. Moreover, by [§Rational Maps, ⁋Example 4](/en/math/algebraic_varieties/rational_maps#ex4), any rational function on $\mathbb{P}^n$ is a ratio $F/G$ of homogeneous polynomials $F,G$ of the same degree, so for any principal divisor $\divisor(F)$ we necessarily have $\deg(\divisor(F))=0$; hence the map is well-defined.

We claim that $\deg$ is an isomorphism. Surjectivity is obvious, as the image of $dH$ is $d$. For injectivity, we must show that any $D$ with $\deg(D)=0$ is principal. We have already seen that $D \sim dH$, and for this to map to $0$ under $\deg$ we must have $d=0$; hence $D$ is linearly equivalent to $0$.

Intuitively, the difference from $\mathbb{A}^n$ is that global regular functions on $\mathbb{P}^n$ are constant. Thus if a function on $\mathbb{P}^n$ has a zero at some point, it must also have a pole somewhere else, and the sum of the orders of the zeros equals the sum of the orders of the poles. The isomorphism $\Cl(\mathbb{P}^n)\cong \mathbb{Z}$ means that the $dH$ with $d\neq 0$ are essentially all non-principal divisors of $\mathbb{P}^n$, while the remaining divisors $\sum n_i D_i$ merely distribute this total order $d$ among the $D_i$ via a rational function $f$.
:::

## Cartier Divisors

Weil divisors are geometrically intuitive, but they fail to work well on singular varieties. For example, on the cone $X=Z(\x^2 + \y^2 - \z^2) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^3$, imagine defining a principal divisor following the discussion after [Definition 3](#def3). At a smooth point of this cone, say $(1,0,1)$, consider a codimension $1$ subvariety such as $X\cap Z(\y)$. This subvariety is defined by the local equation $\y=0$ near that point, so for any rational function $f$ we can extract zero and pole information with respect to $\y$. The problem arises at the singular point. Consider the codimension $1$ subvariety $L=(t,0,t)$ near the origin $(0,0,0)$. To describe it, both equations $\y=0$ and $\x-\z=0$ are needed, which means we cannot define $v_L(f)$. More rigorously, $\mathcal{O}_{X,(0,0,0)}$ is not a (one-dimensional) regular local ring and hence not a discrete valuation ring, so a valuation cannot be defined in the first place.

Thus the core issue is that on a singular variety, a codimension $1$ subvariety may fail to be expressed locally by a single equation. We therefore restrict our attention to objects that are locally given by a single equation. As usual, we define this by an appropriate gluing procedure.

::: Definition 12
A *Cartier divisor* on a variety $X$ is data of the form

$$\{(U_i, f_i)\}_{i \in I}$$

where $\{U_i\}$ is an open cover of $X$, each $f_i \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$ is a nonzero rational function, and for all $i, j$ the quotient $f_i/f_j$ is regular and non-vanishing (hence invertible) on $U_i \cap U_j$.

Two such data $\{(U_i, f_i)\}$ and $\{(V_j, g_j)\}$ represent the same Cartier divisor if there exists a common refinement $\{W_k\}$ of the two open covers on which each $f_i/g_j$ is regular and non-vanishing.
:::

By definition, a Cartier divisor is a collection of *locally principal* codimension 1 subvarieties. The data $U_i$ specify exactly which open set is local, and by which single function the divisor is defined there. Consequently, the line $L$ examined above cannot be expressed by a single equation on any open neighborhood of the origin, so it is not a Cartier divisor.

::: Example 13
In the cone example above, consider the line $L'=(t,0,-t)$. This is not a Cartier divisor for the same reason as $L$. However, their sum $L+L'$ is a Cartier divisor, because $L+L'$ is defined by the zero set of $\y$.
:::

As remarked above, a Cartier divisor can be viewed as a codimension 1 subvariety satisfying the additional locally principal condition. Specifically, given a Cartier divisor $\{(U_i, f_i)\}$, we may consider the principal Weil divisor $\divisor(f_i)$ of $f_i$ on each $U_i$. Since $f_i/f_j$ is invertible on $U_i \cap U_j$, we have $v_Y(f_i)=v_Y(f_j)$ for every codimension $1$ subvariety $Y$, and thus we can glue these together to obtain a Weil divisor.

The converse does not hold in general, as we saw above; but in the smooth case the stalk is a regular local ring, so there is no difficulty. Hence the following holds.

::: Proposition 14
On a smooth variety $X$, Weil divisors and Cartier divisors are in natural one-to-one correspondence.
:::

Essentially, a Weil divisor plays a role analogous to a homology class—certain subsets of the space $X$—while a Cartier divisor, by its very definition, plays a role analogous to a cohomology class—functions defined (locally) on $X$. From this perspective, the proposition may be regarded as a kind of Poincaré duality. ([[Algebraic Topology] §Poincaré Duality, ⁋Theorem 11](/en/math/algebraic_topology/Poincare_duality#thm11))

We now define linear equivalence and the divisor class group for Cartier divisors, just as we did for Weil divisors. To do so, we first need the following.

::: Definition 15
For a rational function $f \in \mathbb{K}(X)^\times$, the *principal Cartier divisor* $\divisor(f)$ is defined as $\{(X, f)\}$.
:::

Then the following definition is the Cartier analogue of [Definition 7](#def7).

::: Definition 16
Two Cartier divisors $D_1, D_2$ are *linearly equivalent* if $D_1 - D_2$ is a principal divisor.
:::

We denote the group of Cartier divisors by $\CaDiv(X)$, and the subgroup generated by principal divisors by $\Prin(X)$. Then the *Cartier divisor class group* is

$$\CaCl(X) = \CaDiv(X) / \Prin(X)$$

By [Proposition 14](#prop14), on a smooth variety we have $\CaCl(X) \cong \Cl(X)$.
