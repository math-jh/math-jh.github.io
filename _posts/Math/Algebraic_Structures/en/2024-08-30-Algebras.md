Now we define the notion of an $A$-algebra. An algebra is a structure obtained by adding to an $A$-module $M$ a multiplication structure compatible with the addition and scalar multiplication of $M$, which is the same process as defining on an abelian group $G$ a multiplication compatible with the addition of $G$. However, there are two problems with using [§Definition of a Ring, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/algebraic_structures/rings#def1){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } directly.

First, the first problem is that for a general ring $A$, $\lMod{A}$ or $\rMod{A}$ is not a monoidal category. In the definition above, we defined the multiplication of a ring as $A\otimes A\rightarrow A$; similarly, when attempting to define multiplication on a module, $M\otimes_AM$ may not be defined, so this approach cannot be used. This is because if $A$ is a commutative ring, $(\lMod{A},\otimes_A, A)$ becomes a symmetric monoidal category, so this is resolved by assuming that $A$ is a commutative ring. ([§Direct Products, Direct Sums, and Tensor Products of Modules, §§Tensor Products of Modules over Commutative Rings](/en/math/algebraic_structures/operations_of_modules#tensor-products-of-modules-over-commutative-rings){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" })

The second problem is a more practical one: while this structure is widely used in many fields, the properties required in each case are all different. That is, if we repeat [§Definition of a Ring, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/algebraic_structures/rings#def1){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } as is, and in the symmetric monoidal category $(\lMod{A},\otimes_A,A)$, call a monoid object $E$ an $A$-algebra, then due to the associativity axiom and unit axiom of a monoid object, the multiplication of $E$ must always satisfy associativity and have an identity. On the other hand, important examples such as [\[Lie Theory\] §Lie Groups, ⁋Definition 8](/en/math/lie_theory/Lie_groups#def8){: data-relation="forward" reviewed="" } may not satisfy these conditions.

For these reasons, from now on we always assume $A$ to be a commutative ring, and we define an $A$-algebra in its most general form as follows.

::: Definition 1
If an $A$-module $E$ is equipped with an $A$-bilinear map $\mu:E\times E \rightarrow E$, we call $E$ an *$A$-algebra*. In addition, depending on the properties satisfied by $E$'s multiplication $\mu$, we define the following:

- If the multiplication of $E$ is commutative, we call it a *commutative $A$-algebra*.
- If the multiplication of $E$ is associative, we call it an *associative $A$-algebra*.
- If the multiplication of $E$ has an identity element, we call it a *unital $A$-algebra*.
:::

Then, a monoid object of $(\lMod{A},\otimes_A,A)$ is precisely an associative unital $A$-algebra. 

Meanwhile, an associative unital $A$-algebra can be thought of as an $A$-module $E$ equipped with a multiplication, but it can also be regarded as a ring $E$ equipped with a scalar multiplication. Fix an arbitrary ring homomorphism $\rho:A \rightarrow E$, and assume that $\rho(A)$ is contained in the center of $E$, $Z(E)$. Then, viewing $E$ as a module over itself, the restriction of scalars

$$\rho^\ast:\lMod{E} \rightarrow \lMod{A}$$

defines an $A$-module $\rho^\ast E$. Then, on $\rho^\ast E$, the multiplication of $E$ defines $\mu:\rho^\ast E\times \rho^\ast E \rightarrow \rho^\ast E$. Since

$$\mu(\alpha\cdot x, y)=(\rho(\alpha)x)y=\rho(\alpha)xy$$

and $\rho(\alpha)\in Z(E)$, we can verify the following relation:

$$\alpha\cdot \mu(x,y)=\mu(\alpha\cdot x,y)=\mu(x,\alpha\cdot y)$$

Conversely, given any associative unital $A$-algebra $E$,

$$\rho_E:A\rightarrow E;\qquad \alpha\mapsto \alpha\cdot 1_E$$

is a ring homomorphism, and these constructions are inverses of each other. That is, these data are precisely the same, and a ring homomorphism endowing $E$ with an $A$-algebra structure, $\rho:A \rightarrow E$, is called a *structure morphism*.

::: Definition 2
Let two $A$-algebras $E,E'$ be given. An $A$-module homomorphism $u: E \rightarrow E'$ is an *$A$-algebra homomorphism* if the equation

$$u(xy)=u(x)u(y)$$

holds for all $x,y\in E$. If $E$ and $E'$ are unital and $u(1)=1$, we call it a *unital $A$-algebra homomorphism*.
:::

Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 3
The composition of $A$-algebra homomorphisms is an $A$-algebra homomorphism. A bijective $A$-algebra homomorphism is always an isomorphism. Moreover, the composition of unital $A$-algebra homomorphisms is unital, and the inverse of a bijective unital $A$-algebra homomorphism is also unital.
:::
::: Proof
That the composition is an $A$-algebra homomorphism follows directly by substituting into the definition. Meanwhile, for a bijective $A$-algebra homomorphism $u:E\rightarrow E'$, the fact that $u^{-1}$ is $A$-linear is a consequence of [§Modules, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/algebraic_structures/modules#prop7){: data-relation="required" reviewed="" }, and since for any $x,y\in E'$ we have $u(u^{-1}(x)u^{-1}(y))=xy$, the injectivity of $u$ yields $u^{-1}(xy)=u^{-1}(x)u^{-1}(y)$. In the case of a unital $A$-algebra homomorphism, in addition to this calculation, it suffices to verify that the following two identities

$$(u\circ v)(1)=u(1)=1,\qquad u(1)=1\implies 1=u^{-1}(1)$$

hold.
:::

Therefore, the category of $A$-algebras is well-defined, and the category of unital $A$-algebras and unital $A$-algebra homomorphisms is also well-defined. We denote the category of associative unital $A$-algebras and unital $A$-algebra homomorphisms by $\Alg{A}$, and write $\cAlg{A}$ for its full subcategory consisting of commutative ones.

## Examples of Algebras

Just as a free ring is defined on an abelian group, for any $A$-module, a free associative unital $A$-algebra is defined. This process is completely identical to the construction in [§Definition of a Ring, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/algebraic_structures/rings#prop4){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" }. On reflection, this is natural: the only additional thing we need to show is that the multiplication defined here is compatible with the scalar multiplication of $A$, and this is because requiring $A$ to be commutative already defines a scalar multiplication on $F(M)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}M^{\otimes n}$.

That is, the following holds.

::: Proposition 4
For any $A$-module $M$, if we define

$$F(M)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0} M^{\otimes n}$$

then $F$ is the left adjoint to the forgetful functor $\Alg{A} \rightarrow \lMod{A}$. ([\[Category Theory\] §Adjoint Functors, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/category_theory/adjoints#def1){: data-relation="required" reviewed="" })
:::

The $F(M)$ obtained from the above proposition directly uses the addition defined on $M$ and is obtained by defining multiplication as a new operation. On the other hand, it is also possible to make this into an $A$-algebra using the multiplication defined on an appropriate group $G$.

::: Definition 5
Fix a ring $A$, not necessarily commutative, and an arbitrary group $G$. Then the *group ring* $AG$ is, first, as a set, the collection of finitely supported functions from $G$ to $A$, where the operations for two functions $\alpha:G \rightarrow A$ and $\beta: G \rightarrow A$ are given by

$$\alpha+\beta: x\mapsto \alpha(x)+\beta(x),\qquad \alpha\beta:x\mapsto \sum_{uv=x}\alpha(u)\beta(v)$$

If $A$ is a commutative ring, $AG$ also has a natural $A$-module structure and thus becomes an associative unital $A$-algebra. In this case, $AG$ is called a *group algebra*.
:::

For each $x\in G$, if we define $\delta_x: G \rightarrow A$ by

$$\delta_x(y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x=y$}\\0&\text{if $x\neq y$}\end{cases}$$

then any element of $AG$ can be written, for a family indexed by elements of $G$, namely $\alpha_x\in A$, as

$$\sum_{x\in G} \alpha_x\delta_x,\qquad\text{$\alpha_x=0$ for all but finitely many $x$}$$

For convenience, if we denote the function $\delta_x$ by $x$, the multiplication in $AG$ can be written as

$$\left(\sum_{x\in G} \alpha_xx\right)\left(\sum_{y\in G} \beta_yy\right)=\sum_{x,y\in G}\alpha_x\beta_yxy=\sum_{z\in G}\left(\sum_{x\in G}\alpha_x\beta_{x^{-1}z}\right)z$$

Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 6
Consider two functors

$$A-: \Grp \rightarrow \Alg{A},\qquad (-)^\times:\Alg{A} \rightarrow \Grp$$

Here, $(-)^\times$ is the functor that, given an arbitrary $A$-algebra $E$, yields the group of units $E^\times$. Then the adjunction

$$A{-}\dashv (-)^\times$$

holds.
:::
::: Proof
That is, for any group $G$ and $A$-algebra $E$, we must show the isomorphism

$$\Hom_{\Alg{A}}(AG, E)\cong \Hom_\Grp(G, E^\times)$$

. First, suppose a group homomorphism $f:G \rightarrow E^\times$ is given. Then from this, through the formula

$$\tilde{f}:AG \rightarrow E;\quad \sum_{x\in G} \alpha_x\delta_x\mapsto \sum_{x\in G} \alpha_xf(x)$$

we can define $\tilde{f}:AG \rightarrow E$. That $\tilde{f}$ preserves addition and scalar multiplication follows directly from the definition, and for multiplication as well, since

$$\begin{aligned}\tilde{f}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in G} a_xx\right)\left(\sum_{y\in G} b_yy\right)\right)&=\tilde{f}\left(\sum_{x,y\in G}a_xb_yxy\right)=\tilde{f}\left(\sum_{z\in G}\left(\sum_{x\in G}a_xb_{x^{-1}z}\right)z\right)\\&=\sum_{z\in G}\left(\sum_{x\in G}a_xb_{x^{-1}z}\right)f(z)=\sum_{x,y\in G} a_xb_yf(xy)\\&=\left(\left(\sum_{x\in G} a_xf(x)\right)\left(\sum_{y\in G} b_yf(y)\right)\right)=\tilde{f}\left(\sum_{x\in G} a_xx\right)\tilde{f}\left(\sum_{y\in G} b_yy\right)\end{aligned}$$

holds, the desired identity is satisfied. Also, since

$$\tilde{f}(1_{AG})=\tilde{f}(\delta_e)=f(e)=1_E$$

, $\tilde{f}$ is unital. Conversely, suppose an arbitrary unital $A$-algebra homomorphism $u: AG \rightarrow E$ is given. Then the function defined by

$$\bar{u}: G \rightarrow E;\qquad x\mapsto u(\delta_x)$$

preserves multiplication. This is because for any $x,y\in G$,

$$\bar{u}(xy)=u(\delta_{xy})=u(\delta_x\delta_y)=u(\delta_x)u(\delta_y)=\bar{u}(x)\bar{u}(y)$$

holds. Also, since $u$ sends $1_{AG}=\delta_e$ to $1_E$, for any $x\in G$,

$$\bar{u}(x)\bar{u}(x^{-1})=u(\delta_x\delta_{x^{-1}})=u(\delta_e)=1_E$$

holds, and therefore the image of $\bar{u}$ is contained in $E^\times$. These two constructions are inverses of each other because $\tilde{f}$ sends $\delta_x$ to $f(x)$, so $\bar{\tilde{f}}=f$, and conversely, because the elements $\delta_x$ generate $AG$ as an $A$-module, agreeing on each $\delta_x$ implies that the two $A$-algebra homomorphisms $\tilde{\bar{u}}$ and $u$ are equal.
:::

The final example is already somewhat familiar, revisiting what we briefly examined as a ring in [§Graded Rings, ⁋Example 5](/en/math/algebraic_structures/graded_rings#ex5){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } now as an $A$-algebra. Earlier, in [Definition 5](#def5){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" }, we exceptionally considered the case where $A$ is not commutative in order to define general group rings, but when dealing with $A$-algebras, our assumption that $A$ is always a commutative ring still remains in effect.

::: Definition 7
For a commutative ring $A$, the *polynomial algebra* $A[\x]$ is, as a set,

$$A[\x]=\{p(\x)=a_n\x^n+\cdots+a_1\x+a_0\mid a_i\in A\}$$

where addition and multiplication are given by polynomial addition and multiplication, and scalar multiplication by $A$ is likewise given by scalar multiplication of polynomials. More generally, for any set $S$, we can define $A[S]$ in a similar manner.
:::

In the case of the multivariable polynomial algebra $A[S]$, for convenience of notation, it is customary to choose an index set $I$ satisfying $\lvert S\rvert=\lvert I\rvert$ and write the elements of $S$ as $\x_i$. Then, for any

$$\alpha=(\alpha_i)_{i\in I}\in \mathbb{N}_{\geq0}^I\qquad\text{finitely supported}$$

we write

$$\x^\alpha=\prod_{i\in I}\x_i^{\alpha_i}$$

and the elements of $A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$ can be written as

$$\sum_{d=0}^n \sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=d} a_\alpha\x^\alpha,\qquad\text{$a_\alpha=0$ for all but finitely many $\alpha$}$$

Here, $\lvert\alpha\rvert=\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i$.

Similarly, the polynomial algebra also satisfies the following universal property.

::: Proposition 8
The functor that takes a set $S$ and, with each element of $S$ as a variable, associates the polynomial algebra $A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$, denoted $A[-]:\Set \rightarrow \cAlg{A}$, is a left adjoint to the forgetful functor $U: \cAlg{A} \rightarrow \Set$. 
:::
::: Proof
That is, we must show that the isomorphism

$$\Hom_{\cAlg{A}}(A[\x_i]_{i\in I}, E)\cong\Hom_\Set(S, UE)$$

holds. First, suppose an arbitrary function $f:S \rightarrow U(E)$ is given. Then the map $\tilde{f}: A[\x_i]_{i\in I} \rightarrow E$ defined by

$$\tilde{f}: \sum_{d=0}^n \sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=d} a_\alpha\x^\alpha\mapsto \sum_{d=0}^n\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=d}a_\alpha\prod_{i\in I}f(\x_i)^{\alpha_i}$$

is a map from $A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$ to $E$ that is an $A$-algebra homomorphism. Indeed, $\tilde{f}$ is by definition $A$-linear, and since $\tilde{f}(\x^\alpha\x^\beta)=\tilde{f}(\x^{\alpha+\beta})=\tilde{f}(\x^\alpha)\tilde{f}(\x^\beta)$ holds on monomials, it preserves multiplication by the distributive law, and from the term with $\alpha=0$, we have $\tilde{f}(1)=1$.

Conversely, given any unital $A$-algebra homomorphism $u:A[\x_i]_{i\in I} \rightarrow E$, we may define $\bar{u}:S \rightarrow UE$ by $\x_i\mapsto u(\x_i)$. These two correspondences are inverses of each other: since $\tilde{f}$ sends $\x_i$ to $f(\x_i)$, we have $\bar{\tilde{f}}=f$, and conversely, since the $\x_i$ generate $A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$ as an $A$-algebra, and because they agree on the $\x_i$, the two $A$-algebra homomorphisms $\tilde{\bar{u}}$ and $u$ are equal.
:::

That is, we can think of a polynomial algebra as a kind of free commutative associative unital $A$-algebra. Comparing [Definition 5](#def5){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } and [Definition 7](#def7){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" }, there is a slight similarity: if we generalize [Definition 5](#def5){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } to consider the *monoid ring*, the polynomial ring of [Definition 7](#def7){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" } becomes the monoid ring formed by the monoid of elements of the form $\x^\alpha$. 

## Subalgebras, Ideals, and Quotient Algebras

::: Definition 9
For an $A$-algebra $E$, if a submodule $F$ is closed under multiplication, then $F$ is called a *subalgebra* of $E$. Also, if $E$ is unital and $1_E\in F$, then $F$ is called a *unital subalgebra*.
:::

Meanwhile, just as we defined in [§Definition of a Ring, ⁋Definition 7](/en/math/algebraic_structures/rings#def7){: data-relation="weak" reviewed="" }, we also define ideals of an $A$-algebra.

::: Definition 10
For an $A$-algebra $E$, a submodule $\mathfrak{a}$ is a *left ideal* of $E$ if for any $x\in \mathfrak{a}$ and $\alpha\in E$, we have $\alpha x\in \mathfrak{a}$. Similarly, we define a *right ideal*. A submodule that is both a left ideal and a right ideal is called a *two-sided ideal*. 
:::

In particular, when $E$ is associative and unital, an ideal of the $A$-algebra is the same as an ideal of $E$ obtained by forgetting the $A$-action defined on it and viewing $E$ as a ring. Indeed, for any $\alpha\in A$ and $x\in E$, we have $\alpha\cdot x=(\alpha\cdot 1_E)x$, so an ideal as a ring is automatically a submodule. On the other hand, since a unital subalgebra in [Definition 9](#def9){: data-relation="required" reviewed="" } contains $1_E$, the only subset that is both an ideal and a unital subalgebra is $E$ itself. As an example, the kernel of an $A$-algebra homomorphism $u:E \rightarrow F$

$$\ker u=\{x\in E\mid u(x)=0\}$$

is a two-sided ideal of $E$. Then we can define the following.

::: Definition 11
For any $A$-algebra $E$ and any two-sided ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $E$, we call $E/\mathfrak{a}$ the *quotient algebra* of $E$ by $\mathfrak{a}$.
:::

By the two-sided ideal condition,

$$(x+\mathfrak{a})(y+\mathfrak{a})=xy+\mathfrak{a}$$

is well-defined independently of the choice of representatives. Moreover, since $\mathfrak{a}$ is a submodule, the addition and $A$-action of $E$ also descend to $E/\mathfrak{a}$, from which we obtain that $E/\mathfrak{a}$ is an $A$-algebra. If $E$ is associative, commutative, or unital, then $E/\mathfrak{a}$ also has the respective property. Furthermore, the following holds.

::: Proposition 12
For an $A$-algebra homomorphism $u:E \rightarrow F$ and its kernel $\ker u$, the following hold.

1. $\ker u$ is a two-sided ideal of $E$, and $x+\ker u \mapsto u(x)$ defines a well-defined isomorphism $E/\ker u \rightarrow \im u$.
2. In $E$, for a subalgebra $E'$, $E'+\ker u=\{x'+y\mid x'\in E', y\in\ker u\}$ is a subalgebra of $E$, $E'\cap\ker u$ is a two-sided ideal of $E'$, and there exists an isomorphism $(E'+\ker u)/\ker u\cong E'/(E'\cap \ker u)$.
3. In $E$, if two two-sided ideals $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$ satisfy $\mathfrak{b}\subseteq \mathfrak{a}$, then $\mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{b}$ is a two-sided ideal of $E/\mathfrak{b}$ and $(E/\mathfrak{b})/(\mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{b})\cong E/\mathfrak{a}$ holds.
4. In $E$, for a two-sided ideal $\mathfrak{a}$, there exists an inclusion-preserving bijection between the set of two-sided ideals of $E/\mathfrak{a}$ and the set of two-sided ideals containing $\mathfrak{a}$ in $E$.
:::

The proof of each part is obtained by the same construction as in [§Quotient Rings and Ring Isomorphisms, ⁋Theorem 3](/en/math/algebraic_structures/quotient_rings#thm3){: data-relation="required" reviewed="" }. Here, only addition, bilinear multiplication, and the two-sided ideal condition are used, and the fact that each map is $A$-linear follows from the corresponding subset of $E$ being closed under the $A$-action. Therefore, the four assertions hold for $A$-algebras without assuming associativity, commutativity, or a unit. Even when the $A$-algebras involved are associative or commutative, the same construction preserves those properties, and when unital $A$-algebras, unital homomorphisms, and unital subalgebras are used, the isomorphisms above are also unital.
