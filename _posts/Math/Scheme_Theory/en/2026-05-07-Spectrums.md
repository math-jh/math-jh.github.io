::: remark Remark {#rmk}
In every post in this category, a ring means a commutative ring (with unity).
:::

In this post we define the spectrum, the most fundamental object of algebraic geometry. The spectrum is a topological space equipped with a suitable structure sheaf; we first define it as a set in this post, and then put a topology on it. Afterwards, in the next post, we will define a structure sheaf on $\Spec A$.

## $\Spec A$ as a set

::: Definition 1
For a ring $A$, $\Spec A$ is the collection of all prime ideals of $A$, and we call it the *spectrum* of $A$.
:::

Now suppose a ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow B$ is given. Then by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#prop9), the following map

$$\Spec\phi: \Spec B \rightarrow \Spec A;\qquad \mathfrak{q}\mapsto \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})$$

is well-defined.

::: Proposition 2
The $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Set$ defined above is a functor.
:::

That is, $\Spec(\phi\circ\psi)=(\Spec\psi)\circ(\Spec\phi)$ and $\Spec(\id_A)=\id_{\Spec A}$, and the proof is not difficult either.

## $\Spec A$ as a topological space

Now we define an appropriate topological structure on $\Spec A$.

::: Definition 3
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any nonempty subset $S$ of $A$, we define the subset $Z(S)$ of $\Spec A$ by the formula

$$Z(S)=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}.$$
:::

Then several facts can be easily proved. First, $Z$ is inclusion-reversing. That is, if two subsets $S_1\subseteq S_2$ of $A$ are given,

$$Z(S_1)=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S_1\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}\supseteq \{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S_2\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}=Z(S_2)$$

holds. However, the above inclusion may not be strict.

::: Proposition 4
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any subset $S$ of $A$ and the ideal $(S)$ of $A$ generated by $S$, we have $Z(S)=Z((S))$.
:::
::: Proof
Since $S\subseteq (S)$ trivially, the inclusion $Z((S))\subseteq Z(S)$ is obvious from the above discussion. Thus it suffices to prove the opposite direction. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be an arbitrary element of $Z(S)$. That is, $S\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$. But the ideal generated by the subset $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ is itself, so we know $(S)\subseteq (\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{p}$.
:::

Moreover, even for two *ideals* $\mathfrak{a}_1\subseteq \mathfrak{a}_2$ of $A$, the inclusion $Z(\mathfrak{a}_1)\supseteq Z(\mathfrak{a}_2)$ may in general be an equality.

::: Proposition 5
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$ and its radical $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{a})=Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})$.
:::
::: Proof
Again, the inclusion $Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{a})$ is obvious from $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$. Conversely, for any $\mathfrak{p}\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$, using [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8) we have

$$\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{q}$ a prime containing $\mathfrak{a}$}\mathfrak{q}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$$

so we know $\mathfrak{p}\in Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})$.
:::

The most important thing when defining a topological structure on $\Spec A$ is the following lemma.

::: Lemma 6
The following hold.

1. For any ideals $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{ab})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})$.
2. For a collection of ideals $\{\mathfrak{a}_i\}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\sum \mathfrak{a}_i)=\bigcap Z(\mathfrak{a}_i)$.
3. For any ideals $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{b})\iff \sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}\supseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{b}}$.
:::
::: Proof
1. It is obvious that a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ containing $\mathfrak{a}$ or $\mathfrak{b}$ also contains the smaller ideal $\mathfrak{ab}$, so it suffices to show the opposite inclusion. Suppose $\mathfrak{p}\supset \mathfrak{ab}$. If $\mathfrak{p}\not\supseteq \mathfrak{b}$, then we can find an element $b$ of $\mathfrak{b}$ with $b\not\in \mathfrak{p}$. On the other hand, for any $a\in \mathfrak{a}$, we have $ab\in \mathfrak{ab}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, and by the previous assumption $b\not\in \mathfrak{p}$, so necessarily $a\in \mathfrak{p}$ and therefore $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$.
2. This is obvious because $\sum \mathfrak{a}_i$ is defined as the smallest ideal containing all the ideals $\mathfrak{a}_i$.
3. [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8).
:::

Then by [\[Topology\] §Interior, Closure, Boundary, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/topology/other_concepts#prop2), there exists a unique topology $\mathcal{T}$ having the $Z(\mathfrak{a})$ as closed sets, which makes $\Spec A$ a topological space.

::: Definition 7
The topology on $\Spec A$ defined above is called the *Zariski topology*.
:::

The Zariski topology is generally not the kind of topology we would have liked. For example, for any integral domain $A$, $(0)$ is itself a prime ideal, and the only nonempty subset of $A$ contained in it is $(0)$ itself. That is, the only closed set containing $(0)\in\Spec A$ is $Z(0)=\Spec A$, so as long as $A$ is not a field, the singleton $\{(0)\}$ is not a closed set. In particular, the Zariski topology is generally not a Hausdorff space. However, as we proceed through the posts in this category, we will discover familiar geometric concepts in this structure.

Earlier we saw in [Proposition 2](#prop2) that we can regard $\Spec$ as a functor $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Set$. Moreover, $\Spec$ is also a functor from $\cRing^\op$ to $\Top$.

::: Proposition 8
If we equip the spectrum $\Spec A$ of a ring $A$ with the topological structure of [Definition 7](#def7), the functor $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Top$ of [Proposition 2](#prop2) is a functor.
:::
::: Proof
What remains to be shown in addition to [Proposition 2](#prop2) is that for any ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow B$, the map $\Spec \phi: \Spec B \rightarrow \Spec A$ is *continuous*. Thus it suffices to show that for any closed set of $\Spec A$, its preimage under $\Spec\phi$ is also a closed set in $\Spec B$. ([\[Topology\] §Interior, Closure, Boundary, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/topology/other_concepts#prop2))

Meanwhile, any closed set of $\Spec A$ is of the form $Z(\mathfrak{a})$, and any closed set of $\Spec B$ is of the form $Z(\mathfrak{b})$, so to show this it suffices to show that for any ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$, there exists an ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ of $B$ satisfying the equation

$$(\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))=Z(\mathfrak{b}).$$

Our claim is that the equation

$$(\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))=Z(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))$$

holds. Then the ideal generated by $\phi(\mathfrak{a})$ satisfies the above equation, so the proof is complete.

First suppose $\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec B$ belongs to the left-hand side. That is, $(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q})=\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$ holds. Then from $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})$ we have $\phi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$, so $\mathfrak{q}\in Z(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))$ holds.

Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec B$ belongs to the right-hand side. Then from $\phi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$, we obtain the inclusion

$$\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \phi^{-1}(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))\subseteq\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})=(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q})$$

and this proves that $(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q})\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$, i.e. $\mathfrak{q}\in (\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))$.
:::

The two important ring homomorphisms we will use from now on are the quotient $\pi:A \rightarrow A/\mathfrak{a}$ and the localization $\epsilon: A \rightarrow S^{-1}A$. ([\[Commutative Algebra\] §Basic Notions, ⁋Proposition 11](/en/math/commutative_algebra/basic_notions#prop11) and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8))

::: Proposition 9
For the $\pi:A \rightarrow A/\mathfrak{a}$ and $\epsilon: A \rightarrow S^{-1}A$ defined above, the following hold.

1. $\Spec\pi$ and $\Spec\epsilon$ are both injective, and they define homeomorphisms onto their respective images.
2. The image of $\Spec\pi$ in $\Spec A$ is a closed set.
3. If $S=\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$ for some $f\in A$, then the image of $\Spec \epsilon$ in $\Spec A$ is an open set.
:::
::: Proof
The first result is the result of the two propositions [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Basic Notions, ⁋Proposition 11](/en/math/commutative_algebra/basic_notions#prop11) and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8) mentioned above. For the second result, the image of $\Spec\pi$ in $\Spec A$ is exactly $Z(\mathfrak{a})$. Finally, for the third result, the elements of $\Spec\epsilon$ are the collection of prime ideals not containing $f$, and these can be written as $\Spec A\setminus Z(f)$.
:::

It is of course true that a base for the topological space $\Spec A$ exists, but we also want this base to have some relationship with the algebraic structure of $A$. Let us define the following.

::: Definition 10
For any element $f\in A$ of a ring $A$, we write the complement of $Z(f)$ in $\Spec A$ as $D(f)$. An open set of this form is called a *principal open set*.
:::

Now for any $f\in A$, let $S_f=\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$ and define $A_f=S_f^{-1}A$. Then by [Proposition 9](#prop9), the image of $\Spec A_f$ in $\Spec A$ under $\Spec \epsilon$ is an open set. That this open set is exactly the same as $D(f)$ as a set is obvious from

$$\mathfrak{p}\not\in Z(f)\iff (f)\not\subseteq \mathfrak{p} \iff f\not\in \mathfrak{p}\iff f^k\not\in \mathfrak{p}\text{ for all $k\geq 0$}\iff S_f\cap \mathfrak{p}=\emptyset$$

and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8). Moreover, that $D(f)$ has the same topological structure as $\Spec A_f$ also follows from the fact that the correspondence in [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8) preserves inclusion.

::: Lemma 11
The collection of principal open sets forms a base for $\Spec A$. ([\[Topology\] §Bases of Topological Spaces, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/topological_bases#def1))
:::
::: Proof
For any open set $\Spec A \setminus Z(S)$, using the second result of [Lemma 6](#lem6) we have the following computation

$$\Spec A\setminus Z(S)=\Spec A\setminus Z\left(\sum_{f\in S} (f)\right)=\Spec A\setminus\left(\bigcap_{f\in S}Z(f)\right)=\bigcup_{f\in S} (\Spec A\setminus Z(f))=\bigcup_{f\in S} D(f)$$

holds.
:::

Then by a computation similar to this lemma, we can verify that $D(fg)=D(f)\cap D(g)$. However, since this computation uses the first result of [Lemma 6](#lem6), it cannot in general be extended to infinite indices.

Separately, we can show that $\Spec A$ always satisfies the condition of [\[Topology\] §Compact Spaces, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/compact_spaces#def1). However, many of the properties we usually expect from a compact space often require the Hausdorff condition as well ([\[Topology\] §Compact Spaces, §§Compact Hausdorff Spaces](/en/math/topology/compact_spaces#compact-hausdorff-spaces)), and since the Zariski topology is generally not a Hausdorff space, we call it *quasi-compact*.

::: Lemma 12
$\Spec A$ is a quasi-compact space as a topological space.
:::
::: Proof
From [Lemma 11](#lem11) it suffices to show that whenever a collection of principal open sets $\{D(f_i)\}_{i\in I}$ covering $\Spec A$ is given, there exists a finite subset $J$ of $I$ such that still $\Spec A=\bigcup_{j\in J} D(f_j)$.

From the given assumption $\Spec A=\bigcup_{i\in I} D(f_i)$ we know

$$\emptyset=\Spec A\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I} D(f_i)=\bigcap_{i\in I}(\Spec A\setminus D(f_i))=\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i).$$

Meanwhile, from [Lemma 6](#lem6)

$$\emptyset=\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i)=Z\left(\sum_{i\in I}(f_i)\right)$$

and if $\sum (f_i)$ were a proper ideal of $A$, then a maximal ideal containing it would be an element of $\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i)$, so the above equation is equivalent to $\sum(f_i)=(1)$. Now since $1\in\sum(f_i)$, we can find a finite subset $J\subseteq I$ and a family of elements $(a_j)_{j\in J}$ of $A$ such that $\sum_{j\in J} a_jf_j=1$. That is,

$$Z\left(\sum_{j\in J} (f_j)\right)=\emptyset$$

and reversing the above computations from this yields the desired result.
:::


## Galois correspondence

By definition $Z$ is a function that takes an ideal of $A$ and outputs a closed set of $\Spec A$. Conversely, we define the following.

::: Definition 13
For any subset $T\subseteq \Spec A$,

$$I(T)=\{f\in A\mid\text{$f\in \mathfrak{p}$ for all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime in $T$} \mathfrak{p}$$

is defined.
:::

Then $I(T)$ is an ideal because it is an intersection of ideals. Moreover, if $T_1\subseteq T_2$, then $I(T_2)\subseteq I(T_1)$ is obvious.

Now combining [Definition 3](#def3) and [Definition 13](#def13), we have defined two functions between the two ordered sets $\mathcal{P}(A)$, $\mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$

$$Z: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A);\quad S\mapsto Z(S),\qquad I: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A);\quad T\mapsto I(T).$$

Then for any $S\in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and any $T\in \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$,

$$T\subseteq Z(S)\iff\text{$\mathfrak{p}\in Z(S)$ for all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\iff\text{$f\in \mathfrak{p}$ for all $f\in S$ and all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\iff S\subseteq I(T)$$

so $(Z, I)$ defines an antitone Galois connection between $\mathcal{P}(A)$ and $\mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$. ([\[Set Theory\] §Filters and Ideals, Galois Correspondence, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/set_theory/filter_and_ideal#def6)) Therefore, the two equations

$$Z(I(Z(S)))=Z(S),\qquad I(Z(I(T)))=I(T)$$

hold for all $S\in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and any $T\in \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$.

Now consider the two closure operators $IZ: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$, $ZI: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$.

::: Proposition 14
For the closure operators $IZ: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$, $ZI: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$, the following hold.

1. $$IZ(S)=\sqrt{(S)}$$
2. $ZI(T)=\cl(T)$
:::
::: Proof
1. This is obvious from the formula
    
    $$I(Z(S))=I(Z((S)))=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime in $Z((S))$} \mathfrak{p}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime containing $(S)$} \mathfrak{p}=\sqrt{(S)}.$$
2. This is obvious from the formula
    
    $$\cl(T)
    =\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize $Z(S)\supseteq T$} Z(S)
    =\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize $S\subseteq I(T)$} Z(S)
    =Z\left(\sum_\text{\scriptsize $S\subseteq I(T)$}(S)\right)
    =Z(I(T)).$$
:::

Thus we obtain the following.

::: Theorem 15
There is a Galois correspondence between the radical ideals of a ring $$A$$ and the closed sets of $$\Spec A$$.
:::

What can be somewhat confusing in this correspondence is that any prime ideal of $$A$$ is always a radical ideal, so the collection of radical ideals of a ring $$A$$ always contains (as a set) $$\Spec A$$.

::: Proposition 16
There is a Galois correspondence between the prime ideals of a ring $$A$$ and the irreducible closed subsets of $$\Spec A$$.
:::
::: Proof
That is, we must show that for any prime ideal $$\mathfrak{p}$$, $$Z(\mathfrak{p})$$ is irreducible, and that for any irreducible closed subset $$Y$$, $$I(Y)$$ is a prime ideal.

First assume $$Z(\mathfrak{p})$$ is not irreducible. Then there exist two proper closed subsets $$Z(\mathfrak{a}), Z(\mathfrak{b})$$ of $$Z(\mathfrak{p})$$ such that $$Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})$$. ([\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/topology/dimension#def6)) Now

$$Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})=Z(\mathfrak{ab})\iff \mathfrak{p}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{ab}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}}$$

holds. Meanwhile

$$\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$$

so combining the above two equations we know that $$\mathfrak{p}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}$$ or $$\mathfrak{p}\supseteq \mathfrak{b}$$ must hold, and therefore $$Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})$$ or $$Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{b})$$, a contradiction.

Conversely, for any irreducible closed subset $$Y$$, we must show that $$I(Y)$$ is a prime ideal. First, since $$Y$$ is a closed set, by [Theorem 15](#thm15) and [Proposition 5](#prop5) there exists a radical ideal $$\mathfrak{a}$$ such that $$Y=Z(\mathfrak{a})$$. Then it suffices to show that $$\mathfrak{a}=IZ(\mathfrak{a})=I(Y)$$ is prime.

For this, suppose $$fg\in \mathfrak{a}$$, and consider the two nonempty open sets $$D(f), D(g)$$ of $$\Spec A$$. Then

$$(D(f)\cap Y)\cap (D(g)\cap Y)=D(f)\cap D(g)\cap Y=D(fg)\cap Y$$

must be empty. But $$D(f)\cap Y$$ and $$D(g)\cap Y$$ are two open sets of the irreducible closed set $$Y$$, so for this to hold one of them must be empty. ([\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/topology/dimension#prop7)) From this we know that $$f\in \mathfrak{a}$$ or $$g\in \mathfrak{a}$$ must hold, and therefore $$\mathfrak{a}$$ is a prime ideal.
:::

In particular, if for a prime ideal $$\mathfrak{p}$$ there is no prime ideal $$\mathfrak{q}\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}$$, that is, if $$\mathfrak{p}$$ is a *minimal* prime ideal, then $$Z(\mathfrak{p})$$ is the largest among the irreducible components, that is, an irreducible component. From this we obtain the following.

::: Corollary 17
There is a Galois correspondence between the minimal prime ideals of a ring $$A$$ and the irreducible components of $$\Spec A$$.
:::
::: Proof
By [Proposition 14](#prop14), for any prime ideal $$\mathfrak{p}$$ we have $$IZ(\mathfrak{p})=\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{p}$$, and for any closed set $$Y$$ of $$\Spec A$$ we have $$ZI(Y)=\cl(Y)=Y$$, so $$Z$$ and $$I$$ are mutually inverse correspondences. Therefore, by [Proposition 16](#prop16), $$\mathfrak{p}\mapsto Z(\mathfrak{p})$$ is a bijection between the prime ideals of $$A$$ and the irreducible closed subsets of $$\Spec A$$, and its inverse is $$Y\mapsto I(Y)$$. Moreover, applying the third result of [Lemma 6](#lem6) to two prime ideals $$\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}$$,

$$Z(\mathfrak{q})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{p})\iff \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}\supseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}\iff \mathfrak{q}\supseteq \mathfrak{p}$$

so this bijection reverses inclusion.

Meanwhile, by [\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Definition 9](/en/math/topology/dimension#def9), an irreducible component of $$\Spec A$$ is a maximal irreducible subset, and the closure of an irreducible set is again irreducible, so they are always closed sets. Conversely, given an irreducible closed subset $$Y$$ that is maximal among irreducible closed subsets, and considering any irreducible subset $$S$$ containing $$Y$$, then $$\cl(S)$$ is an irreducible closed subset containing $$Y$$, so by the maximality of $$Y$$ we have $$\cl(S)=Y$$, and therefore $$S\subseteq Y$$, i.e. $$S=Y$$. From the above, the irreducible components of $$\Spec A$$ are exactly the maximal irreducible closed subsets with respect to inclusion.

Now since the above bijection reverses inclusion, it corresponds the minimal ones among prime ideals, i.e. minimal prime ideals, and the maximal ones among irreducible closed subsets, i.e. the irreducible components of $$\Spec A$$, to each other.
:::
