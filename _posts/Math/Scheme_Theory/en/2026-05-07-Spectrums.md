::: remark Remark {#rmk}
In all posts in this category, a ring means a commutative ring (with unity).
:::

In this post we define the spectrum, the most fundamental object in algebraic geometry. The spectrum is a topological space equipped with a suitable structure sheaf; here we first define it as a set and then put a topology on it. In the next post, we will define the structure sheaf on $\Spec A$.

## $\Spec A$ as a set

::: Definition 1
For a ring $A$, $\Spec A$ is the set of all prime ideals of $A$, called the *spectrum* of $A$.
:::

Now suppose a ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow B$ is given. Then by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#prop9), the map

$$\Spec\phi: \Spec B \rightarrow \Spec A;\qquad \mathfrak{q}\mapsto \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})$$

is well-defined.

::: Proposition 2
The $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Set$ defined above is a functor.
:::

That is, $\Spec(\phi\circ\psi)=(\Spec\psi)\circ(\Spec\phi)$ and $\Spec(\id_A)=\id_{\Spec A}$, and neither is difficult to prove.

## $\Spec A$ as a topological space

Now let us define an appropriate topology on $\Spec A$.

::: Definition 3
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any nonempty subset $S$ of $A$, we define the subset $Z(S)$ of $\Spec A$ by

$$Z(S)=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}.$$
:::

Then several facts are easily proved. First, $Z$ is inclusion-reversing: if $S_1\subseteq S_2$ are subsets of $A$, then

$$Z(S_1)=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S_1\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}\supseteq \{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A\mid S_2\subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}=Z(S_2).$$

However, this inclusion need not be strict.

::: Proposition 4
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any subset $S$ of $A$ and the ideal $(S)$ generated by $S$, we have $Z(S)=Z((S))$.
:::
::: Proof
Since $S\subseteq (S)$ trivially, the inclusion $Z((S))\subseteq Z(S)$ is clear from the preceding discussion. Thus it suffices to prove the reverse direction. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be an arbitrary element of $Z(S)$, i.e. $S\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$. But the ideal generated by the subset $\mathfrak{p}$ of $A$ is $\mathfrak{p}$ itself, so $(S)\subseteq (\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{p}$.
:::

Moreover, even for two *ideals* $\mathfrak{a}_1\subseteq \mathfrak{a}_2$ of $A$, the inclusion $Z(\mathfrak{a}_1)\supseteq Z(\mathfrak{a}_2)$ may be an equality.

::: Proposition 5
Fix a ring $A$ and its spectrum $\Spec A$. For any ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$ and its radical $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{a})=Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})$.
:::
::: Proof
Again, the inclusion $Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{a})$ is obvious from $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$. Conversely, for any $\mathfrak{p}\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$, using [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8) we obtain

$$\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{q}$ a prime containing $\mathfrak{a}$}\mathfrak{q}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$$

and thus $\mathfrak{p}\in Z(\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}})$.
:::

The most important ingredient for defining a topology on $\Spec A$ is the following lemma.

::: Lemma 6
The following hold.

1. For any ideals $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{ab})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})$.
2. For any collection of ideals $\{\mathfrak{a}_i\}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\sum \mathfrak{a}_i)=\bigcap Z(\mathfrak{a}_i)$.
3. For any ideals $\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}$ of $A$, we have $Z(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{b})\iff \sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}\supseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{b}}$.
:::
::: Proof
1. It is obvious that a prime ideal containing $\mathfrak{a}$ or $\mathfrak{b}$ also contains the smaller ideal $\mathfrak{ab}$, so it suffices to show the reverse inclusion. Suppose $\mathfrak{p}\supset \mathfrak{ab}$. If $\mathfrak{p}\not\supseteq \mathfrak{b}$, then we can find an element $b\in\mathfrak{b}$ with $b\not\in \mathfrak{p}$. On the other hand, for any $a\in \mathfrak{a}$, we have $ab\in \mathfrak{ab}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, and since $b\not\in \mathfrak{p}$ by the preceding assumption, necessarily $a\in \mathfrak{p}$; therefore $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$.
2. This is obvious because $\sum \mathfrak{a}_i$ is defined as the smallest ideal containing all the $\mathfrak{a}_i$.
3. [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Properties of Localization, ⁋Corollary 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#cor8).
:::

Then by [\[Topology\] §Interior, Closure, Boundary, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/topology/other_concepts#prop2), there exists a unique topology $\mathcal{T}$ having the $Z(\mathfrak{a})$ as closed sets, which makes $\Spec A$ a topological space.

::: Definition 7
The topology on $\Spec A$ defined above is called the *Zariski topology*.
:::

The Zariski topology is generally not a topology we would find pleasant. For example, for any integral domain $A$, the zero ideal $(0)$ is itself prime, and the only nonempty subset of $A$ contained in it is $(0)$ itself. Thus the only closed set containing $(0)\in\Spec A$ is $Z(0)=\Spec A$, and therefore unless $A$ is a field, the singleton $\{(0)\}$ is not closed. In particular, the Zariski topology is generally not Hausdorff. However, as we proceed through the posts in this category, we will discover familiar geometric concepts in this structure.

Earlier, in [Proposition 2](#prop2), we saw that $\Spec$ can be regarded as a functor $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Set$. Moreover, $\Spec$ is also a functor from $\cRing^\op$ to $\Top$.

::: Proposition 8
If we endow the spectrum $\Spec A$ of a ring $A$ with the topological structure of [Definition 7](#def7), then the functor $\Spec: \cRing^\op \rightarrow \Top$ of [Proposition 2](#prop2) is a functor.
:::
::: Proof
In addition to [Proposition 2](#prop2), what we need to show is that for any ring homomorphism $\phi: A \rightarrow B$, the map $\Spec \phi: \Spec B \rightarrow \Spec A$ is continuous. Thus it suffices to show that for any closed set in $\Spec A$, its preimage under $\Spec\phi$ is also closed in $\Spec B$. ([\[Topology\] §Interior, Closure, Boundary, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/topology/other_concepts#prop2))

Now, any closed set in $\Spec A$ is of the form $Z(\mathfrak{a})$, and any closed set in $\Spec B$ is of the form $Z(\mathfrak{b})$, so to show this it suffices to show that for any ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$, there exists an ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ of $B$ satisfying

$$(\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))=Z(\mathfrak{b}).$$

Our claim is that

$$(\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))=Z(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))$$

holds. Then the ideal generated by $\phi(\mathfrak{a})$ satisfies the above equation, completing the proof.

First, suppose $\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec B$ lies in the left-hand side. That is, $(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q})=\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$. Then from $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})$ we obtain $\phi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$, so $\mathfrak{q}\in Z(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))$.

Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec B$ lies in the right-hand side. Then from $\phi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$ we obtain the inclusion

$$\mathfrak{a}\subseteq \phi^{-1}(\phi(\mathfrak{a}))\subseteq\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})=(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q}),$$

and this proves that $(\Spec\phi)(\mathfrak{q})\in Z(\mathfrak{a})$, i.e. $\mathfrak{q}\in (\Spec\phi)^{-1}(Z(\mathfrak{a}))$.
:::

The two important ring homomorphisms we will use are the quotient $\pi:A \rightarrow A/\mathfrak{a}$ and the localization $\epsilon: A \rightarrow S^{-1}A$. ([\[Commutative Algebra\] §Basic Notions, ⁋Proposition 11](/en/math/commutative_algebra/basic_notions#prop11) and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8))

::: Proposition 9
For the $\pi:A \rightarrow A/\mathfrak{a}$ and $\epsilon: A \rightarrow S^{-1}A$ defined above, the following hold.

1. $\Spec\pi$ and $\Spec\epsilon$ are both injective, and they define homeomorphisms onto their respective images.
2. The image of $\Spec\pi$ in $\Spec A$ is closed.
3. If $S=\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$ for some $f\in A$, then the image of $\Spec \epsilon$ in $\Spec A$ is open.
:::
::: Proof
The first statement follows from the two propositions [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Basic Notions, ⁋Proposition 11](/en/math/commutative_algebra/basic_notions#prop11) and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8) mentioned above. For the second, the image of $\Spec\pi$ in $\Spec A$ is exactly $Z(\mathfrak{a})$. Finally, for the third, the elements in the image of $\Spec\epsilon$ are the prime ideals not containing $f$, and these can be written as $\Spec A\setminus Z(f)$.
:::

That a base exists for $\Spec A$ as a topological space is obvious, but we would like this base to be related in some way to the algebraic structure of $A$. Let us define the following.

::: Definition 10
For any element $f\in A$ of a ring $A$, we write $D(f)$ for the complement of $Z(f)$ in $\Spec A$, i.e. $\Spec A\setminus Z(f)$. An open set of this form is called a *principal open set*.
:::

Now for any $f\in A$, let $S_f=\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$ and define $A_f=S_f^{-1}A$. Then by [Proposition 9](#prop9), the image of $\Spec A_f$ under $\Spec \epsilon$ in $\Spec A$ is open. That this open set coincides with $D(f)$ as a set follows from

$$\mathfrak{p}\not\in Z(f)\iff (f)\not\subseteq \mathfrak{p} \iff f\not\in \mathfrak{p}\iff f^k\not\in \mathfrak{p}\text{ for all $k\geq 0$}\iff S_f\cap \mathfrak{p}=\emptyset$$

and [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8). Moreover, that $D(f)$ carries the same topology as $\Spec A_f$ also follows from the fact that the correspondence in [\[Commutative Algebra\] §Localization, ⁋Proposition 8](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#prop8) preserves inclusion.

::: Lemma 11
The collection of principal open sets forms a base for $\Spec A$. ([\[Topology\] §Bases of Topological Spaces, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/topological_bases#def1))
:::
::: Proof
For any open set $\Spec A \setminus Z(S)$, using the second statement of [Lemma 6](#lem6) we have the computation

$$\Spec A\setminus Z(S)=\Spec A\setminus Z\left(\sum_{f\in S} (f)\right)=\Spec A\setminus\left(\bigcap_{f\in S}Z(f)\right)=\bigcup_{f\in S} (\Spec A\setminus Z(f))=\bigcup_{f\in S} D(f).$$
:::

Then by a computation similar to that of this lemma, we can check that $D(fg)=D(f)\cap D(g)$. However, since this computation uses the first statement of [Lemma 6](#lem6), it cannot in general be extended to infinite indices.

Separately, we can show that $\Spec A$ always satisfies the condition of [\[Topology\] §Compact Spaces, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/compact_spaces#def1). However, many properties we usually expect of a compact space require the Hausdorff condition as well ([\[Topology\] §Compact Spaces, §§Compact Hausdorff Spaces](/en/math/topology/compact_spaces#compact-hausdorff-spaces)), and since the Zariski topology is generally not Hausdorff, we call it *quasi-compact*.

::: Lemma 12
$\Spec A$ is a quasi-compact space.
:::
::: Proof
By [Lemma 11](#lem11), it suffices to show that whenever a collection of principal open sets $\{D(f_i)\}_{i\in I}$ covers $\Spec A$, there exists a finite subset $J\subseteq I$ such that $\Spec A=\bigcup_{j\in J} D(f_j)$.

From the assumption $\Spec A=\bigcup_{i\in I} D(f_i)$ we have

$$\emptyset=\Spec A\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I} D(f_i)=\bigcap_{i\in I}(\Spec A\setminus D(f_i))=\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i).$$

On the other hand, by [Lemma 6](#lem6)

$$\emptyset=\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i)=Z\left(\sum_{i\in I}(f_i)\right),$$

and if $\sum (f_i)$ were a proper ideal of $A$, then a maximal ideal containing it would lie in $\bigcap_{i\in I} Z(f_i)$; thus the above equation is equivalent to $\sum(f_i)=(1)$. Since $1\in\sum(f_i)$, there exist a finite set $J\subseteq I$ and a family of elements $(a_j)_{j\in J}$ of $A$ such that $\sum_{j\in J} a_jf_j=1$. Hence

$$Z\left(\sum_{j\in J} (f_j)\right)=\emptyset,$$

and reversing the above computations yields the desired result.
:::

## Galois correspondence

By definition, $Z$ is a function that takes an ideal of $A$ to a closed subset of $\Spec A$. Conversely, we define the following.

::: Definition 13
For any subset $T\subseteq \Spec A$,

$$I(T)=\{f\in A\mid\text{$f\in \mathfrak{p}$ for all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime in $T$} \mathfrak{p}$$

is defined.
:::

Then $I(T)$ is an ideal, being an intersection of ideals. Moreover, if $T_1\subseteq T_2$ then $I(T_2)\subseteq I(T_1)$ is obvious.

Now combining [Definition 3](#def3) and [Definition 13](#def13), we have defined two functions between the ordered sets $\mathcal{P}(A)$ and $\mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$:

$$Z: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A);\quad S\mapsto Z(S),\qquad I: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A);\quad T\mapsto I(T).$$

Then for any $S\in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and any $T\in \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$,

$$T\subseteq Z(S)\iff\text{$\mathfrak{p}\in Z(S)$ for all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\iff\text{$f\in \mathfrak{p}$ for all $f\in S$ and all $\mathfrak{p}\in T$}\iff S\subseteq I(T),$$

so $(Z, I)$ defines an antitone Galois connection between $\mathcal{P}(A)$ and $\mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$. ([\[Set Theory\] §Filters and Ideals, Galois Correspondence, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/set_theory/filter_and_ideal#def6)) Therefore, the following two identities

$$Z(I(Z(S)))=Z(S),\qquad I(Z(I(T)))=I(T)$$

hold for all $S\in \mathcal{P}(A)$ and all $T\in \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$.

Now consider the two closure operators $IZ: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$ and $ZI: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$.

::: Proposition 14
For the closure operators $IZ: \mathcal{P}(A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$ and $ZI: \mathcal{P}(\Spec A) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Spec A)$, the following hold.

1. $$IZ(S)=\sqrt{(S)}$$
2. $ZI(T)=\cl(T)$
:::
::: Proof
1. This is obvious from the formula
    
    $$I(Z(S))=I(Z((S)))=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime in $Z((S))$} \mathfrak{p}=\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize$\mathfrak{p}$ a prime containing $(S)$} \mathfrak{p}=\sqrt{(S)}.$$
2. This is obvious from the formula
    
    $$\cl(T)
    =\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize $Z(S)\supseteq T$} Z(S)
    =\bigcap_\text{\scriptsize $S\subseteq I(T)$} Z(S)
    =Z\left(\sum_\text{\scriptsize $S\subseteq I(T)$}(S)\right)
    =Z(I(T)).$$
:::

Thus we obtain the following.

::: Theorem 15
There is a Galois correspondence between the radical ideals of a ring $A$ and the closed subsets of $\Spec A$.
:::

What can be somewhat confusing in this correspondence is that any prime ideal of $A$ is always a radical ideal, so the collection of radical ideals of $A$ always contains (as a set) $\Spec A$.

::: Proposition 16
There is a Galois correspondence between the prime ideals of a ring $A$ and the irreducible closed subsets of $\Spec A$.
:::
::: Proof
That is, we must show that for any prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, the set $Z(\mathfrak{p})$ is irreducible, and that for any irreducible closed subset $Y$, the ideal $I(Y)$ is prime.

First, suppose $Z(\mathfrak{p})$ is not irreducible. Then there exist two proper closed subsets $Z(\mathfrak{a}), Z(\mathfrak{b})$ of $Z(\mathfrak{p})$ such that $Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})$. ([\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Definition 6](/en/math/topology/dimension#def6)) Now

$$Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})\cup Z(\mathfrak{b})=Z(\mathfrak{ab})\iff \mathfrak{p}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{ab}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}}$$

holds. On the other hand,

$$\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}\cap \mathfrak{b}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$$

so combining the above two formulas we see that $\mathfrak{p}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}$ or $\mathfrak{p}\supseteq \mathfrak{b}$ must hold, and therefore $Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{a})$ or $Z(\mathfrak{p})=Z(\mathfrak{b})$, a contradiction.

Conversely, for any irreducible closed subset $Y$, we must show that $I(Y)$ is a prime ideal. First, since $Y$ is closed, by [Theorem 15](#thm15) and [Proposition 5](#prop5) there exists a radical ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ such that $Y=Z(\mathfrak{a})$. Then it suffices to show that $\mathfrak{a}=IZ(\mathfrak{a})=I(Y)$ is prime.

To this end, suppose $fg\in \mathfrak{a}$, and consider the two nonempty open sets $D(f), D(g)$ of $\Spec A$; then

$$(D(f)\cap Y)\cap (D(g)\cap Y)=D(f)\cap D(g)\cap Y=D(fg)\cap Y$$

must be empty. But $D(f)\cap Y$ and $D(g)\cap Y$ are two open subsets of the irreducible closed set $Y$, so for this to hold one of them must be empty. ([\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Proposition 7](/en/math/topology/dimension#prop7)) From this we see that $f\in \mathfrak{a}$ or $g\in \mathfrak{a}$ must hold, and therefore $\mathfrak{a}$ is a prime ideal.
:::

In particular, if for a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ there is no prime ideal $\mathfrak{q}\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}$, i.e. if $\mathfrak{p}$ is a *minimal* prime ideal, then $Z(\mathfrak{p})$ is the largest among the irreducible components, that is, an irreducible component itself. From this we obtain the following.

::: Corollary 17
There is a Galois correspondence between the minimal prime ideals of a ring $A$ and the irreducible components of $\Spec A$.
:::
::: Proof
By [Proposition 14](#prop14), for any prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ we have $IZ(\mathfrak{p})=\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{p}$, and for any closed subset $Y$ of $\Spec A$ we have $ZI(Y)=\cl(Y)=Y$, so $Z$ and $I$ are mutually inverse. Therefore, by [Proposition 16](#prop16), $\mathfrak{p}\mapsto Z(\mathfrak{p})$ is a bijection between the prime ideals of $A$ and the irreducible closed subsets of $\Spec A$, and its inverse is $Y\mapsto I(Y)$. Moreover, applying the third statement of [Lemma 6](#lem6) to two prime ideals $\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}$ gives

$$Z(\mathfrak{q})\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{p})\iff \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}\supseteq \sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}\iff \mathfrak{q}\supseteq \mathfrak{p}$$

so this bijection reverses inclusion.

On the other hand, by [\[Topology\] §Dimension, ⁋Definition 9](/en/math/topology/dimension#def9), the irreducible components of $\Spec A$ are the maximal ones among the irreducible subsets, and since the closure of an irreducible set is again irreducible, they are always closed. Conversely, suppose a maximal irreducible closed subset $Y$ is given, and consider any irreducible subset $S$ containing $Y$. Then $\cl(S)$ is an irreducible closed subset containing $Y$, so by the maximality of $Y$ we have $\cl(S)=Y$, and therefore $S\subseteq Y$, i.e. $S=Y$. Thus the irreducible components of $\Spec A$ are exactly the maximal elements among the irreducible closed subsets under inclusion.

Now since the above bijection reverses inclusion, it pairs the minimal ones among the prime ideals, i.e. the minimal prime ideals, with the maximal ones among the irreducible closed subsets, i.e. the irreducible components of $\Spec A$.
:::
