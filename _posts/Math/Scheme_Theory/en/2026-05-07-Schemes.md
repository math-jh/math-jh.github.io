## Definition of Schemes

We are now in a position to define what a scheme is.

::: Definition 1
A locally ringed space $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ is called a *scheme* if for every $x\in X$, there exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ such that $(U, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_U)$ is an affine scheme. The notions of morphisms and isomorphisms between schemes are defined as those of locally ringed spaces.
:::

Given an arbitrary point $x\in X$ of a scheme $X$, choose an affine open neighborhood $U$ of $x$. Then by definition there exists a suitable ring $A$ such that $(U, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_U)\cong (\Spec A, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A})$ as locally ringed spaces. In particular, letting $\mathfrak{p}_x$ denote the point of $\Spec A$ corresponding to $x\in U$ via the above isomorphism, we know that

$$\mathcal{O}_{X,x}=\varinjlim_{V\ni x} \mathcal{O}_X(V)=\varinjlim_{U\supseteq V\ni x} \mathcal{O}_X(V)=\mathcal{O}_{U, x}\cong \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A, \mathfrak{p}_x}\tag{$\ast$}$$

In general, the restriction $(U, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}\vert_U)$ of an affine scheme $(\Spec A, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A})$ to an arbitrary open subset is not always an affine scheme. ([Example 8](#ex8)) However, the principal open subset $D(f)$ of an affine scheme $\Spec A$ is always an affine scheme via the isomorphism

$$(D(f), \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}\vert_{D(f)})\cong (\Spec A_f, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f})\tag{$\ast\ast$}$$

::: Lemma 2
For an affine scheme $\Spec A$ and $f\in A$, the set $D(f)$ is always an affine scheme via the above isomorphism ($\ast\ast$).
:::
::: Proof
We already know from the third result of [[Spectrums] §Spectrums, ⁋Proposition 9](/en/math/scheme_theory/spectrums#prop9) that the injective continuous map $\Spec\epsilon: \Spec A_f \rightarrow A$ between topological spaces gives a homeomorphism $D(f)\cong \Spec A_f$, but to justify the above isomorphism we must justify this as a scheme morphism.

First, the ring homomorphism $\epsilon: A \rightarrow A_f$ induces a scheme morphism

$$(\Spec \epsilon, (\Spec\epsilon)^\sharp): (\Spec A_f, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f}) \rightarrow (\Spec A, \mathcal{O}_A)$$

and in this case the homeomorphism between $D(f)$ and $\Spec A_f$ comes from the following decomposition, considering the canonical inclusion $\iota: D(f)\hookrightarrow \Spec A$:

![decomposition](/assets/images/Math/Scheme_Theory/Schemes-1.svg){:style="width:10.60em" class="invert" .align-center}

Now, for the above isomorphism ($\ast\ast$) to be an isomorphism between schemes, we must define the morphism $(\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})^\sharp$ in $\Sh(D(f); \cRing)$ corresponding to the homeomorphism $\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)}$. Then by [[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Example 12](/en/math/topology/sheaves#ex12),

$$(\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})^\sharp: \mathcal{O}_{D(f)} \rightarrow (\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f}$$

defining this is exactly the same as defining

$$(\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})^{\sharp}: \iota^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A} \rightarrow (\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f}$$

and again by [[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Lemma 11](/en/math/topology/sheaves#lem11),

$$\begin{aligned}\Hom_{\Sh(D(f); \cRing)}(\iota^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}, (\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})_\ast\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f})&\cong \Hom_{\Sh(\Spec A; \cRing)}(\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}, \iota_\ast(\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f})\\&\cong \Hom_{\Sh(\Spec A; \cRing)}(\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}, (\iota\circ\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f})\\&=\Hom_{\Sh(\Spec A; \cRing)}(\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A}, (\Spec\epsilon)_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f})\end{aligned}$$

so we can define $(\Spec\epsilon\vert^{D(f)})^\sharp$ via $(\Spec\epsilon)^\sharp:\mathcal{O}_{\Spec A} \rightarrow (\Spec \epsilon)_\ast \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A_f}$, and that this is an isomorphism can be seen by following the proof of the corresponding lemma, essentially because $\Spec A_f$ is an open subset of $\Spec A$.
:::

In particular, by definition a scheme can be covered by affine schemes, and since these have a base consisting of principal open sets, any scheme has a base consisting of affine open subsets.

The following lemma shows, by a similar argument, that any open subset of a scheme is always a scheme.

::: Lemma 3
Let $U$ be an arbitrary open subset of a scheme $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$. Then $(U, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_U)$ is also a scheme.
:::
::: Proof
Let $x$ be an arbitrary point of the open subset $U$. Since $X$ is a scheme, there exist a suitable open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ (in $X$) and a ring $A$ such that $(V, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_V)\cong(\Spec A, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A})$. Also, since $U$ is open, $U\cap V$ is an open subset of $V$. Now by [[Spectrums] §Spectrums, ⁋Lemma 11](/en/math/scheme_theory/spectrums#lem11), there exist $f_i\in A$ satisfying

$$U\cap V=\bigcup_{i\in I} D(f_i)\qquad\text{in $\Spec A$}$$

and thus $x\in D(f_i)$ for some $i$. Now $(D(f_i), \mathcal{O}_X\vert_{D(f_i)})$ is an affine scheme and $x\in D(f_i)\subseteq V$, so we obtain the desired result.
:::

::: Definition 4
For any open subset $U$ of a scheme $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$, we call the scheme $(U, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_U)$ an *open subscheme* of $X$.
:::

## Algebraic Functions Defined on Schemes

In [[Affine Schemes] §§Algebraic Functions Defined on $\Spec A$](/en/math/scheme_theory/affine_schemes#spec-a-위에-정의된-대수적인-함수들), we decided to think of the value of a function $f\in A$ at an arbitrary point $\mathfrak{p}$ as the image of $f$ in $A/\mathfrak{p}$. Applying this directly to a general scheme $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ is somewhat tricky, not only because the above process is unclear in $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$, but also because even if we repeat the above discussion using an affine open neighborhood of $x\in X$, it is not obvious that this is independent of the choice of affine open neighborhood.

To resolve this, we use a slightly more clever approach and make the following definition.

::: Definition 5
Consider an arbitrary point $x\in X$ of a scheme $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ and the local ring $(\mathcal{O}_{X, x}, \mathfrak{m}_x)$. Then the *residue field* of $X$ at $x$ is defined as $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}/\mathfrak{m}_x$, and we denote this by $\kappa(x)$. For a function $f\in \mathcal{O}_X(U)$ defined on an open subset $U$ containing the point $x$, the *value* of $f$ at $x$ is defined as the image of $f$ in $\kappa(x)$.
:::

Let $x\in X$ be an arbitrary point of a scheme $X$, and choose an affine open neighborhood $(U, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_U)\cong(\Spec A, \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A})$ of $x$. Then, if we write $\mathfrak{p}_x$ for the point of $\Spec A$ corresponding to $x\in U$, we checked in the previous isomorphism ($\ast$) that $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\cong \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A, \mathfrak{p}_x}$, and then from [[Affine Schemes] §Affine Schemes, ⁋Lemma 8](/en/math/scheme_theory/affine_schemes#lem8) we obtain the isomorphism

$$\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\cong \mathcal{O}_{\Spec A, \mathfrak{p}_x}\cong A_{\mathfrak{p}_x}$$

and in particular this isomorphism corresponds the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}_xA_{\mathfrak{p}_x}$ of the local ring $A_{\mathfrak{p}_x}$ with $\mathfrak{m}_x$. Therefore, thinking of the definition from [[Commutative Algebra] §Localization, ⁋Definition 10](/en/math/commutative_algebra/localization#def10), we obtain

$$\kappa(x)=\mathcal{O}_{X,x}/\mathfrak{m}_x\cong A_{\mathfrak{p}_x}/\mathfrak{p}_xA_{\mathfrak{p}_x}=\kappa(\mathfrak{p}_x)$$

On the other hand, since localization and quotient commute ([[Commutative Algebra] §Properties of Localization, ⁋Proposition 2](/en/math/commutative_algebra/properties_of_localization#prop2)), we obtain

$$\kappa(x)\cong A_{\mathfrak{p}_x}/\mathfrak{p}_xA_{\mathfrak{p}_x}\cong \Frac(A/\mathfrak{p}_x)$$

and thus we see that [Definition 5](#def5) generalizes the notion of function values on affine schemes well. Then from this expression, for arbitrary $f\in \mathcal{O}_X(X)$ we define

$$X_f=\{x\in X\mid\text{$f_x\neq 0$ in $\kappa(x)$}\}=\{x\in X\mid f_x\not\in \mathfrak{m}_x\}$$

Then for any $x\in X_f$, choosing an affine open neighborhood $U=\Spec A$ containing $x$, we know from the above discussion that $U\cap X_f$ equals the open set $D(f\vert_{U\cap X_f})$, and therefore by [[Spectrums] §Spectrums, ⁋Lemma 11](/en/math/scheme_theory/spectrums#lem11) we know that $X_f$ is an open subset of $X$.

On the other hand, at a point $x\in X$ a function $f$ can be examined not only by its value but also by its germ. Let us make the following definition.

::: Definition 6
For a scheme $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ and $f\in \mathcal{O}_X(X)$, the *support* of $f$ is given by the following formula:

$$\supp(f)=\{x\in X\mid f_x\neq 0\text{ in $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$}\}$$

Here $f_x$ denotes the stalk of $f$ at $x$.
:::

Then if the stalk of $f$ at $x\in X$ is zero, we can choose a suitable open neighborhood $U=\Spec A$ of $x$ such that $f$ is identically zero on $U$, so we know that $\supp(f)$ is a closed subset of $X$. On the other hand, if $f_x=0$ in $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ then it is trivial that $f_x$ is zero in $\kappa(x)$, so the following inclusion

$$X\setminus \supp(f)\subseteq X\setminus X_f\iff X_f\subseteq \supp(f)$$

holds.

## Examples of Schemes

For convenience, we shall henceforth omit the structure sheaf of a scheme and write simply $X$ instead of $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$, and denote a scheme morphism simply by $\varphi$ instead of $(\varphi, \varphi^\sharp)$.

::: Example 7
In [[Spectrums] §§Classical Algebraic Geometry](/en/math/scheme_theory/spectrums#고전적인-대수기하학), we defined the (classical) affine $n$-space as

$$\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K},\mathrm{classical}}^n=\{(x_1,\ldots, x_n)\mid x_i\in \mathbb{K}\}=\MaxSpec \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$$

Generalizing this, we shall henceforth define the affine $n$-space over $\mathbb{K}$ as

$$\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^n=\Spec \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$$

More generally, replacing the field $\mathbb{K}$ by an arbitrary ring $A$, we obtain the affine $n$-space over $A$

$$\mathbb{A}_A^n=\Spec A[\x_1,\ldots, \x_n]$$

The passage from the maximal spectrum $\MaxSpec A$ to $\Spec A$ not only makes algebra easier, but also has geometric meaning. Now each point of $\mathbb{A}^n$ means not only the points of the $n$-space $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K},\mathrm{classical}}^n$ in the traditional sense, but also the closed subsets appearing as zero loci of functions on this space are treated as points.
:::

Earlier we mentioned that an open subset of an affine scheme need not be an affine scheme, and the following example illustrates this.

::: Example 8
Consider the affine plane $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2=\Spec \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]$. Then

$$\{(0,0)\}=Z(\x_1)\cap Z(\x_2)$$

so $\{(0,0)\}$ is a closed set, and therefore

$$U=\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2\setminus\{0\}=D(\x_1)\cup D(\x_2)$$

is an open set. On the other hand, functions defined on $D(\x_1)$ and $D(\x_2)$ are of the form

$$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(D(\x_1))\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]_{\x_1}=\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2, \x_1^{-1}],\qquad \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(D(\x_2))\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]_{\x_2}=\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2, \x_2^{-1}]$$

Now functions on $D(\x_1)\cup D(\x_2)$ are those obtained by gluing these together, and such functions must agree when restricted to the intersection $D(\x_1)\cap D(\x_2)=D(\x_1\x_2)$. Now comparing elements of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(D(\x_1))$ and $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(D(\x_2))$ in

$$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(D(\x_1\x_2))=\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]_{\x_1\x_2}$$

we see that these functions are only polynomial functions. That is,

$$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}(U)=\mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]$$

If $(U, \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^2}\vert_U)$ were affine, then $U$ would have to be isomorphic to $\Spec \mathbb{K}[\x_1,\x_2]$ obtained by taking the global sections, but considering the point in $U$ corresponding to the prime ideal $(\x_1,\x_2)$,

$$Z(\x_1,\x_2)=Z(\x_1)\cap Z(\x_2)=\emptyset\qquad\text{ in $U$}$$

so this is impossible.
:::

By definition a scheme is made by gluing affine schemes, and in the above example we also understood the sections on $U$ by viewing it as obtained by gluing $D(\x_1)$ and $D(\x_2)$. Before looking at more examples, it will be helpful to first examine the following lemma for dealing with various examples.

::: Lemma 9
Fix an index set $I$, and suppose the following data are given:

- Schemes $X_i$,
- Open subschemes $X_{ij}$ of the $X_i$,
- For each $i,j\in I$, isomorphisms $\varphi_{ij}:X_{ij} \rightarrow X_{ji}$

Here we define $X_{ii}=X_i$ and $\varphi_{ii}=\id_{X_i}$. Then if these data satisfy the *cocycle condition*

$$\varphi_{ik}\vert_{X_{ij}\cap X_{ik}}=\varphi_{jk}\vert_{X_{ji}\cap X_{jk}}\circ \varphi_{ij}\vert_{X_{ij}\cap X_{ik}}\qquad\text{for all $i,j,k\in I$}$$

there exists a unique scheme $X$ and open subschemes $U_i$ of $X$ such that $U_i\cong X_i$ and $U_i\cap U_j\cong X_{ij}$.
:::
::: Proof
First, for the composition on the right-hand side of the cocycle condition to be defined, we must have $\varphi_{ij}(X_{ij}\cap X_{ik})\subseteq X_{ji}\cap X_{jk}$, which we understand as part of the given data. Now the cocycle condition for the case $k=i$ gives, from $X_{ii}=X_i$ and $\varphi_{ii}=\id_{X_i}$,

$$\id_{X_{ij}}=\varphi_{ji}\circ\varphi_{ij}$$

so $\varphi_{ji}=\varphi_{ij}^{-1}$, and thus applying the above inclusion to $(i,j,k)$ and $(j,i,k)$ respectively we obtain

$$\varphi_{ij}(X_{ij}\cap X_{ik})=X_{ji}\cap X_{jk}$$

Now on the set $\coprod_{i\in I} X_i$, define a relation $\sim$ for $x\in X_i$ and $y\in X_j$ by

$$x\sim y\iff \text{$x\in X_{ij}$ and $\varphi_{ij}(x)=y$}$$

Then from $X_{ii}=X_i$ and $\varphi_{ii}=\id_{X_i}$, the relation $\sim$ is reflexive, and from $\varphi_{ji}=\varphi_{ij}^{-1}$ it is symmetric. To show transitivity, suppose $x\in X_i$, $y=\varphi_{ij}(x)$, and $z=\varphi_{jk}(y)$ are given. Then $y\in X_{ji}\cap X_{jk}$, so by the above equality $x\in X_{ij}\cap X_{ik}$, and by the cocycle condition

$$z=\varphi_{jk}(\varphi_{ij}(x))=\varphi_{ik}(x)$$

so $x\sim z$. That is, $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

Now consider the set $X=\coprod_{i\in I}X_i\big/{\sim}$ and the canonical maps $u_i: X_i \rightarrow X$, and give $X$ the final topology with respect to the $u_i$. ([[Topology] §Initial and Final Topology, ⁋Definition 4](/en/math/topology/initial_and_final_topology#def4)) Then by [[Topology] §Initial and Final Topology, ⁋Proposition 5](/en/math/topology/initial_and_final_topology#prop5), $U\subseteq X$ being open is equivalent to $u_i^{-1}(U)$ being open in $X_i$ for all $i$, which is precisely the quotient space topology on $\coprod_{i\in I} X_i$. ([[Topology] §Quotient Spaces, ⁋Definition 3](/en/math/topology/quotient_spaces#def3))

First, each $u_i$ is injective, because for $x,x'\in X_i$, $x\sim x'$ is equivalent to $\varphi_{ii}(x)=x'$, i.e., $x=x'$. Now let $U_i=u_i(X_i)$. Then for any $y\in X_j$, $u_j(y)\in U_i$ is equivalent to the existence of an element of $X_i$ equivalent to $y$, i.e., $y\in X_{ji}$, so

$$u_j^{-1}(U_i)=X_{ji}$$

and thus $U_i$ is an open subset of $X$. By the same calculation, for any open subset $V$ of $X_i$,

$$u_j^{-1}(u_i(V))=\varphi_{ij}(V\cap X_{ij})$$

and this is an open subset of $X_{ji}$, hence of $X_j$, so $u_i$ is an open map. From the above, $u_i$ is a homeomorphism from $X_i$ onto the open subset $U_i$, and from the above calculation we also have

$$u_i(X_{ij})=U_i\cap U_j,\qquad u_j\circ\varphi_{ij}=u_i\quad\text{on $X_{ij}$}$$

Now we define a structure sheaf on $X$. Since each $u_i$ is a homeomorphism, the pushforward $(u_i)_\ast\mathcal{O}_{X_i}$ is a sheaf on $U_i$. ([[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Example 9](/en/math/topology/sheaves#ex9)) On the other hand, since $\varphi_{ij}$ is an isomorphism of schemes, for any open subset $W\subseteq U_i\cap U_j$, $\varphi_{ij}^\sharp$ gives an isomorphism

$$\mathcal{O}_{X_j}(u_j^{-1}(W)) \rightarrow \mathcal{O}_{X_i}(u_i^{-1}(W))$$

and writing its inverse as $\theta_{ij}(W)$, we obtain a sheaf isomorphism on $U_i\cap U_j$

$$\theta_{ij}: ((u_i)_\ast\mathcal{O}_{X_i})\vert_{U_i\cap U_j} \rightarrow ((u_j)_\ast\mathcal{O}_{X_j})\vert_{U_i\cap U_j}$$

Then the cocycle condition is an equality of scheme morphisms including the sheaf components, so this means exactly that $\theta_{jk}\circ\theta_{ij}=\theta_{ik}$ on $U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k$, and in particular $\theta_{ii}=\id$ and $\theta_{ji}=\theta_{ij}^{-1}$. Now for any open subset $U\subseteq X$, define

$$\mathcal{O}_X(U)=\left\{(s_i)_{i\in I}\in \prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{O}_{X_i}(u_i^{-1}(U))\middle\vert \theta_{ij}\left(s_i\vert_{u_i^{-1}(U\cap U_j)}\right)=s_j\vert_{u_j^{-1}(U\cap U_i)}\quad\text{for all $i,j$}\right\}$$

and define the restriction maps componentwise. Then $\mathcal{O}_X$ becomes a sheaf, which can be checked by verifying the two conditions of [[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/sheaves#def1) componentwise. That is, the identity axiom is trivial because each $(u_i)_\ast \mathcal{O}_{X_i}$ is a sheaf, and for the gluability axiom, the fact that the resulting $(s_i)$ from gluing the given sections componentwise satisfies the above condition follows from this condition holding on each element of the given open covering and the fact that $\theta_{ij}$ commutes with restrictions, again by the identity axiom.

If $U\subseteq U_i$, then for any $k$ we have $u_k^{-1}(U)\subseteq u_k^{-1}(U_i)=X_{ki}$, so the above condition can be rewritten as $s_k=\theta_{ik}(s_i\vert_{u_i^{-1}(U\cap U_k)})$. That is, $(s_k)_{k\in I}$ is completely determined by $s_i$, and conversely for any $s_i\in \mathcal{O}_{X_i}(u_i^{-1}(U))$, the $(s_k)_{k\in I}$ defined by this formula satisfies the above condition by $\theta_{kl}\circ\theta_{ik}=\theta_{il}$. Therefore the projection $(s_k)_{k\in I}\mapsto s_i$ defines a sheaf isomorphism

$$\mathcal{O}_X\vert_{U_i}\cong (u_i)_\ast\mathcal{O}_{X_i}$$

and considering this together with the homeomorphism $u_i$, we have $(X_i,\mathcal{O}_{X_i})\cong (U_i, \mathcal{O}_X\vert_{U_i})$ as locally ringed spaces. In particular, for any $x\in X_i$, $\mathcal{O}_{X, u_i(x)}\cong \mathcal{O}_{X_i,x}$ is a local ring, so $(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ is a locally ringed space, and since $X_i$ is a scheme, choosing an affine open neighborhood $V\subseteq X_i$ of $x$ makes $u_i(V)$ an affine open neighborhood of $u_i(x)$. Since the $U_i$ cover $X$, we know from this that $(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ is a scheme in the sense of [Definition 1](#def1). Also, $U_i$ is an open subscheme of $X$ ([Lemma 3](#lem3)), and restricting the above isomorphism to $U_i\cap U_j=u_i(X_{ij})$ gives $U_i\cap U_j\cong X_{ij}$.

Finally we show uniqueness. This means the following. Given a scheme $X'$ and open subschemes $U_i'$ covering $X'$, and isomorphisms $\psi_i: X_i \rightarrow U_i'$ such that for each $i,j$, $\psi_i(X_{ij})=U_i'\cap U_j'$ and $\psi_j\circ\varphi_{ij}=\psi_i$ holds on $X_{ij}$, there exists a unique isomorphism $h:X \rightarrow X'$ such that $h\circ u_i=\psi_i$ for all $i$. We have already verified that the $X$ and $u_i$ we constructed satisfy this condition.

First, as a function between sets $h$ is defined by $h(u_i(x))=\psi_i(x)$, and this is well-defined because whenever $x\in X_{ij}$, we have $\psi_j(\varphi_{ij}(x))=\psi_i(x)$. Since each restriction $h\vert_{U_i}=\psi_i\circ u_i^{-1}$ is continuous and the $U_i$ form an open covering of $X$, by [[Topology] §Presheaves, ⁋Lemma 1](/en/math/topology/presheaves#lem1) the function $h$ is continuous. Also $X'=\bigcup U_i'$ so $h$ is surjective, and if $h(u_i(x))=h(u_j(y))$ then

$$\psi_i(x)=\psi_j(y)\in U_i'\cap U_j'=\psi_i(X_{ij})=\psi_j(X_{ji})$$

so $x\in X_{ij}$ and $y\in X_{ji}$, and now from $\psi_j(\varphi_{ij}(x))=\psi_i(x)=\psi_j(y)$ and the injectivity of $\psi_j$ we obtain $\varphi_{ij}(x)=y$, i.e., $u_i(x)=u_j(y)$. Therefore $h$ is bijective, and since each $h\vert_{U_i}$ is a homeomorphism onto the open subset $U_i'$, the map $h$ is a homeomorphism.

On the other hand, the sheaf components $\psi_i^\sharp$ give isomorphisms $\mathcal{O}_{X'}\vert_{U_i'} \rightarrow (h\vert_{U_i})_\ast (\mathcal{O}_X\vert_{U_i})$, and since the condition $\psi_j\circ\varphi_{ij}=\psi_i$ holds as a scheme morphism, these agree with each other on $U_i'\cap U_j'$. Therefore by the two conditions of [[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Definition 1](/en/math/topology/sheaves#def1), these glue to a unique morphism $h^\sharp: \mathcal{O}_{X'} \rightarrow h_\ast\mathcal{O}_X$, which is a sheaf isomorphism because it is an isomorphism on all stalks. ([[Topology] §Sheaves, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/topology/sheaves#prop4)) That is, $h=(h,h^\sharp)$ is an isomorphism between schemes. Finally, the condition $h\circ u_i=\psi_i$ determines $h$ on $U_i$ as a function between topological spaces and as a sheaf morphism, and since the $U_i$ cover $X$, by the identity axiom such an $h$ is unique.
:::

That is, we can glue schemes along open subsets to make a new scheme. Let us see the following example through this.

::: Example 10
Let two affine lines $X_0=\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^1=\Spec \mathbb{K}[\x_0]$, $X_1=\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{K}^1=\Spec \mathbb{K}[\x_1]$, and their open subsets

$$U_0=X_0\setminus \{(\x_0)\}=D(\x_0),\quad U_1=X_1\setminus \{(\x_1)\}=D(\x_1)$$

be given. Then by definition

$$\mathcal{O}_{X_0}(U_0)\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_0]_{\x_0}=\mathbb{K}[\x_0,1/\x_0]$$

and similarly $\mathcal{O}_{X_1}(U_1)\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_1,1/\x_1]$ holds. In this example we shall examine two different ways of gluing $X_0$ and $X_1$ concretely.

First, consider the case where the isomorphism $\varphi:(U_0, \mathcal{O}_{X_0}\vert_{U_0})\rightarrow (U_1, \mathcal{O}_{X_1}\vert_{U_1})$ comes from an isomorphism $k[\x_0,1/\x_0]\rightarrow k[\x_1,1/\x_1]$ identifying $\x_0$ and $\x_1$. Then as a topological space $X=X_0\cup_\varphi X_1$ is the line with double origin, and the structure sheaf is defined for any open subset $U\subseteq X$ by the following formula

$$\mathcal{O}_X(U)=\mathcal{O}_{X_0}(U\cap X_0)\times_{\mathcal{O}_{X_0}(U\cap U_0)\cong \mathcal{O}_{X_1}(U\cap U_1)} \mathcal{O}_{X_1}(U\cap X_1)$$

In particular the global sections are

$$\Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X)=\mathcal{O}_{X_0}(X_0)\times_{\mathcal{O}_{X_0}(U_0)\cong \mathcal{O}_{X_1}(U_1)} \mathcal{O}_{X_1}(X_1)=\mathbb{K}[\x_0]\times_{\mathbb{K}[\x_0,1/\x_0]\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_1,1/\x_1]}\mathbb{K}[\x_1]\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_0]$$

![line_with_two_origins](/assets/images/Math/Scheme_Theory/Schemes-2.svg){:style="width:242.7px" class="invert" .align-center}

On the other hand, this time consider the case where the isomorphism $\varphi$ comes from an isomorphism $\mathbb{K}[\x_0,1/\x_0]\rightarrow \mathbb{K}[\x_1,1/\x_1]$ identifying $\x_0$ and $1/\x_1$. Then in particular the closed point $(\x_0-\alpha)$ of $U_0$ will correspond to the closed point $(\x_1-1/\alpha)$ of $X_1$, and the closed point $(\x_1-\beta)$ of $U_1$ corresponds to the closed point $(\x_0-1/\beta)$ of $U_0$. That is, the space obtained from this is the projective space $\mathbb{P}^1$.

![projective_line](/assets/images/Math/Scheme_Theory/Schemes-3.png){:style="width:127.8px" class="invert" .align-center}

Thinking of the global sections in this case,

$$\Gamma(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1})=\mathbb{K}[\x_0]\times_{\mathbb{K}[\x_0,1/\x_0]\cong \mathbb{K}[\x_1,1/\x_1]} \mathbb{K}[\x_1]\cong \mathbb{K}$$

only. The last isomorphism means, in everyday language, that among the elements of $\mathbb{K}[\x_0]$, only constant functions remain elements of $\mathbb{K}[\x_1]$ when $\x_0$ is replaced by $1/\x_1$.
:::

The last computation in [Example 10](#ex10) above shows that $\mathbb{P}^1$ is not an affine scheme. If $\mathbb{P}^1$ were an affine scheme, it would necessarily have to be $\Spec \mathbb{K}$, because $\Spec \mathbb{K}$ is a scheme consisting of a single point.
