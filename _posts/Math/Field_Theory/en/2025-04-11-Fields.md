In the posts of this category we discuss fields. In particular, the purpose of this category is to introduce Galois theory. 

## Prime Fields

Let us first recall the following definition. 

::: Definition 1
A commutative division ring is called a *field*. ([\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Definition 3](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#def3){: data-lid="lugls" })
:::

Usually, a field is written as $\mathbb{K}$. On the other hand, since a field is a ring satisfying certain conditions, by collecting only these we can form a subcategory of $\Ring$, namely $\Field$. That is, $\Field$ is a full subcategory of $\Ring$. 

Earlier we defined every object of $\Ring$ to have $1$, and every ring homomorphism to preserve $1$. ([\[Algebraic Structures\] §Definition of a Ring, ⁋Definition 3](/en/math/algebraic_structures/rings#def3){: data-lid="bjgbo" }) Thus any subring of a ring $A$ also has a multiplicative identity, and this is precisely equal to $A$'s multiplicative identity $1$. From this we also know that $\mathbb{Z}$ is an initial object of $\Ring$. 

The following proposition, though obvious, is quite interesting: it shows that a ring homomorphism between fields can only be an inclusion or the zero map. 

::: Proposition 2
Let arbitrary fields $\mathbb{K},\mathbb{K}'$ be given, and consider a ring homomorphism $f:\mathbb{K} \rightarrow \mathbb{K}'$. Then $f$ is either the zero map or an inclusion. 
:::
::: Proof
$\ker f$ is an ideal of $\mathbb{K}$. Now see [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Proposition 4](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#prop4){: data-lid="eqvq3" }.
:::

Meanwhile, in that post [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions){: data-lid="r2lsl" }, we were able to obtain $\mathbb{Q}$ as the total field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}$, $\Frac(\mathbb{Z})$. On the other hand, the following example shows an example of a field different from $\mathbb{Q}$.

::: Example 3
Fix a prime $p$; then the quotient ring of $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{F}_p := \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, is a field. 

To verify this, let an arbitrary nonzero $a+p\mathbb{Z}\in \mathbb{F}_p$ be given. Then since $\gcd(a,p)=1$, from [\[Ring Theory\] §Integral Domains, ⁋Corollary 9](/en/math/ring_theory/integral_domains#cor9){: data-lid="kk03f" }, 

$$1=ax+bp$$

holds for some $x, b\in \mathbb{Z}$, and it suffices to consider the remainder of both sides upon division by $p$. 
:::

Collectively we call these *prime fields*. Then the following holds.

::: Theorem 4
Suppose a ring $A$ has some subfield. Then $A$ has a unique prime subfield $\mathbb{P}$. Here, $\mathbb{P}$ belongs to the center of $A$ and is contained in every subfield of $A$.
:::

::: Proof
Let $\mathbb{K}$ be a subfield of $A$. If we let $Z$ be the center of $A$, then $\mathbb{K}':= \mathbb{K} \cap Z$ is also a subfield of $A$.

Consider the unique ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $A$, $f: \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow A$, and let its kernel be $\mathfrak{p}$. Then every subring of $A$ contains $\im(f)$, so in particular $\mathbb{K}'$ also contains $\im(f)$. Now since $\mathbb{K}'$ is a field, it is an integral domain, and therefore $(0)$ is a prime ideal of $\mathbb{K}'$; hence by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Proposition 10](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#prop10){: data-lid="g00jz" }, either $\mathfrak{p} = (0)$ or, for some prime $p$, $\mathfrak{p} = (p)$.

If $\mathfrak{p}=0$, then $f$ is an embedding of $\mathbb{Z}$ into $\mathbb{K}'$, and thus by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Theorem 1](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#thm1){: data-lid="gfhri" }, $\mathbb{Q}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{K}'$ is induced.

If $\mathfrak{p} = (p)$, then by the first isomorphism theorem the homomorphic image of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{F}_p$ defines a subfield of $\mathbb{K}'$.

Thus in both cases we see that a prime field $\mathbb{P}$ exists inside $\mathbb{K}'$, and this $\mathbb{P}$ is a subfield of $A$ and lies in the center $Z$.

Now consider another subfield of $A$, $L$. Then $\mathbb{P} \cap L$ is a subfield of $A$ and a subset of $\mathbb{P}$, so $\mathbb{P} \cap L = \mathbb{P}$, and therefore $\mathbb{P} \subseteq L$. That is, since every subfield contains $\mathbb{P}$, $\mathbb{P}$ is contained in every subfield of $A$ and is unique as a prime field.
:::

Since we are mostly interested only in the case where the ring $A$ is a field, the hypothesis of the above theorem has little significance. For instance, we obtain the following corollaries.

::: Corollary 5
A field $\mathbb{K}$ has a unique prime subfield $\mathbb{P}$, and $\mathbb{P}$ is the smallest among the subfields of $\mathbb{K}$.
:::

::: Corollary 6
A field $\mathbb{K}$ is a prime field if and only if $\mathbb{K}$ has no subfield other than itself.
:::

Now from this we know that whenever an arbitrary field $\mathbb{K}$ is given, there exist a prime field $\mathbb{P}$ and an injection $\mathbb{P}\hookrightarrow \mathbb{K}$ defined in a unique way. In general, we call a ring homomorphism $f:A \rightarrow B$ an *extension* if it is injective, so this can be rewritten as the following slogan:

> Every field is, in a unique way, an extension of some prime field.

## Characteristic

Using prime fields, we can define the characteristic of a ring. Suppose a ring $A$ has some subfield. Then by [Theorem 4](#thm4){: data-lid="4dkil" }, this $A$ has a unique prime subfield $\mathbb{P}$. Meanwhile, examining the proof of [Theorem 4](#thm4){: data-lid="4mmbu" }, the method of obtaining this $\mathbb{P}$ is also given quite explicitly: since $\mathbb{P}$ must contain the multiplicative identity of $A$, $1$, from this

$$n.1=\underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_\text{\scriptsize$n$ times}$$

must also belong to $\mathbb{P}$, and if this ever becomes $0$, then $\mathbb{P}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{F}_p$, and otherwise $\mathbb{P}$ becomes $\mathbb{Q}$. Taking this as intuition, we define the following.

::: Definition 7
Suppose a ring $A$ has a subfield. Here, for the kernel of $f:\mathbb{Z} \rightarrow A$ in the proof of [Theorem 4](#thm4){: data-lid="wqh7c" }, we define its non-negative integer generator $n$ as the *characteristic* of the ring $A$ and denote it by $\ch(A)$.
:::

That is, if $A$ has a subfield, and thus has a prime subfield $\mathbb{P}$, then $\ch A=0$ is equivalent to $\mathbb{P}\cong \mathbb{Q}$, and $\ch A=p$ is equivalent to $\mathbb{P}\cong \mathbb{F}_p$; no other cases exist.

::: Proposition 8
Let $A$ be a ring that is not $0$. The following hold:

1. The characteristic of $A$ is $0$ if and only if for any $n\neq 0$, the map $x\mapsto n.x$ is a bijection from $A$ to $A$.
2. For a prime $p$, $\ch(A)=p$ if and only if for all $x\in A$, $p.x=0$.
:::
::: Proof
This is obtained because for any integer $n$ and element $x\in A$, considering, from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $A$, the unique ring homomorphism $f$, the equation

$$n.x=\underbrace{x+x+\cdots+x}_\text{\scriptsize$n$ times}=f(n)x$$

holds.
:::

Naturally, the objects we are familiar with are those where $\ch(A)=0$. If $\ch(A)=p$, several interesting results hold. To this end, let us first prove the following lemma.

::: Lemma 9
Fix a prime $p$. Then for $1 \leq i \leq p-1$ with $i$ an integer, $\binom{p}{i}$ is a multiple of $p$.
:::

::: Proof
We prove this by mathematical induction on $i$. In the case $i = 1$, since $\binom{p}{1} = p$, there is nothing more to prove. Now assume for $2 \leq i \leq p-1$ that $\binom{p}{i-1}$ is divisible by $p$. Then in the identity

$$i \cdot \binom{p}{i} = (p - i + 1) \cdot \binom{p}{i-1}$$

, the right-hand side is a multiple of $p$ by the induction hypothesis. Meanwhile, $i$ is not a multiple of $p$, so for this identity to hold, $\binom{p}{i}$ must be a multiple of $p$.
:::

Now the following holds.

::: Theorem 10
Let $A$ be a commutative ring of characteristic $p>0$. Then the function

$$\Frob_p: A \rightarrow A;\qquad a \mapsto a^p$$

is an endomorphism of $A$. That is, the two equations

$$(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p,\qquad (ab)^p = a^p b^p$$

hold for all $a,b\in A$.
:::

::: Proof
The equation for multiplication requires no further proof since $A$ is commutative. In the case of the equation for addition, considering the binomial expansion

$$(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p + \sum_{i = 1}^{p-1} \binom{p}{i}. a^i b^{p-i}$$

, by [Lemma 9](#lem9){: data-lid="39ccx" }, for all $1 \leq i \leq p-1$, every $i$ satisfies $\binom{p}{i} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, and therefore by [Proposition 8](#prop8){: data-lid="9jna7" }

$$\binom{p}{i}. a^i b^{p-i} = 0$$

holds for all $1\leq i\leq p-1$. From this we obtain the desired equality.
:::

::: Definition 11
When a commutative ring $A$ has characteristic $p$, the function in [Theorem 10](#thm10){: data-lid="e05mq" }

$$A \rightarrow A,\qquad a \mapsto a^p$$

is called the *Frobenius endomorphism*.
:::

Then the $f$-fold composition $\Frob_p^f$ of the Frobenius endomorphism is given by the formula

$$a\mapsto a^{p^f}$$

and since this is still an endomorphism of $A$, in particular

$$(a_1+\cdots+a_n)^{p^f}=a_1^{p^f}+\cdots+a_n^{p^f}$$

always holds and each $a_k^{p^f}$ is an element of $A$. More generally, for a subset of $A$, $S$, we write the image under the $f$-fold composition of the Frobenius endomorphism as $S^{p^f}$.

In addition, we introduce a few notations. For a subring of $A$, $K$, and an arbitrary subset $S\subseteq A$, $K[S]$ means the subring of $A$ generated by $K\cup S$. If $A$ is a field, then by [\[Algebraic Structures\] §Field of Fractions, ⁋Theorem 1](/en/math/algebraic_structures/field_of_fractions#thm1){: data-lid="doxai" }, from $K[S]\hookrightarrow A$ a ring homomorphism

$$\Frac(K[S]) \rightarrow A$$

is induced, and through this we can regard $\Frac(K[S])$ as a subfield of $A$. We agree to write this as $K(S)$. Then $K(S)$ is by definition the smallest subfield containing $K\cup S$ in $A$.

Then under this notation, the following holds.

::: Proposition 12
Fix a commutative ring $A$ of characteristic $p>0$, a subring $K$ of $A$, a subset $S$ of $A$, and a positive integer $f$.

1. $K[S]^{p^f} = K^{p^f}[S^{p^f}]$ holds. If $A$ is a field, then $K(S)^{p^f} = K^{p^f}(S^{p^f})$ also holds.
2. If the set $\{a_i\}_{i \in I}$ is a $K$-module generating $K[S]$, then the set $\{a_i^{p^f}\}_{i \in I}$ is a $K^{p^f}$-module generating $K[S]^{p^f}$.
:::

::: Proof
1. The left-hand side of the equality is $\Frob_p^f(K[S])$, so the claim is that the subring of $A$ generated by $K^{p^f}\cup S^{p^f}=\Frob_p^f(K)\cup\Frob_p^f(S)$ equals $\Frob_p^f(K[S])$. This holds because the image of the subring generated by $K\cup S$ under $\Frob_p^f$ is equal to the subring generated by $\Frob_p^f(K\cup S)$.
2. Since $K[S]$ is generated by $K$-linear combinations of the $a_i$, from the binomial expansion and [Theorem 10](#thm10){: data-lid="zyyb7" } we know that $K[S]^{p^f}$ is generated by $K^{p^f}$-linear combinations of the respective $a_i^{p^f}$.
:::

## Perfect Rings

Now we define the following.

::: Definition 13
Consider a ring $A$ of characteristic $p \neq 0$ and the Frobenius map $\Frob_p : A \rightarrow A$.

1. If $\Frob_p$ is surjective, we call $A$ a *semi-perfect ring*.
2. If $\Frob_p$ is bijective, we call $A$ a *perfect ring*.
:::

If $A$ is a perfect ring, then $\Frob_p^{-1}$ is also an (auto)morphism from $A$ to $A$, so it is appropriate to write it as $a\mapsto a^{p^{-f}}$ or $a^{1/p^f}$. As before, for a subset $S$ of $A$, we write the image of $S$ under $(\Frob_p^{-1})^f$ as $S^{1/p^f}$.


::: Definition 14
For a commutative ring $A$ of characteristic $p \ne 0$, a *perfect closure* is a pair $(A^{1/p^\infty}, \phi)$ satisfying the following two conditions.

- $A^{1/p^\infty}$ is a perfect ring of characteristic $p$.
- $\phi : A \rightarrow A^{1/p^\infty}$ is a ring homomorphism and satisfies the following universal property.
    >
    > Whenever a perfect ring $B$ of characteristic $p$ and a ring homomorphism $f:A \rightarrow B$ are given, there exists a unique ring homomorphism $\hat{f}: A^{1/p^\infty} \rightarrow B$ such that $f=\hat{f}\circ\phi$.
:::

If a perfect closure of $A$ exists, then it is uniquely determined by definition. The following theorem shows that a ring of characteristic $p\neq 0$ always has a perfect closure.

::: Theorem 15
For a characteristic $p \ne 0$ commutative ring $A$, the perfect closure of $A$, $(A^{1/p^\infty}, \phi)$, exists. Moreover, the following hold.

1. The kernel of $\phi$ is the set of all nilpotent elements of $A$.
2. For any $x \in A^{1/p^\infty}$, there exists some $n > 0$ such that $x^{p^n} \in \phi(A)$.
:::

Instead of a proof, we briefly outline the flow. Intuitively, the condition that $\Frob_p$ is bijective means that any element of $A$ has a (unique) $p$-th root. Therefore, the perfect closure of $A$ is obtained by adjoining, for any element of $A$, its $p$-th roots.

To this end, we consider the following directed system:

$$A_0=A\overset{\Frob_p}{\longrightarrow} A_1=A\overset{\Frob_p}{\longrightarrow} A_2=A\rightarrow\cdots$$

If we consider the direct limit of this directed system,

$$\varinjlim A=\left(\coprod A\right)\bigg/{\sim},\qquad \text{$a_i\sim a_j\iff \Frob_p^{k-i}(a_i)=\Frob_p^{k-j}(a_j)$ for some $k\geq i,j$}$$

any element of $\varinjlim A$ is represented by a suitable $i$ and $a_i\in A_i$. We denote this by

$$[a_i\in A_i]$$

and define the canonical morphisms by

$$\phi_i: A_i \rightarrow \varinjlim A;\qquad a_i\mapsto [a_i\in A_i]$$

Our claim is that $A^{1/p^\infty}=\varinjlim A$ and $\phi=\phi_0$ satisfy the desired conditions.

This conforms to our intuition if we write out the multiplication in $\varinjlim A$ explicitly. By definition, in $\varinjlim A$, the multiplication of two elements $[a_i\in A_i]$ and $[a_j\in A_j]$ is, assuming without loss of generality that $j\geq i$, given by the formula

$$[\Frob_p^{j-i}(a_i)a_j\in A_j]=[a_i^{p^{j-i}}a_j\in A_j]$$

Our intuition is that $[a_j\in A_j]$ corresponds in $\varinjlim A$ to $a_j^{1/p^j}$, which is to say that

$$[a_j\in A_0]=[a_j\in A_j]^{p^j}$$

Then the right-hand side is $[a_j^{p^j}\in A_j]=[\Frob_p^j(a_j)]$, so the above identity holds.

What should be noted, as can be seen from the first result of the theorem, is that $\phi: a\mapsto [a\in A_0]$ being injective is equivalent to $A$ being reduced.

For unified notation, it is sometimes convenient to set the case $\ch A=0$ to $1$. For this purpose, we set the *characteristic exponent* of a field $\mathbb{K}$ to be, in the case $\ch \mathbb{K}=0$, $1$, and in the case $\ch \mathbb{K}=p>0$, $p$.

::: Proposition 16
Consider a characteristic exponent $q$ field $\mathbb{K}$. Then, for each $f\geq 0$, $x\mapsto x^{q^f}$ induces an isomorphism from $\mathbb{K}$ onto some subfield of $\mathbb{K}$, $\mathbb{K}^{q^f}$.
:::
::: Proof
If $p=0$, then $q=1$, and hence the given function is the identity function, so there is nothing to prove. If $q \ne 1$, since $q$ is a positive prime, the characteristic of $\mathbb{K}$ is a positive prime $p$, and $q = p$. Then the given function is the $f$-fold composition of the Frobenius endomorphism and is a surjective ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{K}$ onto $\mathbb{K}^{q^f}$. That this is injective is clear because $\mathbb{K}$ is a field and therefore has no nilpotent elements.
:::

::: Definition 17
For a characteristic exponent $q$ field $\mathbb{K}$, if the function of [Proposition 16](#prop16){: data-lid="1gpqv" } is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{K}$ onto $\mathbb{K}$, we call $\mathbb{K}$ a *perfect field*; otherwise, we call it an *imperfect field*.
:::

That is, for a field $\mathbb{K}$ to be perfect, either (1) the characteristic exponent must be $1$, or (2) the characteristic exponent must be $q>1$ and it must be a perfect ring in the sense of [Definition 13](#def13){: data-lid="uq1ta" }.

Then the following holds.

::: Proposition 18
For a field $\mathbb{K}$, the following hold.

1. If $\ch(\mathbb{K})=0$, then $\mathbb{K}$ is perfect.
2. If $\mathbb{K}$ is a finite set, then $\mathbb{K}$ is perfect.
:::

::: Proof
It suffices to show only the second claim, and we have already observed in the proof of that proposition that the function $\mathbb{K} \rightarrow \mathbb{K}^{q^f}\subseteq \mathbb{K}$ of [Proposition 16](#prop16){: data-lid="n68tr" } is always injective without any conditions on $\mathbb{K}$. Now if $\mathbb{K}$ is a finite set, then by its size we must have $\mathbb{K}^{q^f}=\mathbb{K}$.
:::

Meanwhile, for an arbitrary commutative ring $A$ and its polynomial algebra $E=A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$, we have examined the module of Kähler differentials $\Omega_{E/A}$. ([\[Multilinear Algebra\] §Differential Modules, ⁋Example 10](/en/math/multilinear_algebra/differential_modules#ex10){: data-lid="9df1b" }) According to this structure, for an arbitrary polynomial $f\in E$, the element $\dd{f}\in\Omega_{E/A}$ is given by the following formula:

$$\dd{f}=\sum_{i\in I} \frac{\partial f}{\partial\x_i}\dd{\x_i}$$

Therefore, if $A$ has characteristic $p$, then the image of the polynomial $\x_i^p$ in $\Omega_{E/A}$ will be $0$. The following proposition refines this observation more completely.

::: Proposition 19
For a commutative ring $A$ and polynomial algebra $E=A[\x_i]_{i\in I}$, let the subset of $E$, $S$, be the set of those satisfying $\dd{f}=0$ among polynomials $f$. Then the following hold.

1. If $\ch(A)=0$, then $S=A$.
2. If $\ch(A)=p$, then $S=A[\x_i^p]_{i\in I}$, and moreover, if $A$ is perfect, then $S=E^p$ holds.
:::
